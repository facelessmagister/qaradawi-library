# Module 1.2 — Iman and Happiness

**Phase:** 1 — Roots: Faith as the Foundation
**Week:** 2
**Duration:** 45–60 minutes

---

## Learning Objective

Family members understand that true happiness comes from faith (iman), not from material possessions, wealth, children, or scientific achievement. The session establishes the Qur'anic principle that contentment of the heart is a divine gift reserved for the believer whose intention is directed toward the Hereafter.

---

## Opening (3 min)

Begin with the standard opening:

> بِسْمِ اللَّهِ وَالصَّلَاةُ وَالسَّلَامُ عَلَى رَسُولِ اللَّهِ

State the niyyah: we gather as a family to learn where true happiness comes from, so that we may seek it where it can actually be found — in faith, not in the things the world tells us will make us happy.

## Hadith (3 min)

### Hadith 1 — The Intention Directed Toward the Hereafter

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "Whoever's intention is directed to the Hereafter, Allah makes his richness in his heart (i.e., grants him contentment), brings his self together (i.e., grants him tranquility and peace of mind), and the good things of worldly life that have been predestined for him come to him unwillingly. And whoever's intention is directed to worldly life, Allah makes his poverty before his eyes (i.e., inflicts him with dependence on others), disunites his self (i.e., inflicts him with confusion and bewilderment), and none of the good things of worldly life comes to him except those that Allah has predestined for him."
>
> — **Narrated by at-Tirmidhi** on the authority of Anas ibn Malik. Ibn Majah and others narrated a similar hadith on the authority of Zayd ibn Thabit.

### Hadith 2 — Part of a Son of Adam's Happiness

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "Part of the happiness of a son of Adam is: a good wife, a good house, and a good means of transport."
>
> — **Narrated by al-Bukhari, at-Tirmidhi, and Ibn Majah**

### Hadith 3 — Whoever Comes Upon the Morning Safe

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "Whoever comes upon the morning safe, with his body healthy, and having the food that he needs on this day, seems to have possessed the whole world."
>
> — **Narrated by at-Tirmidhi** on the authority of Ubaydullah ibn Muhsin al-Ansari

### Hadith 4 — Were a Son of Adam to Have Two Valleys of Gold

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "Were a son of Adam to have two valleys of gold, he would seek a third."
>
> — **Narrated by al-Bukhari and Muslim** on the authority of Anas ibn Malik

## Teaching: Qaradawi's Treatment (15 min)

*Source: Yusuf al-Qaradawi, **Faith and Life** (الإيمان والحياة), Chapter 2: "Iman and Happiness." All citations refer to this chapter unless otherwise noted.*

### 1. The Universal Search

Qaradawi opens with a universal observation: "Happiness is the paradise of dreams that mankind seek, including philosophers with their meditation and abstraction and the common people with their naivety and simplicity; and kings in their towering palaces and the wretched people in their small huts."

The question that has always confused people is: **Where is happiness?** Many sought it where it could never be found, "so they returned just like a seeker of pearls in a desert, with nothing, and feeling exhausted, depressed, and disappointed."

### 2. Is Happiness Found in Material Pleasure?

Qaradawi systematically evaluates the most common candidates for happiness, beginning with wealth and luxury. He presents the case of **Sweden** — a country with a very high economic level where people "scarcely fear poverty, senility, unemployment, or any calamity or hardship." The government provides:

- Free education at all stages
- Free medical treatment in hospitals
- Pensions, illness support, housing support, blindness support
- Motherhood support covering delivery and medical care
- Insurance against work injuries (obligatory)
- The best unemployment support on an international level
- Child support until age sixteen
- Student loans and living support for the disabled
- Loans for newly-weds to furnish their homes

Yet despite all these guarantees, Qaradawi reports: "the Swedish people suffer from worry, solitude, distress, tension, complaints, dissatisfaction, and despair. As a result, they flee from this life of hardship through suicide to which thousands of people resort."

The journalist who investigated this concluded: **"The secret behind this hardship relates to one thing: deprivation of faith — any kind of faith."**

Qaradawi then turns to **America**, the richest country in the world, which "has not realized happiness for its people despite its wealth, its skyscrapers, spacecraft, and abundant income." He quotes a thinker: "Life in New York is a beautiful cover of a case of distress and hardship!" The French writer Françoise Sagan wrote after visiting New York: "New York is burdensome for man. Its heart beats faster than the hearts of its residents."

The Qur'anic principle: wealth without faith can become punishment. "Let not their wealth nor their children dazzle you. In reality, Allah's plan is to punish them with these things in this life" (9:55). Qaradawi explains: "Punishment here refers to hardship, suffering, pain, grief, and illness."

### 3. Is Happiness Found in Having Children?

Qaradawi acknowledges that "children are allurements of the life of this world," but notes that many children cause their parents suffering, treating them with "disobedience and ingratitude, instead of the kindness, obedience, and gratitude they deserve." He cites examples of parents killed at the hands of their own sons "because the latter craved after their wealth." Shakespeare's King Lear captures this: "Filial ingratitude is very painful; sharper than a serpent's tooth."

Children, then, are a potential source of joy — but not a guaranteed source of happiness. Without faith guiding the parent-child relationship, children can become a source of grief.

### 4. Is Happiness Found in Science?

Qaradawi acknowledges that science "has made great achievements for man" and that "thirst for knowledge is a characteristic of the human soul." However, he argues that "knowledge in itself is not a means of happiness. Rather, it often leads to worry and confusion."

He quotes Nietzsche and other scholars who "spent their youth pursuing knowledge" and concluded that science is "undoubtedly a violator of the secrets of the unseen." When they realized "the screens of the unseen were countless and endless, they felt weak and imagined that they were just chasing after a mirage."

He cites Bertrand Russell — "in spite of his materialistic view" — who confirmed that "man has achieved victory through science in his struggle against nature, but in his struggle against his own self, he has failed to gain any victory and science has not availed him in any way. He also confessed that religion is still the dominator of this field."

Dr. Henry Link, the American psychiatrist, stated: "Absolute dependence on reasoning is apt to destroy man's happiness... psychologists who have discovered this reached these discoveries by means of education and religion, character, and the philosophy of life." Link concluded: "We will never be guided to decisive solutions to the thorny problems of life or achieve happiness only through the development of information and scientific knowledge. This is because the promotion of science means an increase in confusion and chaos. Unless all these sciences are gathered together under the standard of the obvious daily facts of life and subjected accordingly, they will never lead to the liberation of the minds... they will definitely lead to the collapse of these minds. Still, this unification should be fulfilled through a way other than that of science; that is, **the way of faith**."

### 5. Where Happiness Is Actually Found

After eliminating wealth, children, and science as sufficient sources of happiness, Qaradawi arrives at his central thesis:

"Happiness is an abstract thing that cannot be seen by the eye, measured by quantity, stored in cases, or bought by money. It is something that man feels in the form of **purity of the soul, tranquility of the heart, and peace of mind**. It is something that **springs from within man** and is **not imported from outside**."

He tells a story of a wife who, when threatened by her husband, replied calmly:

> "You cannot make me unhappy, just as you cannot make me happy... If happiness was in my expenses, you would stop giving them to me, and if it was in jewels and precious dresses, you would deprive me of them, but it is in something that no one possesses... I find my happiness in my faith, and my faith is in my heart, and my heart is controlled by my Lord, alone!"

Qaradawi comments: "This is true happiness, which no human being can give to anyone or take by force from whomever Allah has granted it."

He quotes two believers:
- "We enjoy such happiness that if kings knew about it, they would fight us with the sword to get it!"
- "Sometimes I have such great feelings that I say, 'If the residents of Paradise are enjoying what I am enjoying, then they must be enjoying a good life.'"

### 6. The Prophetic Hadith: The Key to Happiness

Qaradawi presents the hadith of Anas ibn Malik as the key proof-text:

"Whoever's intention is directed to the Hereafter, Allah makes his richness in his heart, brings his self together, and the good things of worldly life come to him unwillingly."

He explains: the person whose heart is oriented toward Allah receives **inner richness** (contentment), **tranquility** (the self brought together, not disunited), and **worldly provision** (which comes to him unwillingly — he does not chase it, it chases him).

Conversely, the one whose aim is solely this world suffers **perpetual poverty** (poverty before his eyes — he always feels lacking), **disunity of self** (confusion and bewilderment), and **worldly deprivation** (nothing comes to him except what Allah has predestined).

Qaradawi draws on **Ibn al-Qayyim**, who defined the spiritual suffering of the worldly person as "disuniting one's self and heart and having poverty before one's eyes." The worldly person suffers at least one of three tribulations: "chronic grief, continuous suffering, and endless regret and sorrow. This is because whenever he gets some of its pleasure he seeks something that gives him more pleasure."

The Prophet said: "Were a son of Adam to have two valleys of gold, he would seek a third." Qaradawi adds the allegory of Dr. Faustus — the man who sold his soul for twenty-four years of earthly pleasure, only to realize too late that "nothing can save him from Hell-fire." He also cites Prophet `Isa (Jesus, peace be upon him): "The likeness of a lover of this worldly life is as the likeness of a drinker of intoxicants: the more he drinks from them, the more he feels thirsty."

### 7. The Role of Material Things

Qaradawi is careful not to dismiss material life entirely. He cites the Prophetic hadith: "Part of the happiness of a son of Adam is: a good wife, a good house, and a good means of transport." And: "Whoever comes upon the morning safe, with his body healthy, and having the food that he needs on this day, seems to have possessed the whole world."

He explains: "No one can deny that the material side of life has a role in the achievement of happiness. But how? It is measured by quality, not quantity." Material things contribute to happiness by **removing sources of distress** — a bad wife, a bad house, a bad means of transport. But they are not the source of happiness itself.

Qaradawi's concluding metaphor is one of the most beautiful in the book:

> "If happiness were to be a tree whose stem is the human soul and the human heart, then belief in Allah and the Hereafter would be its water, nutrition, air, and light."

Iman provides the conditions in which happiness grows. Without iman, the tree dies — no matter how much wealth, children, or knowledge you pile around it.

## Family Discussion (15 min)

### Question 1 — What Makes You Truly Happy?

> What makes you truly happy? Is it the same as what makes you excited? Is there a difference between pleasure and happiness?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is the opening question and the most important one. Let everyone answer honestly. Children may say "toys" or "playing" — that is fine. The distinction Qaradawi makes is between **temporary pleasure** (which fades) and **deep happiness** (which stays). Pleasure is the sugar rush; happiness is the nutrition.

**Qaradawi's framework:** "Happiness is something that man feels in the form of purity of the soul, tranquility of the heart, and peace of mind. It is something that springs from within man and is not imported from outside."

### Question 2 — The Emptiness After Getting What You Want

> Have you ever gotten something you really wanted — a new phone, a new game, a trip — and then felt empty afterward? Why does that happen?

**Facilitator guidance:** This question makes the Sweden case study personal. Sweden has everything — healthcare, education, pensions, child support — yet suffers from "worry, solitude, distress, tension." The same principle applies to getting a new toy: the excitement fades, and the emptiness returns. Only faith fills the void permanently.

**Qaradawi's framework:** "Whenever he gets some of its pleasure he seeks something that gives him more pleasure." The Prophet: "Were a son of Adam to have two valleys of gold, he would seek a third."

### Question 3 — What Does "Richness of the Heart" Mean?

> The Prophet said Allah makes "richness in the heart" of the one whose intention is toward the Hereafter. What does it mean to be rich in the heart? Can you be rich without money?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is the core teaching of the module. "Richness of the heart" = contentment (qana'ah). It means feeling that you have enough. It is the opposite of the feeling that "I need more" — which is "poverty before the eyes."

**For children:** "Have you ever felt really happy even though you didn't have any new toys? That's richness of the heart."

### Question 4 — The Story of the Wife and Her Husband

> In Qaradawi's story, the wife said: "I find my happiness in my faith, and my faith is in my heart, and my heart is controlled by my Lord, alone!" What does that mean? Can someone take your happiness away if it comes from faith?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is a powerful story for all ages. The husband threatened to make his wife miserable — but she replied that he could neither give her happiness nor take it away, because her happiness came from faith, and faith is in the heart, which is controlled by Allah alone.

**For teenagers:** "If someone at school tries to make you feel bad, can they actually take your happiness? Only if your happiness depends on what they think. If it depends on Allah, no one can touch it."

### Question 5 — How Can We Find Happiness in Faith Together?

> How can we as a family find happiness in remembering Allah? What is one thing we can do together this week that brings the kind of happiness that doesn't fade?

**Facilitator guidance:** Answers may include: praying together, reading Qur'an together, gratefulness practice, visiting family, helping someone in need. The point is to connect faith and happiness in a concrete family practice.

**Qaradawi's framework:** "In the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest" (13:28). Happiness springs from dhikr — the conscious, living awareness of Allah's presence.

## Family Activity (10 min)

### Activity: "Happiness Moments"

Each family member shares one moment from the past week when they felt genuinely happy. After each person shares, the family discusses together:

- Was this happiness connected to something material (a new thing, a purchase) — or to faith, family, gratitude, or kindness?
- If it was material: did the feeling last? Or did it fade?
- If it was faith/family/gratitude: did it stay with you?

**Goal:** The family learns to distinguish between temporary pleasure and lasting happiness — and to recognize that the moments of deepest joy are usually the simplest ones, connected to faith and relationships, not to possessions.

**Variation for young children (under 7):** Instead of talking, have children draw their "happiest moment" from the week. Ask: "Who was there? Was it a thing or a person? Was Allah part of your happiness?"

**Follow-up for the week:** Each evening, each family member names one thing they are grateful for from that day. This is the simplest form of dhikr — remembrance of Allah through gratitude — and it is where happiness lives.

## Notes for the Facilitator

### Before the Session
- Read the full source text: `/root/qaradawi-library/raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-02.txt`
- The chapter is approximately 25 pages — read it in full. It is one of the most eloquent chapters in the book.

### Key Distinction to Emphasize
Qaradawi is NOT saying material things are bad. He explicitly says: "No one can deny that the material side of life has a role in the achievement of happiness." The Prophetic hadith confirms: "a good wife, a good house, and a good means of transport" are "part of the happiness of a son of Adam."

The point is **priority and foundation**: material things contribute to happiness by removing sources of distress, but they cannot create happiness. Happiness "springs from within" — from iman. If the inner foundation is faith, then material blessings enhance life. If the inner foundation is empty, no amount of material wealth can fill the void.

### The Sweden Case Study
This is the most powerful part of the chapter for discussion. Sweden in the 1970s had:
- Universal healthcare
- Free education at all levels
- Pensions, illness support, housing support, blindness support
- Motherhood support
- Work injury insurance
- Best unemployment support internationally
- Child support until age 16
- Student loans and living support
- Newly-wed home furnishing loans

Despite ALL of this, the Swedish people suffered from "worry, solitude, distress, tension, complaints, dissatisfaction, and despair" and thousands resorted to suicide. The conclusion: "deprivation of faith — any kind of faith."

**For children:** Simplify — "Imagine a country where everyone has free food, free school, free doctors, and free houses. But the people there are still very sad. Why? Because they forgot Allah."

### The Tree Metaphor
End the teaching section with Qaradawi's metaphor: "If happiness were to be a tree whose stem is the human soul and the human heart, then belief in Allah and the Hereafter would be its water, nutrition, air, and light." This image captures the entire teaching in one sentence.

### Adapting for Age

| Age Group | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Under 7 | Focus on the tree metaphor — "Happiness is like a tree. Allah's love is the water. Without water, the tree dies." Skip the Sweden discussion. Use the drawing activity. |
| 7–12 | Tell the Sweden story as a "mystery" — "There was a country where everyone had everything... but they were still sad. Can you guess why?" Tell the wife story as a narrative. |
| 13–17 | Engage with the full Sweden case study. Ask: "We have phones, games, and social media. Are we happier than someone who has none of these but has faith?" Connect to consumer culture. |
| Adults | Read the full source chapter. Discuss the Faustus allegory and the Ibn al-Qayyim framework. Discuss Russell's confession that "religion is still the dominator of this field." |

### If Time Is Short
If 60 minutes is not available, reduce the teaching section to points 2 (Sweden), 5 (where happiness is found), and 7 (the Prophetic hadith and tree metaphor). Keep the full discussion — Questions 2 and 4 are where the learning happens.
