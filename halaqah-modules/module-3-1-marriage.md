# Module 3.1 — Marriage: The Foundation of Family

**Phase:** 3 — Branches: The Family Unit
**Week:** 13
**Duration:** 45–60 minutes

---

## Learning Objective

Family members understand that marriage in Islam is a solemn covenant blessed by Allah — not merely a social contract. The session establishes that Islam categorically rejects celibacy, that spouse selection prioritises religion and character, and that marriage is the foundation upon which the entire social structure is built.

---

> بِسْمِ اللَّهِ وَالصَّلَاةُ وَالسَّلَامُ عَلَى رَسُولِ اللَّهِ

State the niyyah: we gather as a family to understand that marriage is not merely a cultural arrangement but a divine institution — a solemn covenant blessed by Allah, the foundation of the family, and the Sunnah of the Prophets.

## Hadith (3 min)

### Hadith 1 — No Monasticism in Islam; I Marry Women

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "I am the one who knows Allah best and fears Him the most among you, yet I fast and I break my fast, I offer prayers during the night and sleep, and I marry women. He who turns away from my Sunnah has nothing to do with me."
>
> — **Narrated by al-Bukhari** on the authority of Anas ibn Malik

### Hadith 2 — O Young Men, Marry

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "O young men, those of you who can support a wife should marry, for it keeps you from looking at women and preserves your chastity. But those who cannot should fast, for it is a means of cooling passion."
>
> — **Narrated by al-Bukhari** on the authority of Abdullah ibn Mas'ud

### Hadith 3 — Three Who Have a Right to Allah's Help

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "There are three who have a right to the help of Allah: the one who seeks to marry out of the desire to live a chaste life, the slave whose master has agreed to his buying his freedom, and the one who fights in the cause of Allah."
>
> — **Narrated by Ahmad, an-Nasa'i, at-Tirmidhi, Ibn Majah, and al-Hakim**

### Hadith 4 — Marry the Religious One

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "A woman is married for four reasons: her wealth, her social status, her beauty, and her religion. Marry the religious one; otherwise you will regret it."
>
> — **Narrated by al-Bukhari (5090)** and **Muslim (1466)** on the authority of Abu Hurayrah

### Hadith 5 — The Best Temporary Joy

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "This world is all but temporary joy, and the best temporary joy in this world is a righteous woman."
>
> — **Narrated by Muslim (1467)** and Ahmed (6567)

### Hadith 6 — When Someone with Good Religion Proposes

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "When someone with whose religion and character you are satisfied asks for your daughter in marriage, accede to his request. If you do not do so, there will be corruption and great evil on the earth."
>
> — **Narrated by at-Tirmidhi (1084)** and Ibn Majah (1967)

## Teaching: Qaradawi's Treatment (15 min)

*Sources: Yusuf al-Qaradawi, **The Lawful and the Prohibited in Islam** (الحلال والحرام), Chapter 3: "The Lawful and Prohibited in Marriage and Family Life" (pp. 190-200); **Ethics in Islam** (أخلاق المسلم), Chapters 1 and 4 (section 4.3: Collective Ethics — Marriage); **Economic Security in Islam**, Chapters 1 and 4. All citations refer to these sources unless otherwise noted.*

### 1. The Three Approaches to the Sexual Drive

Qaradawi opens his treatment of marriage in *The Lawful and the Prohibited* by identifying three historical approaches to the sexual drive:

1. **Free sex** — "satisfy one's sexual need freely with whomever is available and whenever one pleases, without any restraints of religion, morality, or custom." This reduces the human being to an animal and destroys the family.

2. **Suppression** — "suppress and try to annihilate the sexual drive." This is advocated by ascetic schools and monastic philosophies. It is "contrary to Allah's plan and purpose" and "in conflict with the course of the natural order."

3. **Regulation** — "regulate the satisfaction of this urge, allowing it to operate within certain limits, neither suppressing nor giving it free rein." **This is the stand of the revealed religions**, and in particular Islam.

Qaradawi writes: "This is the just and intermediate position. If marriage were not permitted, the sexual instinct would not play its role in the continuation of the human species; and if fornication and adultery were not prohibited, the foundation of the family would be eroded."

The Qur'an: "And do not come near zina; indeed, it is an abomination and an evil way" (17:32). Note: not just "do not commit zina" but "do not come NEAR zina" — Islam blocks every avenue leading to it.

### 2. No Monasticism in Islam

Islam categorically rejects celibacy. Qaradawi writes: "As long as he possesses the means to marry, the Muslim is not permitted to refrain from marriage on the grounds that he has dedicated himself to the service or the worship of Allah and to a life of monasticism."

He cites the famous incident: three Companions came to the Prophet's wives and asked about his worship. Considering it insufficient, one said: "I will fast all the year without breaking the fast." Another said: "I will always offer prayers during the night." The third said: "I will have nothing to do with women and will never marry."

The Prophet responded: "I am the one who knows Allah best and fears Him the most among you, yet I fast and I break my fast, I offer prayers during the night and sleep, and I marry women. He who turns away from my Sunnah has nothing to do with me."

And: "O young men, those of you who can support a wife should marry, for it keeps you from looking at women and preserves your chastity."

Qaradawi notes: "From this statement some scholars have inferred that marriage is obligatory for the Muslim who is able to support a wife."

### 3. Marriage as a Solemn Covenant

Islam does not view marriage as merely a civil contract. The Qur'an calls it a "solemn covenant" (*mīthāqan ghalīẓan*, Q 4:21). Qaradawi explains that this language elevates marriage beyond a social arrangement — it is a covenant blessed by Allah, carrying divine weight.

Marriage is:
- The **foundation** upon which the entire social structure is built
- A **Sunnah** of the Prophets — the Prophet declared: "I marry women too. He who turns away from my Sunnah has no relation with me"
- A source of **tranquility** — "that you may dwell in tranquility with them" (30:21)
- A vehicle for **affection and mercy** — "He has placed between you affection and mercy" (30:21)
- A means of **kinship expansion** — "He created from water mankind and made them related through lineage and marriage" (25:54)

### 4. Spouse Selection: Religion and Character First

Qaradawi strongly emphasises the Prophetic criteria for spouse selection. The Prophet said: "A woman is married for four reasons: her wealth, her social status, her beauty, and her religion. **Marry the religious one; otherwise you will regret it.**"

And: "When someone with whose religion and character you are satisfied asks for your daughter in marriage, accede to his request. If you do not do so, there will be corruption and great evil on the earth."

Qaradawi comments: the Prophetic priority reverses the cultural tendency to prioritise wealth, status, beauty, or family connections. Religion and character are the primary criteria because:
- Wealth can be lost
- Beauty fades
- Status changes
- But religion and character endure and shape the family for generations

In *Ethics in Islam* (section 4.3), Qaradawi adds: "Choose wisely for your progeny, for veins run deep" (Ibn Majah) — spouse selection affects not only the couple but the character of future children.

### 5. The Girl's Consent

Qaradawi affirms the Islamic principle that a woman has the right to choose her husband. The Prophet said: "A woman who has been previously married has more right concerning her person than her guardian, and a virgin must be asked about herself, her consent being her silence."

He cites the case of a girl who came to the Prophet saying her father had married her to her cousin against her wishes. The Prophet allowed her to exercise her choice. She then said: "I am reconciled to what my father did, but I wanted to make it known to women that fathers have no say in this matter."

This is a crucial principle: forced marriage is not permitted in Islam. The guardian cannot override the woman's objections. The consent of the woman — whether through explicit statement or silence (for a virgin) — is a requirement.

### 6. Marriage and Poverty

Qaradawi addresses a common barrier to marriage: poverty. He writes: "It is not befitting that a Muslim should refrain from marriage out of fear of poverty or of not being able to meet his obligations."

The Qur'an promises: "If they are in poverty, Allah will enrich them out of His bounty" (24:32). And the Prophet said: "There are three who have a right to the help of Allah: the one who seeks to marry out of the desire to live a chaste life..."

In *Economic Security in Islam* (chapters 1 and 4), Qaradawi extends this: the community bears responsibility to facilitate marriage for those who cannot afford it. Zakat funds may be used for this purpose. Umar ibn al-Khattab arranged his son's marriage and paid from the Public Treasury. The principle: poverty should not be a barrier to marriage, and the community must help.

### 7. Marriage Creates Kinship

Qaradawi highlights a often-overlooked dimension: marriage is not only a relationship between two individuals — it binds entire families. "It is He who has created from water mankind and made them related through lineage and marriage" (25:54).

Each marriage extends the sphere of love and kinship. When two families are joined through marriage, new relationships form: in-laws, extended family, new networks of mutual support. Islam's prohibition of marriage between close blood relatives is partly designed to "expand the circle of love" — directing the search for spouses outside the family, thus creating ever-widening networks of affection and social cohesion.

### 8. Seeing the Prospective Spouse

Qaradawi affirms that Islam permits — and encourages — seeing the prospective spouse before marriage. The Prophet told al-Mughirah ibn Shu'bah, who had proposed without seeing the woman: "Look at her, for it may produce love between you." And: "When one of you asks for a woman in marriage, if he is able to look at what will induce him to marry her, he should do so."

However, Qaradawi notes: this permission does not extend to unchaperoned dating. "Neither the father, the suitor, or the fiancée can stretch this permission to such an extent that the young man and woman, under the pretext of betrothal or engagement, go to movie theaters, clubs, and shopping places together without being accompanied by a mahram of hers."

The balance: see enough to make an informed decision, within the boundaries of Islamic propriety.

## Family Discussion (15 min)

### Question 1 — What Makes a Marriage Islamic?

> What makes a marriage Islamic vs. just cultural? Is it the ceremony? The contract? Or something deeper?

**Facilitator guidance:** The answer Qaradawi leads us to: an Islamic marriage is one based on (1) the solemn covenant with Allah, (2) spouse selection by religion and character, (3) the consent of both parties, (4) the mahr (dowry) from groom to bride, (5) witnesses and public announcement. A cultural marriage may have all the external forms but miss the inner principles — forced marriage (no consent), excessive dowry (reversed mahr), spouse selection by wealth/status (not religion).

### Question 2 — No Monasticism in Islam

> The Prophet said: "I marry women too. He who turns away from my Sunnah has no relation with me." What does this tell us about how Islam views family life? Is marriage a distraction from worship — or is it worship?

**Facilitator guidance:** The Prophet was the most devoted worshipper AND he was married with children. He explicitly rejected celibacy. This means: family life is not a distraction from faith — it IS part of faith. Providing for your family is worship. Being kind to your spouse is worship. Raising children with good character is worship. The monk who fasts every day and prays every night has turned away from the Sunnah — the married person who balances worship with family is following the Prophet.

### Question 3 — Religion and Character First

> The Prophet said: "Marry the religious one; otherwise you will regret it." Why does Islam prioritise religion and character over wealth, beauty, and status? What would happen if families chose spouses only for wealth or beauty?

**Facilitator guidance:** Wealth can be lost, beauty fades, status changes — but religion and character endure. A spouse with good religion will raise children with good religion. A spouse with good character will build a home of kindness, patience, and generosity. A spouse chosen only for wealth may be stingy. A spouse chosen only for beauty may be vain. The Prophetic criterion protects the family for generations.

**For teenagers:** "When you think about who you'd want to marry someday, what's the first thing you look for? Is it what the Prophet told us to look for?"

### Question 4 — The Girl's Consent

> A girl came to the Prophet saying her father had married her against her wishes. The Prophet allowed her to choose. She said: "I wanted to make it known to women that fathers have no say in this matter." What does this teach us about forced marriage?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is a crucial teaching: forced marriage is NOT Islamic. The woman's consent is required — explicitly for a previously married woman, and through silence (not objection) for a virgin. A father cannot force his daughter to marry someone she doesn't want. This is a protection that Islam gave women 1,400 years before most cultures recognised it.

### Question 5 — Poverty and Marriage

> The Qur'an promises: "If they are poor, Allah will enrich them of His bounty." And the Prophet said the one who marries to live chastely has "a right to the help of Allah." What does this mean for families who worry they can't afford to marry?

**Facilitator guidance:** Islam does not accept poverty as a reason to delay or avoid marriage. The promise is divine: Allah will provide. AND the community has a responsibility: to help those who cannot afford marriage, including through zakat. The Prophetic model: simple weddings, affordable dowries, community support. The cultural model: extravagant weddings, exorbitant dowries, debt — these are cultural violations of the Islamic principle, not Islamic requirements.

## Family Activity (10 min)

### Activity: "Marriage Wisdom Circle"

Married couples in the family share one piece of marriage wisdom with the unmarried members (children, teenagers, young adults). Unmarried members share one quality they would look for in a future spouse, guided by the Prophetic criteria.

**Structure for married couples:**
- "One thing I've learned about marriage that I wish I knew before: [wisdom]"
- "One thing I appreciate most about my spouse: [quality]"

**Structure for unmarried members:**
- "When I think about marriage, the quality I would prioritise most is: [quality]"
- "The Prophet said 'marry the religious one' — to me, this means: [understanding]"

**Goal:** The family learns from each other across generations. Married couples pass on lived wisdom. Unmarried members articulate their values. The Prophetic criterion (religion and character first) is reinforced through real examples.

**For children (under 7):** Instead of discussing spouse selection, ask: "What makes a good family?" Let them draw what a happy family looks like. Frame it as: "Allah made families so we could love each other."

## Notes for the Facilitator

### Before the Session
- Read the full source text: `/root/qaradawi-library/raw/extracted/halal-haram/ch-03.txt` (the marriage section, approximately lines 1070-1700)
- Read the concept page: `/root/qaradawi-library/concepts/concept-marriage.md`

### Key Distinction to Emphasize
The MOST important teaching: **marriage in Islam is worship, not a distraction from worship.** The Prophet was the most devoted servant of Allah AND he was married with children. He explicitly said: "He who turns away from my Sunnah has nothing to do with me" — and the Sunnah includes marriage.

This means:
- Providing for your family is worship
- Being kind to your spouse is worship
- Raising children with good character is worship
- The balance of worship, family, work, and rest is the Prophetic model
- Celibacy is not a "higher spiritual calling" — it is a rejection of the Sunnah

### The Consent Issue
This is a crucial contemporary application. Forced marriage is NOT Islamic. The Prophet gave women the right to choose. A girl who came to the Prophet complaining of a forced marriage was given the option to annul it. This teaching should be explicit in every Muslim family: no one can be forced to marry against their will.
