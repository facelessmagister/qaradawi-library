# Module 2.4 — Truthfulness and Trustworthiness

**Phase:** 2 — Trunk: Character and Inner Purification
**Week:** 10
**Duration:** 45–60 minutes

---

## Learning Objective

Family members understand that truthfulness is the bridge to Paradise and that sincerity (ikhlas) is the criterion for the acceptance of all deeds. The session establishes the Prophetic principle: "Actions are but by intentions" — and Qaradawi's critical qualification: good intention cannot override divine prohibition, but can elevate permissible acts into worship.

---

## Opening (3 min)

> بِسْمِ اللَّهِ وَالصَّلَاةُ وَالسَّلَامُ عَلَى رَسُولِ اللَّهِ

State the niyyah: we gather as a family to learn that honesty is not just a good habit — it is the bridge to Paradise — and that what we do matters less than WHY we do it. We learn to be truthful in speech and sincere in intention.

## Hadith (3 min)

### Hadith 1 — Actions Are by Intentions

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "The value of an action depends on the intentions behind it. A man will be rewarded only for what he intends."
>
> — **Narrated by al-Bukhari (1)** and **Muslim (1907)** on the authority of Umar ibn al-Khattab. Qaradawi notes: "This is the foundational hadith on intention."

### Hadith 2 — Truth Leads to Taqwa, Taqwa Leads to Paradise

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "Truth leads to piety (taqwa), and piety leads to Paradise. A man persists in telling the truth until he is recorded with Allah as truthful."
>
> — **Narrated by al-Bukhari and Muslim** on the authority of Abdullah ibn Mas'ud

### Hadith 3 — The Righteous Are Hidden

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "The righteous are those who are pious and hidden. If they are absent, they are not missed, and if they are present, they are not recognized. Their hearts are beacons of guidance."
>
> — **Narrated by Ibn Majah (3989)** and al-Hakim — cited by Qaradawi in Ethics in Islam, Section 4.1

### Hadith 4 — Intention Defines Even Sinful Acts

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "When two Muslims engage in combat against each other and one is killed, both are doomed to Hell." Someone asked: "O Messenger of Allah! As to the one who kills, it is understandable, but why the slain one?" He replied: "He was eager to kill his opponent."
>
> — **Narrated by al-Bukhari (31)** and **Muslim (2888)** on the authority of Abu Bakrah

## Teaching: Qaradawi's Treatment (15 min)

*Source: Yusuf al-Qaradawi, **Ethics in Islam** (أخلاق المسلم), Section 4.1: "Divine Ethics — Sincerity to Allah" (pp. 314-316). Also Chapter 3 (truth → taqwa → Paradise). All citations refer to these sections unless otherwise noted.*

### 1. Sincerity (Ikhlas): All Actions for Allah Alone

Qaradawi defines sincerity (ikhlas) as the principle that "all of one's actions are performed for Allah's sake alone." To achieve this, a person must have "no interest in materialistic objectives, such as wealth, social status, or power."

The Qur'an commands: "Say: Indeed, my prayers, my rituals of sacrifice, my living and my dying are for Allah, Lord of the worlds. No partner has He" (6:162-163). This verse encompasses ALL of life — prayer, sacrifice, living, dying — as acts dedicated solely to Allah.

Qaradawi explains: "Only righteous people can achieve this type of sincerity." The Prophet described the righteous as "those who are pious and hidden. If they are absent, they are not missed, and if they are present, they are not recognized. Their hearts are beacons of guidance."

### 2. The Foundational Hadith: Actions Are by Intentions

The hadith of Umar ibn al-Khattab — "Actions are but by intentions. A man will be rewarded only for what he intends" — is what Qaradawi calls the foundational hadith on intention. It establishes that the moral value of any action is determined not by the action itself but by the intention behind it.

Qaradawi presents several implications:

**a) Intention defines righteous action:** "People are even rewarded for their sincere intentions even if these intentions do not materialize as actions." The Qur'an says: "Whoever emigrates for the cause of Allah will find on the earth many locations and abundance. And whoever leaves his home as an emigrant to Allah and His Messenger and then death overtakes him — his reward has already become incumbent upon Allah" (4:100). The intention to emigrate is rewarded even if the person dies before completing the journey.

**b) Intention defines sinful action:** The Prophet said: "When two Muslims engage in combat and one is killed, both are doomed to Hell." The slain one is condemned because "he was eager to kill his opponent." His intention to kill was sinful even though he was the one who was killed. This demonstrates how intention defines the moral character of an act — even the victim's intention matters.

**c) Intention distinguishes murder from manslaughter:** Premeditated murder — where the intention to kill exists — is a sin leading to eternal damnation (4:93). Involuntary manslaughter — where there is no intention to kill — is not a sin but requires compensation (4:92). The difference is not in the outcome (a person died) but in the intention.

### 3. The Chain: Truth → Taqwa → Paradise

In Ethics in Islam Chapter 3, Qaradawi presents the Prophetic chain:

> "Truth leads to piety (taqwa), and piety leads to Paradise. A man persists in telling the truth until he is recorded with Allah as truthful."

This chain reveals the architecture of character development:
1. **Truth** (telling the truth consistently) → produces → **Taqwa** (God-consciousness)
2. **Taqwa** (God-consciousness) → leads to → **Paradise**
3. The persistence in truth → results in → being "recorded with Allah as truthful" — a permanent spiritual station

The implication for families: truthfulness is not just "not lying" — it is a spiritual practice that builds taqwa, which leads to Paradise. Every time a family member chooses truth over a convenient lie, they are building taqwa.

### 4. Good Intention Does NOT Override Prohibition

This is one of Qaradawi's most important qualifications, stated in *Diversion and Arts in Islam* (Chapter 1):

> "Good intentions, piety, or sincerity do not excuse transgression on Allah's Sovereignty."

The principle: intention operates WITHIN Allah's boundaries, not outside them.

| What Intention CAN Do | What Intention CANNOT Do |
|---|---|
| Elevate a permissible act to a meritorious one | Transform a prohibited act into a permissible one |
| Make eating a form of worship (with gratitude) | Make eating haram food permissible (even with gratitude) |
| Make sleeping a form of worship (with intention to wake for prayer) | Make missing prayer permissible (even with a good reason) |
| Transform diversion into "an approach to Allah" | Override a clear divine prohibition |

The logic: "Only Allah has the right to determine the lawful and unlawful." If a person could override Allah's prohibition by claiming a good intention, then human intention would become the ultimate authority — replacing divine authority. This is the essence of *shirk* in legislation: giving oneself the right to declare halal what Allah declared haram.

For families: "I'm lying to protect someone's feelings" — good intention, but lying remains haram. The solution is not to lie — it is to find a halal way to be kind (silence, deflection, or gentle truth). "I'm listening to music with bad lyrics because it helps me study" — good intention, but the content remains haram. The solution is to find music with good lyrics or to study in silence.

### 5. Good Intention Transforms Diversion into Worship

Conversely, Qaradawi states: "Good intention transforms diversion into an approach to Allah. It can intermingle fun with obedience to Allah."

This is the positive side of intention: when a permissible act is done with a good intention, it becomes a form of worship.
- Eating with gratitude → worship
- Sleeping with intention to wake for Fajr → worship
- Playing with children with intention to follow the Sunnah → worship
- Working with intention to provide halal for family → worship
- Smiling at a family member with intention of sadaqah → worship

Qaradawi adds: "Evil intention corrupts good deeds which are done for mere pretence rather than worship of Allah." The same action — prayer, charity, fasting — can be worship (with sincere intention) or hypocrisy (with intention to be seen).

The Qur'an warns: "When the hypocrites stand for prayer, they stand lazily, showing themselves to the people, and they do not remember Allah except a little" (4:142). The action (prayer) is the same; the intention (to be seen vs. to worship Allah) makes the difference.

### 6. The Hypocrisy of Showing Off (Riya')

Qaradawi identifies *riya'* (showing off) as the opposite of *ikhlas* (sincerity). Riya' is performing good deeds to be seen and praised by people rather than for Allah's sake.

The danger of riya' is that it corrupts the BEST deeds:
- Prayer done for show → no reward
- Charity given for reputation → no reward
- Knowledge sought for status → no reward
- Even jihad fought for glory → no reward

The Prophet said (in a hadith frequently cited by Qaradawi): The first person judged on the Day of Resurrection will be a martyr. Allah will remind him of His blessings, and the man will acknowledge them. Allah will ask: "What did you do with them?" He will say: "I fought for You until I died as a martyr." Allah will say: "You lie. You fought so that people would call you brave — and they did." Then he will be dragged on his face into Hell.

The same for the scholar who studied to be called learned, and the generous person who gave to be called generous. The action was good; the intention was corrupt; the result is Hell.

**For the family:** the question is not only "What did you do?" but "WHY did you do it?" A child who prays because Mom is watching has no reward. A child who prays because Allah told them to — even when no one is watching — has full reward. The difference is intention.

### 7. Self-Accountability (Muhasaba)

Qaradawi places intention within a sequence of divine ethics:

1. **Sincerity** (before action) — why am I doing this? For Allah alone.
2. **Observing Allah** (during action) — am I doing this in the best way? Allah sees me.
3. **Self-accountability** (after action) — did I do it well? What can I improve?

For self-accountability, Qaradawi cites the Prophet: "A wise man is one who calls himself to account and does noble deeds to benefit him after death. A foolish person is one who subdues himself to his temptations and seeks from Allah the fulfillment of his vain desires."

And Umar ibn al-Khattab: "Hold yourselves accountable before you are held accountable."

And Maymun ibn Mahran: "He who fears Allah holds himself accountable more strictly than a tyrant would hold his subjects accountable."

This is the daily practice: before bed, review the day. What did I do well? What could I have done better? What was my intention in each act?

## Family Discussion (15 min)

### Question 1 — Why Was the Prophet Called al-Amin?

> The Prophet was called "al-Amin" (the Trustworthy) before he was called a prophet. What does that tell us? Which comes first — being trustworthy or being religious?

**Facilitator guidance:** Character preceded message. The Prophet was trustworthy BEFORE revelation came. This means: good character is the foundation upon which faith is built. A person who is dishonest cannot truly be religious, no matter how much they pray. For children: "Before he became a prophet, everyone already trusted him. That's how important being truthful is."

### Question 2 — The Chain: Truth → Taqwa → Paradise

> The Prophet said truth LEADS TO taqwa, and taqwa LEADS TO Paradise. So telling the truth is actually a spiritual practice. Have you ever noticed that lying makes you feel farther from Allah, and truth makes you feel closer?

**Facilitator guidance:** This makes truthfulness a spiritual act, not just a moral one. Every lie creates a small distance from Allah. Every truth creates a small closeness. The cumulative effect: "A man persists in telling the truth until he is recorded with Allah as truthful." Consistency in truth builds a permanent spiritual identity.

### Question 3 — Doing Something for Allah vs. to Be Seen

> The Prophet said the righteous are "hidden — if they are absent, they are not missed, and if they are present, they are not recognized." What does that mean? Is it wrong to be recognized for good deeds?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is not about hiding good deeds — it is about the INTENTION. If you do good for Allah and people happen to notice, that's fine. If you do good SO THAT people will notice, that's riya'. The difference is in the heart. Ask: "If no one ever found out about your good deed — would you still do it? If the answer is yes, it's sincere. If the answer is no, it's riya'."

### Question 4 — Good Intention vs. Allah's Boundaries

> Qaradawi says: "Good intentions do not excuse transgression on Allah's Sovereignty." Can you think of a situation where someone might say "I have a good reason to do something haram"? Why doesn't that work?

**Facilitator guidance:** Examples for discussion:
- "I'm lying to protect someone's feelings" → lying is still haram; find a halal way to be kind
- "I'm cheating on the test because I need to pass for my parents" → cheating is still haram; study harder and trust Allah
- "I'm listening to inappropriate music because it helps me relax" → find halal relaxation
The principle: good intention is NOT a license to cross Allah's boundaries. It's a reason to find a halal path TO your good goal.

### Question 5 — What Is Your Intention Right Now?

> We're sitting in this halaqah. What is our intention? Are we here to learn for Allah's sake? Or for some other reason? Does our intention change the value of what we're doing?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is a live application of the teaching. The same halaqah can be worship (if the intention is to seek knowledge for Allah) or a social event (if the intention is to spend time together). Both are good — but only the first has full spiritual reward. Take a moment to silently renew your intention: "We are here to learn for Allah's sake."

## Family Activity (10 min)

### Activity: "Trust Challenge"

Each family member commits to ONE act of honesty this week that is DIFFICULT — something that costs them something to be truthful about.

**Examples:**
- Admitting a mistake you've been hiding
- Returning something you borrowed and forgot about
- Telling the truth when a lie would be more convenient
- Confessing to something you did wrong
- Apologizing for something you said
- Refusing to pass on gossip even when everyone else is

**At the next halaqah:** Share (voluntarily) what you did and how it felt. Discuss:
- Was it harder or easier than expected?
- What happened after you told the truth?
- Did you feel closer to Allah or farther?
- Would you do it again?

**The teaching:** "Truth leads to piety, and piety leads to Paradise." Every act of difficult truth is a step on this chain. The family that practices difficult honesty together builds taqwa together.

**Variation for young children (under 7):** Instead of a challenge, play "Truth or Not" — say a statement and have the child guess if it's true or not. Then ask: "How does it feel when someone tells you the truth? How does it feel when someone lies to you?" This builds the emotional foundation for truthfulness.

## Notes for the Facilitator

### Before the Session
- Read the full source text: `/root/qaradawi-library/raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-01.txt` (the "Sincerity to Allah" subsection, approximately lines 1-100)
- Also review the concept of taqwa from Module 1.5 (truth → taqwa → Paradise chain)

### Key Distinction to Emphasize
The MOST important teaching: **intention defines action.** The same prayer can be worship (with sincere intention) or hypocrisy (with intention to be seen). The same charity can be worship or reputation-building. The same jihad can be worship or glory-seeking. The action is identical; the intention makes the difference.

This means: families should focus not only on WHAT their children do (pray, fast, give charity) but WHY they do it (for Allah or for parents' approval? for show or for sincerity?). The spiritual education of children is primarily the education of intention.

### The Intention/Prohibition Balance
This is the most intellectually important point:

| Intention CAN... | Intention CANNOT... |
|---|---|
| Elevate halal to worship | Transform haram to halal |
| Give spiritual value to permissible acts | Override divine prohibition |
| Transform eating, sleeping, working into worship | Justify lying, cheating, or prohibited acts |

The principle: Allah sets the boundaries. Intention operates WITHIN those boundaries. If you have a good intention that requires a haram action, the answer is NOT "my intention makes it OK." The answer is "find a halal path to your good intention."

### Adapting for Age

| Age Group | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Under 7 | Focus on "telling the truth even when it's hard." Use the "Truth or Not" game. Skip the intention/prohibition discussion. |
| 7–12 | The story of al-Amin (the Prophet's title before prophethood): "Everyone trusted him before he was even a prophet! Being trustworthy is the first step." Ask: "Would your friends say you're trustworthy?" |
| 13–17 | The intention/prohibition discussion is crucial: "You can't say 'I have a good reason to do something haram.' Good intention doesn't override Allah's rules. Find a halal way." Also: riya' on social media — "Are you posting this for Allah or for likes?" |
| Adults | Read the full source section. Discuss the sequence: sincerity → observing Allah → self-accountability. Discuss the hadith of the martyr, scholar, and generous person who all ended up in Hell because of riya'. |

### If Time Is Short
If 60 minutes is not available, reduce the teaching to sections 2 (the foundational hadith), 4 (intention does not override prohibition), and 5 (intention transforms diversion to worship). Keep the full discussion — Questions 1 and 4 are essential.
