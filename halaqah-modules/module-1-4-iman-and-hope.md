# Module 1.4 — Iman and Hope

**Phase:** 1 — Roots: Faith as the Foundation
**Week:** 4
**Duration:** 45–60 minutes

---

## Learning Objective

Family members understand that hope is a source of strength rooted in faith, and that despair is contrary to iman. The session establishes the Qur'anic principle that "no one despairs of relief from Allah except the disbelieving people" and illustrates this through the stories of Prophets Ayyub, Ya'qub, Musa, and Muhammad (peace be upon them all).

---

## Opening (3 min)

Begin with the standard opening:

> بِسْمِ اللَّهِ وَالصَّلَاةُ وَالسَّلَامُ عَلَى رَسُولِ اللَّهِ

State the niyyah: we gather as a family to learn that hope is not wishful thinking — it is a power that comes from faith in Allah, and that the believer is the most hopeful and optimistic of all people.

## Hadith (3 min)

### Hadith 1 — The Divine Hadith on Allah's Nearness

The Prophet (peace be upon him) narrated that Allah said:

> "I am as My servant thinks I am, and I am with him whenever he remembers Me: if he draws near to Me a hand's span, I draw near to him an arm's length, and if he comes to Me walking, I go to him at speed."
>
> — **Narrated by al-Bukhari** on the authority of Abu Hurayrah

### Hadith 2 — Allah Stretches Out His Hand for Repentance

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said:

> "Allah stretches out His hand during the night so that the one who has committed a sin during the daytime may repent, and stretches out His hand during the daytime so that the one who has committed a sin during the night may repent. He rejoices at the repentance of His servant more than the stray one when he is found, the absent one when he returns, and the thirsty one when he finds water."
>
> — **Cited by Qaradawi in Faith and Life, Chapter 4**

### Hadith 3 — Relief Follows Difficulty

Ibn Mas'ud said:

> "If difficulty were to enter a hole, relief would surely follow it!"
>
> — **Cited by Qaradawi in Faith and Life, Chapter 4**

## Teaching: Qaradawi's Treatment (15 min)

*Source: Yusuf al-Qaradawi, **Faith and Life** (الإيمان والحياة), Chapter 4: "Iman and Hope." All citations refer to this chapter unless otherwise noted.*

### 1. Hope: The Elixir of Life

Qaradawi opens with a magnificent description of hope:

"Hope is one of the sources of security and tranquility for the believer. It is a ray that appears to man in the darkness of life and illuminates the way for him. This is the hope with which the tree of life grows and man enjoys the taste of happiness and feels the delight of life."

Hope is not passive wishing — it is a **motivating power**:

- It "drives the farmer to work hard" (hope in the coming harvest)
- It "urges the merchant to travel and take risks" (hope for profit)
- It "encourages the student to study hard and persistently" (hope for success)
- It "stimulates the soldier to show extreme courage" (hope for victory)
- It "makes bitter medicine sweet in the mouth of a patient" (hope of recovery)
- It "activates the believer to resist his vain desires and obey his Lord" (hope for Allah's Pleasure and Paradise)

Qaradawi concludes: "In short, hope is the elixir of life, motivator of its energy, alleviator of its calamities, and an incentive of delight and joy therein. Before all this, hope is sweet-tasting and beautiful in its own right, whether it is realized or not."

### 2. Despair: The Poison That Destroys

Despair is the opposite of hope. Qaradawi describes it: "It is an elimination of hope and disappearance of the motives of expectations and wishes in the heart. It is the insurmountable obstacle that destroys the motives of activity in the soul and weakens the incentives of strength in the body."

He cites Ibn Mas'ud: **"Loss is involved in two things: despair and self-satisfaction."**

Imam al-Ghazali commented: "He connected these two because happiness is only obtained through persistent pursuit and continuous perseverance. But the person in despair does not seek to achieve anything 'because what he wants is impossible in his sight.' As for the one who is self-satisfied, he thinks that he has already achieved his goal, so he does not pursue it any further."

Qaradawi illustrates with two examples:

**The despairing student:** "If a pupil is in despair of success, he keeps away from his books, becomes bored at school and at home, and no longer benefits from private lessons, advice, or preparation — unless his hope for success is restored."

**The despairing patient:** "If a patient is in despair of recovery, he hates the medicine, the doctor, the clinic, and the drugstore. No medicine is effective for him unless he restores hope in his recovery."

His conclusion: "When despair overcomes any man, the world turns black in his eyes, all gates are closed before him, all means to recovery seem unavailable, and the earth, despite its vastness, is straitened in his sight. This is despair: **it is a poison that has a slow effect and a volcano that destroys man's activity.**"

### 3. Despair and Disbelief Are Interrelated

Qaradawi makes a profound psychological-theological connection:

"There is no wonder that this kind of person is commonly found among those who deny Allah or who have weak faith in Him. This is because they live only by themselves, disconnecting all relations with the universe and its Lord. Thus there is no wonder that such people are the most afflicted with despair and that the latter are the most disbelieving among the disbelievers, as there is **a connection between despair and disbelief; each of them is a cause and a result of the other. Despair produces disbelief and disbelief produces despair.**"

The Qur'an confirms: "Truly no one despairs of Allah's Soothing Mercy except those who have no faith" (12:87). And: "Who despairs of the mercy of his Lord, but such as go astray?" (15:56).

Qaradawi also notes that despair is a prerequisite of **doubt**, not just disbelief: "Everyone who loses firm certainty in Allah and His meeting, Wisdom, and Justice, has been deprived of hope and an optimistic view towards people, the universe, and life. Such a person looks at the world through black glasses, seeing the earth as a forest, people as wild beasts, and living as an unbearable burden."

### 4. Iman Produces Hope

Conversely, iman and hope are interrelated: "The believer is the most hopeful, optimistic, and cheerful among people and the farthest one from pessimism, boredom, and weariness."

Why? Because iman means believing in:
- A supreme power that disposes of the affairs of this universe
- A supreme power that has knowledge of all things and is able to do all things
- An unrestricted power, an absolute mercy, and a limitless generosity
- An All-Mighty and Ever-Merciful God, Who responds to the distressed when he calls, removes evil, gives plentifully, forgives sins, accepts repentance, and Who is "more merciful to His servants than a mother to her babe and more beneficent to His creatures than themselves"

Qaradawi writes: "The believer who seeks protection with God — Who is Beneficent, Ever-Merciful, Exalted in Might, Most Generous, Oft-Forgiving, Full of loving kindness, Lord of the Throne of Glory, and Doer of all that He intends — enjoys limitless hope, cheerfulness, and optimism. He approaches life with a high spirit and receives its events with a smiling face, and not sullenly."

The believer's hope manifests in every situation:

| Situation | The Believer's Hope |
|---|---|
| If he fights | He is sure of victory — "They would certainly be assisted" (37:172-173) |
| If he falls ill | He never loses hope of recovery — "When I am ill, it is He Who cures me" (26:80) |
| If he commits a sin | He never loses hope of forgiveness — "Despair not of the Mercy of Allah: for Allah forgives all sins" (39:53) |
| If he faces difficulty | He hopes for relief — "With every difficulty there is relief" (94:5-6) |
| If afflicted with calamity | "To Allah we belong, and to Him is our return" (2:156) |
| If someone hates him | He hopes for reconciliation — "It may be that Allah will grant love between you" (60:7) |
| If falsehood triumphs | He is certain truth will prevail — "We hurl the Truth against falsehood, and it knocks out its brain" (21:18) |

### 5. The Story of Prophet Ayyub (Job)

Qaradawi presents the story of Ayyub as "the most illustrious example" of never losing hope:

"Ayyub was a thoroughly good man who almost had everything in life. He was wealthy, had many sons and daughters, had a faithful wife, and good friends. He never ceased thanking Allah for His many blessings, and was bounteous and helpful to people around him."

Then came the trials — one after another:

1. **He lost his friends**
2. **He lost all his children**
3. **He was inflicted with an incurable, hateful disease** that alienated him from all people except his loyal wife
4. **Poverty ensued** — his wife had to sell her lovely hair for sustenance
5. **Even his wife had to desert him**

"Even then he turned to Allah to ask Him to restore his health and to relieve his tongue in order to be able to utter His praise to Him."

Allah answered: "He inspired him to wash his feet in cold running water in the spring that erupted for him and to have a cold drink. When he did, he soon recovered; his health was totally restored, his wife and children returned. **It was one of the miracles of Allah.**"

The Qur'an records Ayyub's supplication: "Truly distress has seized me, but You are the Most Merciful of those that are merciful." So Allah listened to him: "We removed the distress that was on him, and We restored his people to him, and doubled their number, as a Grace from Ourselves" (21:83-84).

### 6. Other Prophetic Examples of Hope

Qaradawi provides a cascade of prophetic examples:

**Prophet Ya'qub (Jacob):** Lost his son Yusuf for many years. Then Yusuf's full brother was confined in Egypt. Despite all this, despair never penetrated his heart. He said: "So patience is most fitting for me. Maybe Allah will bring them all back to me in the end. For He is indeed full of knowledge and wisdom" (12:83). And: "O my sons! Go and enquire about Yusuf and his brother, and never give up hope of Allah's Soothing Mercy: truly no one despairs of Allah's Soothing Mercy, except those who have no faith" (12:87).

**Prophet Musa (Moses):** Traveled by night with his people to escape Pharaoh. The sea was before them, the enemy behind. His people said: "We are sure to be overtaken!" But Musa was not scared nor did he despair: "By no means! My Lord is with me! Soon will He guide me!" (26:60-62). Allah told him to strike the sea with his rod — it divided, and they were saved.

**Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him):** During the Hijra, pursued by the Quraysh, hiding in the cave of Thawr. The tracker said: "Muhammad did not overstep this place. He either ascended to heaven or descended below." Abu Bakr wept: "If anyone of them looks beneath his feet, he will see us!" The Prophet said calmly: **"What do you think of two people when Allah makes the third among them?"** (9:40).

**The Battle of al-Ahzab:** All enemies united — Quraysh, Ghatafan, the Jews, the hypocrites. The Qur'an describes: "They came on you from above you and from below you, and the eyes became dim and the hearts gaped up to the throats" (33:10-11). Under these terrible circumstances, "when hope usually fades and one only thinks about salvation, the Prophet helped his Companions dig a trench... and kept speaking to them about the prospective future, when Allah would help them conquer the lands of Khosrau in Persia, the lands of Caesar in Syria, and the Yemeni lands." The hypocrites mocked: "Allah and His Messenger promised us nothing but delusions" (33:12). But the Prophet's hope was not delusion — it was certainty based on Allah's promise.

### 7. The Prophet's Lesson to Khabbab

Qaradawi closes with a powerful hadith. Khabbab ibn al-Aratt was severely tortured — hot iron bars placed on his back until he could bear it no longer. He went to the Prophet and said: "Will you not invoke Allah for us?"

The Prophet became angry at this hastiness and gave a lesson in patience and hope:

> "A believing man in the nations before you would be combed with iron combs that would remove his flesh from the bones and nerves, and he would be cut by a saw into two pieces, but that did not divert him from his religion. By Him in Whose Hand is my soul, Allah will make this matter prevail to the extent that a traveler from San'a' to Hadhramaut will fear nothing but Allah, and a wolf as regards his sheep — but you are hasty!"

Qaradawi concludes: "What do you call this ray of sunlight that appears in the darkness of events and lightens the way before the people? It is hope. You may also call it belief in the help of Allah."

## Family Discussion (15 min)

### Question 1 — What Are You Hoping For?

> What is something you hoped for and got? What is something you are hoping for right now? How does hoping make you feel?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is a warm opening. Let everyone share. The point: hope is "sweet-tasting and beautiful in its own right, whether it is realized or not." The ACT of hoping gives energy and motivation, regardless of the outcome.

**Qaradawi's framework:** Hope "is the elixir of life, motivator of its energy, alleviator of its calamities."

### Question 2 — The Despairing Student and Patient

> Qaradawi says a despairing student stops studying and a despairing patient stops taking medicine. Have you ever felt "what's the point?" How did you get through it?

**Facilitator guidance:** This makes the abstract concept of despair personal. Everyone has felt "what's the point" at some point — a difficult subject at school, a conflict that seems unresolvable, a health issue that drags on. The teaching: despair is a poison that stops action. Hope — even small hope — restarts action. The student who restores hope starts studying again; the patient who restores hope takes medicine again.

### Question 3 — The Story of Prophet Ayyub

> Prophet Ayyub lost his friends, his children, his health, and his wealth. His wife had to sell her hair. But he never lost faith. What gives a person that kind of inner strength? How does his story inspire our family?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is the most powerful story in the chapter. Ayyub lost EVERYTHING — not one thing, but everything, one after another. And he turned to Allah not to complain but to ask for his health back "in order to be able to utter His praise." His concern was not his suffering but his ability to worship. This is the highest form of hope: trusting Allah even when everything is taken away.

**For children:** Tell it as a story — "There was a man who had a big house, lots of children, good friends, and lots of money. Then one by one, he lost everything. But he never said 'I hate Allah.' He always said 'Allah will help me.' And Allah did help him — He gave everything back, double!"

### Question 4 — Despair and Disbelief

> The Qur'an says "no one despairs of relief from Allah except the disbelieving people." Does this mean despair is a form of disbelief? How can we protect our family from despair?

**Facilitator guidance:** Qaradawi's key insight: "despair produces disbelief and disbelief produces despair." The connection is psychological — when you disconnect from Allah, you lose hope because you have nothing beyond yourself. The believer, connected to Allah, always has hope because "Allah is with him." For the family: the protection against despair is maintaining the connection with Allah through prayer, du'a, and dhikr.

### Question 5 — The Prophet in the Cave

> The Prophet and Abu Bakr were hiding in a cave. Their enemies were right outside. Abu Bakr was afraid. The Prophet said: "What do you think of two people when Allah makes the third among them?" How does this apply to our family when we face fear?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is the practical application. When the family faces fear — financial difficulty, health scares, job insecurity, social pressure — the question is: is Allah with us? If we are doing what is right, the answer is yes. And "if Allah is with us, who can be against us?"

## Family Activity (10 min)

### Activity: "Family Hope Jar"

Materials: a jar, small pieces of paper, pens.

Each family member writes down ONE hope or goal for the coming month. It can be anything:

- A personal goal ("I hope to memorize a new surah")
- A family hope ("I hope we can all go somewhere together")
- A spiritual hope ("I hope to pray on time every day")
- A relational hope ("I hope to be more patient with my sibling")

Fold the papers and put them in the jar. Place the jar in a visible family space.

**At the end of the month** (or at the next halaqah cycle), open the jar and read the hopes together. Discuss:

- Which hopes were realized? Thank Allah together.
- Which hopes were not yet realized? Discuss: are we still hoping? What can we do to "tie our camel" while trusting Allah?
- Were any hopes transformed into something different but better?

**The teaching:** Hope is not passive — it motivates action. The farmer who hopes for a harvest WORKS. The student who hopes for success STUDIES. The believer who hopes for Allah's pleasure OBEYS.

**Variation for young children (under 7):** Instead of writing, children draw what they hope for. Help them say it out loud: "I hope [X], and I will ask Allah for it."

## Notes for the Facilitator

### Before the Session
- Read the full source text: `/root/qaradawi-library/raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-04.txt`
- The chapter is approximately 27 pages. Key sections: "Hope is important" (line 24), "Despair and disbelief" (line 114), "Iman produces hope" (line 205), "The necessity of hope in life" (line 529).

### Key Distinction to Emphasize
Qaradawi is very precise: hope is not naive optimism ("everything will be fine") — it is **faith-grounded certainty** that Allah's mercy, power, and wisdom will prevail. The difference:

| Naive Optimism | Faith-Based Hope |
|---|---|
| "Things will work out somehow" | "Allah will make a way out for those who fear Him" (65:2) |
| Based on wishful thinking | Based on Allah's attributes and promises |
| Disappears when things get worse | Grows stronger in hardship |
| Passive — waits for good fortune | Active — motivates effort and patience |

The Prophet in the cave was not blindly optimistic — he had taken every precaution (the route, the cave, the guide, the provisions) AND he trusted Allah completely. This is the model: act AND trust.

### The Story of Prophet Ayyub
This story is the emotional center of the chapter. Present it as a narrative, not a lecture. Key moments to emphasize:
1. Ayyub had everything — wealth, children, wife, friends, health
2. He was grateful and generous — not complacent
3. The losses came one by one — not all at once
4. His wife sold her hair — the ultimate poverty
5. Even his wife had to leave — total isolation
6. He turned to Allah not to complain but to praise
7. Allah's answer: a spring of water, total restoration, double the family

The point for families: no matter how bad things get, Allah can restore everything. The story of Ayyub is the Qur'an's guarantee that hope is never futile.

### The Prophet in the Cave
This story teaches the COMBINATION of hope and action. The Prophet:
- Took an uncommon route (action)
- Chose a cave (action)
- Had a guide (action)
- Trusted provisions to be brought (action)
- Then said: "Allah is with us" (hope)

This is the model for the family: tie your camel AND rely on Allah.

### Adapting for Age

| Age Group | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Under 7 | Focus on the story of Prophet Ayyub — tell it as a story of a man who lost everything but Allah gave it back. Use the hope jar activity. Skip the despair/disbelief discussion. |
| 7–12 | Tell the Ayyub story AND the Musa story (sea parting). Ask: "If you were Musa and saw the sea in front and the army behind, what would you do? Musa said 'My Lord is with me!'" |
| 13–17 | Connect to mental health: "The Qur'an says despair is connected to disbelief. Does that mean depression is a sin?" Answer: No — sadness is natural. Despair (giving up on Allah) is different from grief (feeling pain but still trusting Allah). Ayyub felt pain but never gave up on Allah. |
| Adults | Read the full chapter. Discuss the Ibn Mas'ud/al-Ghazali framework on despair and self-satisfaction. Discuss the connection between materialism and despair — the person who lives "only by themselves, disconnecting all relations with the universe and its Lord." |

### If Time Is Short
