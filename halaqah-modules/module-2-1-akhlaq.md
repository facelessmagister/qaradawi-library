# Module 2.1 — Akhlaq: Good Character as the Core of Islam

**Phase:** 2 — Trunk: Character and Inner Purification
**Week:** 7
**Duration:** 45–60 minutes

---

## Learning Objective

Family members understand that good character (akhlaq) is not an optional add-on to Islam — it IS the core of the Prophet's mission. The session establishes that character is what you ARE (not what you appear to do), that character can be changed through practice and companionship, and that good character outweighs ritual on the scales of deeds.

---

## Opening (3 min)

> بِسْمِ اللَّهِ وَالصَّلَاةُ وَالسَّلَامُ عَلَى رَسُولِ اللَّهِ

State the niyyah: we gather as a family to learn that Islam is not only prayer and fasting — it is character. The Prophet was sent to perfect good morals, and our family's character is the true measure of our faith.

## Teaching: Qaradawi's Treatment (15 min)

*Source: Yusuf al-Qaradawi, **Ethics in Islam** (أخلاق المسلم), Section 1.1: "Definitions and Concepts of Islamic Ethics." All citations refer to this section unless otherwise noted.*

### 1. What Is Akhlaq?

The Arabic word *akhlaq* (أَخْلَاق) is the plural of *khuluq* (خُلُق), meaning an individual's moral disposition. The singular form appears in the Qur'an as a descriptor of the Prophet: "And indeed, you are of a great moral character" (68:4).

The word *khuluq* shares a root with *khalq* (خَلْق), meaning "creation." This linguistic connection has deep significance. Al-Raghib al-Asfahani (d. AH 502) explains: "Moral character and creation are two sides of the same coin. Creation has to do with the physical features that can be perceived visually, whereas moral character has to do with personal qualities that cannot be perceived directly."

Qaradawi adopts a definition based on the hadith "Nothing is heavier on the scales of deeds than good morals":

> **Moral character is the individual's inner self with its properties and traits, which can be good or evil. Divine reward and punishment are directly related to these properties and traits, rather than to physical features.**

### 2. Character vs. Acts: Al-Ghazali's Critical Distinction

Imam al-Ghazali (d. AH 505) makes a distinction that Qaradawi adopts as fundamental:

**Moral character** = stable, well-established dispositions that lead to actions "without thinking or planning."

**Moral acts** = individual behaviors that may or may not reflect true character.

Al-Ghazali explains: "A person can be described as having good *khalq* (appearance) and good *khuluq* (moral character) if he is pleasant on the inside and outside." But the key qualification: "Someone who acts generously on a whim or for ulterior goals cannot be described as genuinely generous. For a person to be described as such, he needs to act generously **consistently**. Similarly, a person who struggles to remain silent when provoked cannot be described as patient; **patience has to be natural, consistent, and effortless**."

This means: **character is what you ARE, not what you APPEAR to do.** A stingy person might perform a generous act — but for hidden reasons. A coward might show bravery once — but under compulsion. Character is the stable disposition, not the isolated act.

### 3. Dr. Draz's Refinement: Willful Powers

Dr. Muhammad Abdullah Draz (mid-20th century) refines the definition further. He argues that the "inner self" includes intellectual abilities (memory, imagination, reasoning) and aesthetic capacities (taste, eloquence) — but these are NOT moral traits. Moral traits are specifically **willful powers** — they involve choices that can be judged as good or evil.

Qaradawi adopts this refinement: "Moral character is a set of well-established **willful powers** that trigger good or evil choices." This means:
- Having a good memory is not a moral trait
- Having artistic talent is not a moral trait
- Choosing honesty over lying IS a moral trait
- Choosing patience over anger IS a moral trait

For an action to reliably indicate character, it must meet two criteria:
1. **Consistency**: it recurs in a pattern — single actions cannot establish character judgment
2. **Self-motivation**: it is willful and self-directed, not driven by fear, shyness, or social pressure

### 4. Two Types of Character: Innate and Acquired

Qaradawi, following the classical tradition, identifies two types:

**a) Innate (gharizi):** moral traits a person is born with, endowed by Allah. Evidence: the hadith of al-Mundhir al-Ashaj — when told he had traits Allah likes (patience and forbearance), al-Mundhir asked if these were his choice or Allah's creation. The Prophet confirmed: "It is Allah who endowed you with them."

**b) Acquired (muktasab):** moral traits developed through practice, training, companionship, and deliberate effort. Evidence: the Qur'an teaches that training can reshape nature (5:4) — "If animals can be trained, people can definitely be well trained, for they are more capable of learning."

Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya (d. AH 751) states: "Moral traits can be acquired through practice and training until they become **second nature**." Innate traits are more powerful initially, but acquired traits — through persistent practice — can become as firmly established as innate ones. **Habits form character.**

### 5. Character IS Mutable — Not Fixed

One of the most important arguments in Qaradawi's treatment: **moral character can be changed.**

Al-Ghazali emphatically rejects the claim that character is immutable: "If that was true, people would not be advised to strive to improve their moral character, and all the moral wisdom and teachings in the world would be an exercise in futility. Deeds are indeed the outcomes of moral character, but moral character can be changed. **How can anyone deny that human behavior can be modified and improved? Animals can learn to behave better, and surely humans can too. Dogs and horses can be trained to be gentle, and people too.**"

This is a message of profound hope for families: no one is stuck with bad character. Just as a horse can be trained to be gentle, a child can be trained to be patient. Just as a dog can learn obedience, a person can learn generosity. Character is not destiny — it is a project.

### 6. Companionship Shapes Character

Al-Raghib al-Asfahani (d. AH 502) warns: "Company makes bad people good, and the reverse is also true." People acquire moral traits not only through interaction but even through observation:

"Looking at people who are constantly sad or happy generates the same feelings in those around them. This is true even for animals and plants. A disobedient camel becomes gentle with regular interactions with gentle camels, and vice versa. A healthy plant might wilt if left with wilted plants."

Qaradawi draws the contemporary implication: if companionship shapes character — then the friends we choose, the media we consume, and the environment we create in our home ALL shape our family's character. The Prophet said: "Choose wisely for your progeny, for veins run deep" — recognizing both hereditary and environmental factors.

### 7. Accountability: Innate Traits vs. Actions

Al-Ezz Ibn Abd al-Salam (d. AH 660) articulates a principle of moral responsibility:

- **People are NOT rewarded merely for having good traits** — because they did not choose them
- **People ARE rewarded for acting upon good traits** — because actions are choices
- **People are NOT punished merely for having evil traits** — because they did not choose them
- **People ARE punished for choosing to act on evil traits**

This balances divine sovereignty (Allah creates dispositions) with human accountability (people choose their actions). Everyone endowed with good traits is rewarded for acting accordingly; everyone with immoral tendencies is punished only for willfully following through.

**For the family:** a child born with a quick temper is not blamed for the tendency — but is responsible for learning to control it. A child born naturally patient is not praised merely for the disposition — but is rewarded for choosing patience in difficult moments.

### 8. Akhlaq Is the Heaviest Deed

Qaradawi elevates akhlaq above all other deeds through the prophetic hadiths:

- "Nothing is heavier on the scales of deeds than good morals" — good character outweighs prayer, fasting, and charity on the Day of Judgment
- "A person can reach the status of those who fast and spend the night praying by having a good moral character" — character achieves the rank of voluntary worship
- "The most faithful are those with the best moral character" — character is the measure of faith itself
- "I have been sent to perfect good morals and conduct" — character was the Prophet's mission statement

This means: a family that prays together but treats each other harshly has missed the point. A family that may not pray perfectly but treats each other with kindness, patience, and generosity is closer to the Prophet's mission.

Aisha's description of the Prophet captures it perfectly: "His manners were the Qur'an." He didn't just recite the Qur'an — he LIVED it. His character was the Qur'an in human form.

## Family Discussion (15 min)

### Question 1 — What Is Character?

> What is character? Is it what you do when people are watching, or what you do when no one is watching?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is the opening question. The answer Qaradawi leads us to (via al-Ghazali): character is what you ARE, not what you APPEAR to do. It is the stable disposition — what comes naturally, consistently, and effortlessly. A single generous act doesn't make you generous. Consistent generosity does.

**For children:** "If you share your toy once because Mom tells you to, does that make you a generous person? What if you share every day, even when no one asks? THAT is character."

### Question 2 — The Prophet's Mission

> The Prophet said he was sent to "perfect good morals." Not to build mosques, not to write books, not to conquer lands — but to perfect good morals. What does that tell us about what Islam is really about?

**Facilitator guidance:** This question reorients the family's understanding of Islam. Islam is not only rules and rituals — it is character. If the Prophet's mission was character, then our family's mission is character. Ask: "If someone looked at our family and said 'they are good Muslims,' what would they be seeing? Rules? Or character?"

### Question 3 — Can Character Be Changed?

> Qaradawi says character CAN be changed — just like horses and dogs can be trained. Al-Ghazali said: "How can anyone deny that human behavior can be modified?" Is there a trait in yourself you want to change? How could you start?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is a message of hope. No one is stuck. The child who is quick-tempered can learn patience. The adult who is stingy can learn generosity. The teenager who is shy can learn confidence. The method: practice the trait until it becomes habit. "Act patiently until you become patient. Act generously until you become generous."

**For children:** "If a horse can learn to be gentle, can you learn to be patient? Yes! You just have to practice, practice, practice, until it becomes natural."

### Question 4 — Who Do You Admire?

> Who in our family has a character trait the others admire? What makes it admirable? Is it something they were born with, or something they worked on?

**Facilitator guidance:** This connects the abstract teaching to real family life. Let each person name a trait they admire in another family member. Discuss: is this trait innate (Allah-given) or acquired (through practice)? The answer is usually both — Allah gives a seed, and the person cultivates it through effort.

### Question 5 — Companionship and Character

> Al-Raghib al-Asfahani said "company makes bad people good, and the reverse is also true." How does who we spend time with — including what we watch and listen to — shape our character?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is the contemporary application. Companionship is not only physical friends — it includes YouTube, TikTok, video games, books, and the general media environment. If you spend hours watching content that promotes arrogance, materialism, or disrespect, it shapes your character. If you spend time with people (in person or through content) who model kindness, patience, and faith, it shapes your character.

## Family Activity (10 min)

### Activity: "Character Spotlight"

Each week, one family member is "spotlighted." The other family members share:

1. One good character trait they see in that person
2. One specific example of when they saw that trait in action
3. How that trait inspires them

**Structure:**
- "I see [trait] in [name]. I saw it when [specific example]. It inspires me to [action]."

**Example for a child:**
- "I see patience in [son]. I saw it when his little sister broke his toy and he didn't yell. It inspires me to be patient when I'm frustrated."

**Example for a parent:**
- "I see generosity in [father]. I saw it when he helped our neighbor without being asked. It inspires me to look for ways to help others."

**Goal:** The family learns to see and name good character in each other. This practice does three things:
1. It teaches the vocabulary of character (patience, generosity, kindness, honesty)
2. It reinforces good character through recognition (the hadith says good character is the heaviest deed — so naming it matters)
3. It builds family bonds through appreciation

**Variation for young children (under 7):** Instead of speaking, draw a picture of the spotlighted person doing the good thing. Show the picture and say: "[Name] is [trait] because [example]."

## Notes for the Facilitator

### Before the Session
- Read the full source text: `/root/qaradawi-library/raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-01-01.txt`
- The section is approximately 25 pages. Key sections: definitions (line 1), al-Ghazali's definition (line 72), Dr. Draz's refinement (line 109), types of ethics (line 159), al-Ghazali on mutability (line 267), genetics and environment (line 298).

### Key Distinction to Emphasize
The MOST important teaching in this module: **character is what you ARE, not what you appear to do.** This is al-Ghazali's distinction, adopted by Qaradawi.

| Character (what you ARE) | Acts (what you appear to DO) |
|---|---|
| Stable, consistent disposition | Isolated behavior |
| Natural and effortless | Forced or calculated |
| Motivated by inner virtue | Motivated by fear, shyness, or external pressure |
| Visible in all situations | Visible only in certain situations |

For families: a child who is polite to guests but rude to siblings is showing acts, not character. A child who is kind to everyone, including siblings, is showing character. The difference is consistency and self-motivation.

### The Mutability Message
This is the most hopeful teaching: **character can be changed.** Al-Ghazali's statement — "How can anyone deny that human behavior can be modified and improved? Animals can learn to behave better, and surely humans can too" — is a message of liberation for any family member who feels stuck in a bad habit.

The method: practice the trait until it becomes habit. "Act patiently until you become patient. Act generously until you become generous." This is not theory — it is a practical program for character development.
