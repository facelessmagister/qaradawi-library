# Module 1.5 — Taqwa: God-Consciousness in Daily Life

**Phase:** 1 — Roots: Faith as the Foundation
**Week:** 5
**Duration:** 45–60 minutes

---

## Learning Objective

Family members understand that taqwa (God-consciousness) is being aware of Allah in every moment — not just during prayer. The session establishes that taqwa is the sole criterion of nobility before Allah, the stated purpose of worship, and the governing principle of how a believer engages with entertainment, relationships, and daily decisions.

---

## Opening (3 min)

Begin with the standard opening:

> بِسْمِ اللَّهِ وَالصَّلَاةُ وَالسَّلَامُ عَلَى رَسُولِ اللَّهِ

State the niyyah: we gather as a family to learn what it means to be conscious of Allah in every moment, not only in prayer — so that taqwa becomes the lens through which we see everything.

>
> — **Narrated by Muslim** on the authority of Abu Hurayrah

## Teaching: Qaradawi's Treatment (15 min)

*Sources: Yusuf al-Qaradawi, **Ethics in Islam** (أخلاق المسلم), Chapters 1, 3, and 4; **Diversion and Arts in Islam** (الترفيه والفنون في الإسلام), Chapters 1 and 2; **Education and Economy in the Sunnah**, Chapter 1. All citations refer to these sources unless otherwise noted.*

### 1. What Is Taqwa?

Qaradawi defines taqwa as "the comprehensive Islamic virtue of God-consciousness — a state of inner awareness of Allah's presence, reverence for His commandments, and diligent avoidance of His prohibitions."

The term derives from the root **w-q-y**, meaning "to protect" or "to shield." The *muttaqi* (one who has taqwa) is thus one who shields himself from Allah's punishment by adhering to His commands. Taqwa encompasses both the **inner** disposition of the heart (fear of Allah, love of Allah, desire for His pleasure) and the **outer** discipline of actions (performing obligations, avoiding sins, fulfilling rights).

The related term *birr* (بِرّ) — often translated "piety" or "righteousness" — is treated by Qaradawi as effectively synonymous with taqwa in many contexts. The Qur'an itself defines *birr* in terms of taqwa: "Piety is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but piety is [in] one who believes in Allah… and fears Allah" (2:177).

### 2. Taqwa as the Foundation of Islamic Ethics

In *Ethics in Islam* (Chapter 3), Qaradawi places taqwa at the foundation of Islamic moral life. He argues that the entire structure of Islamic ethics rests on the believer's consciousness of Allah's omniscience and omnipresence.

**Without taqwa**, moral rules become mere external regulations — a person follows them when watched and breaks them when alone.

**With taqwa**, moral rules become expressions of an inner reality — a person follows them whether watched or not, because they know Allah is always watching.

The Prophetic hadith establishes the chain: "Truth leads to piety (taqwa), and piety leads to Paradise." This chain — **truth → taqwa → Paradise** — demonstrates that taqwa is both the fruit of virtuous action AND the gateway to salvation.

### 3. Taqwa as the Purpose of Worship

Qaradawi emphasizes that taqwa is not an incidental byproduct of worship — it is the **divinely stated purpose** of several acts of worship:

| Act of Worship | Qur'anic Statement | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Fasting | "Fasting is prescribed for you… that you may attain taqwa" | 2:183 |
| Pilgrimage | "The best provision is taqwa" | 2:197 |
| Sacrifice | "What reaches Him is taqwa from you" | 22:37 |

This means the purpose of fasting is not just hunger — it is taqwa. The purpose of Hajj is not just travel — it is taqwa. The purpose of sacrifice is not just meat — it is taqwa. If these acts of worship do not produce taqwa, they have not achieved their divinely intended outcome.

### 4. Taqwa and Intention (Niyyah)

In *Diversion and Arts in Islam* (Chapter 1), Qaradawi addresses the relationship between taqwa and intention with characteristic precision:

"Good intentions, piety, or sincerity do not excuse transgression on Allah's Sovereignty."

This is a critical principle. It means:
- A person cannot say: "I have a good intention, so this haram thing I'm doing is OK."
- Intention cannot transform a prohibited act into a permissible one.
- Intention CAN elevate a permissible act into a meritorious one.

At the same time, Qaradawi emphasizes that "good intention transforms diversion into an approach to Allah." So intention matters — but it operates within Allah's boundaries, not outside them.

Taqwa therefore means: **submitting to Allah's boundaries even when your intention seems good**, while simultaneously recognizing that a genuine act of taqwa must be animated by sincere intention — not mere outward compliance.

### 5. Taqwa Balances Fear and Hope

Qaradawi, following the classical tradition, insists that taqwa must balance fear (*khawf*) and hope (*rajā'*). The believer who fears Allah without hoping in His mercy falls into despair. The one who hopes without fear falls into complacency.

In *Ethics in Islam* (Chapter 4), Qaradawi presents this balance as one of the complementary principles of Islamic ethics — alongside mutual consultation (*shūrā*) and enjoining good and forbidding evil.

The Prophet's comprehensive du'a captures this balance perfectly: "O Allah! I beseech You for **guidance, piety (taqwa), chastity ('afaf), and self-sufficiency (ghina)**." Taqwa is paired with guidance (you need to know the right path before you can walk it with taqwa), chastity (the practical fruit of taqwa in behavior), and self-sufficiency (the inner contentment that taqwa produces).

### 6. Taqwa in Entertainment and Daily Life

One of Qaradawi's most distinctive contributions is extending taqwa into the domain of entertainment and leisure. In *Diversion and Arts in Islam* (Chapter 1), he argues:

- Moderate diversion is lawful and even psychologically necessary
- BUT the person of taqwa exercises self-regulation: diversion must not encroach upon religious obligations, must not involve prohibited content, and must be kept proportionate

The Qur'an affirms: "O children of Adam, take your adornment at every masjid… Who has forbidden the adornment of Allah which He has produced for His servants?" (7:31-32). Taqwa does not mean rejecting all pleasure — it means channeling pleasure within divine boundaries.

The Prophet's hadith — "Allah does not look at your appearances and your wealth, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds" — establishes that taqwa is an **inward reality**, not an outward display. The person who dresses piously but has a corrupt heart has no taqwa. The person whose heart is pure and whose deeds are good has taqwa — regardless of outward appearance.

### 7. Taqwa as the Criterion of Nobility

Drawing on Surah al-Hujurat (49:13) — "Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the one with the most taqwa" — Qaradawi argues that taqwa is the sole basis of human distinction before Allah.

This principle undergirds the entire Islamic ethic of equality and anti-discrimination:
- Race counts for nothing
- Lineage counts for nothing
- Wealth counts for nothing
- Power counts for nothing
- **Only taqwa determines worth before Allah**

This means: the poorest person with the most taqwa is more noble than the richest person with less taqwa. The unknown person of taqwa is more noble than the famous person without it. In the family context: the family member who is most conscious of Allah — not the one who is most successful, most beautiful, or most popular — is the most noble.

### 8. Taqwa and Social Ethics

In *Ethics in Islam* (Chapter 4), Qaradawi connects taqwa to collective moral obligations: "Cooperate in righteousness and piety (taqwa), but do not cooperate in sin and aggression" (5:2).

Taqwa is not merely a personal virtue — it has social dimensions. The person of taqwa:
- Not only avoids sin individually
- Works to promote good and prevent evil in society
- Cooperates with others in righteousness
- Refuses to cooperate in sin

This connects taqwa directly to the Islamic obligation of *al-amr bi'l-ma'rūf wa'l-nahy 'an al-munkar* (enjoining good and forbidding evil) — a communal responsibility that flows from personal taqwa.

### 9. Taqwa Is Here — In the Heart

The Prophet pointed to his chest three times and said: "Taqwa is here." Qaradawi emphasizes this: taqwa is not in the outward appearance, not in the length of the beard or the style of the hijab, not in public displays of piety. Taqwa is in the heart — and the heart's condition is known only to Allah.

But the heart's taqwa produces visible fruits: truthfulness, kindness, generosity, patience, justice. If these fruits are absent, the taqwa is absent — regardless of outward appearance.

## Family Discussion (15 min)

### Question 1 — What Does "Being Aware of Allah" Mean?

> What does it mean to "be aware of Allah" when no one is watching? Can you give an example from your own life?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is the core question. Taqwa is what you do when you are alone — when no parent, no teacher, no friend can see you. The child who doesn't cheat on a test when the teacher leaves the room has taqwa. The adult who doesn't cut corners at work when the boss isn't looking has taqwa. Ask for concrete examples.

**Qaradawi's framework:** Taqwa = inner awareness + outer discipline. Without taqwa, moral rules are external regulations. With taqwa, they become inner reality.

### Question 2 — How Does Fasting Build Taqwa?

> The Qur'an says fasting is prescribed "that you may attain taqwa." How does not eating and drinking make you more conscious of Allah?

**Facilitator guidance:** Fasting trains the body to obey the soul. When you feel hunger and choose not to eat — not because you can't, but because Allah said not to — you are practicing taqwa in its purest form. Every moment of fasting is a conscious choice: "Allah told me not to eat, so I won't — even though I'm hungry and no one would know if I sneaked a bite."

**For children:** "When you fast and you're really hungry, and you don't eat even though the kitchen is right there — that's taqwa! You're saying 'Allah is watching me even though Mom and Dad can't see me.'"

### Question 3 — Taqwa as the Only Thing That Makes Someone Noble

> If taqwa is the only thing that makes someone noble in Allah's sight, how should we treat people who seem "less important" — the cleaner, the guard, the waiter? How should we treat each other in this family?

**Facilitator guidance:** This is where theology becomes ethics. If 49:13 is true — "the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the one with the most taqwa" — then our social hierarchies are inverted. The CEO with little taqwa is less noble than the street sweeper with much taqwa. In the family: the child who prays sincerely is more noble than the parent who prays for show.

### Question 4 — Good Intention vs. Allah's Boundaries

> Qaradawi says: "Good intentions do not excuse transgression on Allah's Sovereignty." Can you think of a situation where someone might say "I have a good reason to do something haram"? Why doesn't that work?

**Facator guidance:** This is a crucial distinction for teenagers especially. "I'm lying to protect someone's feelings" — good intention, but lying is still haram. "I'm listening to music with bad lyrics because it helps me study" — good intention, but the content is still haram. Taqwa means: find a halal way to achieve your good intention. Don't use a good intention to justify crossing Allah's boundaries.

### Question 5 — Taqwa in Entertainment

> How does taqwa apply to how we use phones, watch videos, and spend our free time? Is entertainment haram? What does taqwa look like in entertainment?

**Facilitator guidance:** Qaradawi's position: entertainment is NOT haram by default. The default is permissibility. BUT the person of taqwa exercises self-regulation:
- Does this entertainment encroach on my obligations? (If prayer time comes, stop.)
- Does it involve prohibited content? (If it promotes haram, avoid it.)
- Is it proportionate? (If it takes over my life, reduce it.)

This is not about banning entertainment — it's about governing it with taqwa.

## Family Activity (10 min)

### Activity: "Allah Is Watching Me" Practice

Each family member chooses **one daily activity** (eating, getting dressed, checking the phone, leaving the house, speaking to a sibling) and for one week adds a conscious awareness practice:

**Before starting the activity, say silently:**
> "Allah is watching me right now. I am doing this for Allah, not for anyone else."

**After completing the activity, say silently:**
> "Thank You, Allah, for helping me do this with awareness of You."

**At the next halaqah**, each family member shares:
- What activity did they choose?
- Did the awareness change how they did it?
- Did they notice a difference between "doing it for Allah" and "doing it for habit"?

**Goal:** To make taqwa a lived, daily experience — not an abstract concept. The Prophet said "Allah does not look at your appearances, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds." This practice trains the heart.

**Variation for young children (under 7):** Instead of a silent practice, use a visible reminder. Create a small card with "اللَّهُ يَرَانِي" (Allah sees me) written on it. Place it in the child's room or on their desk. When they see it, they say: "Allah is watching me!" This makes the abstract concept concrete.

## Notes for the Facilitator

### Before the Session

- Read Ethics in Islam sections: the concept page cites ch-01, ch-03, ch-04, section-04-02

### Key Distinction to Emphasize
The most important distinction in this module: **taqwa is NOT about outward appearance — it is about the heart**. The Prophet said "Allah does not look at your appearances, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds." This means:
- A person can dress modestly but have no taqwa (if the heart is corrupt)
- A person can have simple clothes but have great taqwa (if the heart is pure)
- Taqwa is known by its fruits: truthfulness, kindness, patience, justice, contentment

For families with teenagers especially, this distinction is crucial — taqwa is not about looking religious; it is about being conscious of Allah in everything.

### The Intention vs. Prohibition Distinction
This is the most intellectually important point for older children and adults. Qaradawi's principle: "Good intentions do not excuse transgression on Allah's Sovereignty."

This means:
- Intention can elevate a permissible act to a meritorious one (eating with gratitude = worship)
- Intention CANNOT transform a prohibited act into a permissible one (stealing to give charity is still stealing)
- The boundary is set by Allah, not by our good intentions

### Adapting for Age

| Age Group | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Under 7 | Focus on "Allah is watching me" — use the card activity. Skip the intention/prohibition discussion. Emphasize fasting as the way we practice taqwa. |
| 7–12 | Ask: "If the teacher leaves the room during a test, and you could cheat without anyone knowing — would you? Why or why not? That's taqwa." |
| 13–17 | Engage with the entertainment discussion: "How does taqwa apply to what you watch on YouTube, TikTok, or Netflix?" NOT to ban entertainment, but to govern it: does it encroach on obligations? Does it involve haram content? Is it proportionate? |
| Adults | Read the full concept page and the Diversion and Arts chapter. Discuss the principle of "good intention cannot override prohibition" in the context of modern ethical dilemmas. |

### If Time Is Short
If 60 minutes is not available, reduce the teaching to sections 1 (what is taqwa), 3 (purpose of worship), and 7 (criterion of nobility). Keep the full discussion — Questions 1 and 5 are essential for practical application.
