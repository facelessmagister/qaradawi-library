# Divorce (طَلَاق)

**Arabic:** طَلَاق (*ṭalāq*), from the root ط-ل-ق meaning "to release" or "to set free"
**Domain:** Fiqh al-Aḥwāl al-Shakhṣiyyah (Personal Status Law)
**Related Arabic Terms:** عِدَّة (*ʿiddah* — waiting period), خُلْع (*khulʿ* — wife-initiated dissolution), فَسْخ (*faskh* — annulment), رَجْعَة (*rajʿah* — revocation/return)

## Definition

Divorce (*ṭalāq*) in Islamic law is the dissolution of the marriage bond by the husband's pronouncement, mutual agreement, or judicial decree. It is a legally recognized but religiously discouraged mechanism — the Prophet صلى الله عليه وسلم reportedly said: "In the eyes of Allah, divorce is the least favored permissible thing."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

Qaradawi frames divorce not as a right to be exercised casually but as a last resort when all attempts at reconciliation (*ṣulḥ*) have failed, given that the norm in marriage (*nikah*) is "love and kindness" (*muwaddah wa raḥmah*).^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

## Qaradawi's Treatment

### Divorce as a Last Resort

Qaradawi emphasizes that Islamic ethics does not allow forcing anyone to stay married to someone they do not like, because the norm in marriage is love and kindness. However, he stresses patience before resorting to divorce:

> "If problems emerge after the marriage, one needs to be patient. If nothing changes, Allah has allowed Muslims to seek divorce."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

He cites two Quranic verses in tandem:

- **Qur'an 2:229** — "Divorce is twice. Then, either keep [her] on acceptable terms or release [her] kindly."
- **Qur'an 4:19** — "Live with them in kindness. For if you dislike them — perhaps you dislike something that Allah makes therein much good."

This dual citation illustrates Qaradawi's methodology: presenting both the permissibility of divorce and the Quranic encouragement to persevere in marriage.

### The Graduated System of Divorce

Qaradawi outlines Islam's three-stage divorce framework as directly derived from Qur'an 2:229-230:

1. **First divorce (*ṭalāq awwal*)** — Retractable. The husband can take back his wife during the waiting period (*ʿiddah*).
2. **Second divorce (*ṭalāq thānī*)** — Also retractable. The couple can reconcile and resume married life.
3. **Third divorce (*ṭalāq thālith*)** — Final and irrevocable. The wife is no longer lawful to her former husband unless she marries another man who then divorces her or dies.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

Qaradawi highlights that this graduated structure creates natural pauses and opportunities for reconsideration, preventing hasty or impulsive dissolution of marriage.

### Reconciliation Before Divorce

Before divorce can be contemplated, Qaradawi identifies a graduated sequence of remedial measures drawn from Qur'an 4:34-35:

1. **Kind words and advice** (*al-mawʿiẓah*) — Verbal counsel and reminders.
2. **Warnings** — Firm but measured communication.
3. **Turning away in bed** (*al-hajr fī al-maḍājʿ*) — A symbolic withdrawal.
4. **Family arbitration** (*ḥakam*) — Qur'an 4:35 prescribes sending an arbitrator from each family: "If you fear dissension between the two, send an arbitrator from his family and an arbitrator from hers."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

Qaradawi cites the example of Caliph ʿUmar, who sent two arbitrators to reconcile a married couple. When they initially failed, ʿUmar told them to clear their intentions first through prayer, after which they succeeded.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

### The Waiting Period (*ʿIddah*)

Divorce is followed by a mandatory waiting period during which the wife remains in the marital home, providing a window for reconciliation. Qaradawi details the *ʿiddah* provisions from Qur'an 65:1-2 and 2:228:

- **Standard duration:** Three menstrual periods (Qur'an 2:228).
- **Post-menopausal women or irregular menstruation:** Three months (Qur'an 65:4).
- **Pregnant women:** Until they give birth (Qur'an 65:4).
- **Widows:** Four months and ten days (Qur'an 2:234).

During *ʿiddah*, the husband is financially responsible for the wife: "And their husbands have the right to take them back in this [period] if they want reconciliation" (Qur'an 2:228).^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

The wife must not leave the marital home during *ʿiddah* unless she has committed a clear dishonorable act (Qur'an 65:1). If the period expires without reconciliation, the divorce is final and remarriage requires a new contract and new dowry (*mahr*).

### Financial Rights After Divorce

Qaradawi is emphatic about the post-divorce financial obligations:

- **Provision for the divorced woman** (*mutʿah*): "For divorced women is a provision according to what is acceptable — a duty upon the righteous" (Qur'an 2:241).^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]
- **Prohibition of harm**: "Do not harm them [your ex-wives] in order to oppress them" (Qur'an 65:6).
- **Breastfeeding and child support**: A divorced mother who nurses her child has the right to financial support from the former husband for two years (Qur'an 2:233; 65:6).

### Divorce and Social Custom (*ʿUrf*)

In his discussion of the role of custom (*ʿurf*) in Islamic law, Qaradawi uses divorce-related provisions as a prime example. Social norms help define abstract Quranic terms like "kindness" (*maʿrūf*) in Qur'an 4:19 and "acceptable terms" (*bil-maʿrūf*) in Qur'an 2:229-232 regarding retention or release of divorced women. Customs thus shape the concrete meaning of alimony, living standards, and the definition of what constitutes "acceptable" provision.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-02-06.txt]

### Prohibition of Preventing Remarriage

Qur'an 2:232 explicitly forbids preventing divorced women from remarrying their former husbands by mutual consent. Qaradawi includes this verse among the ethical acts of self-purification (*tazkiyah*) listed by the Qur'an, showing that the ethics of divorce are not merely legal but deeply spiritual:

> "When you divorce women and they have completed their term, do not prevent them from remarrying their [former] husbands if they agree among themselves on an acceptable basis." (Qur'an 2:232)^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-01-03.txt]

### Mutʿah and Deferred Dowry in Zakah Calculations

In *Fiqh al-Zakah*, Qaradawi addresses divorce in the context of zakatable wealth. A wife's deferred *mahr* (dowry payable upon divorce or death of the husband) is treated as a debt. Some scholars argue the deferred *mahr* is not deductible from zakatable assets, while others treat it like any other debt. Qaradawi notes that all money owed to an ex-wife or for children in the mother's custody are deductible debts.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

### Hadith in Approaching the Sunnah

In *Approaching the Sunnah*, Qaradawi discusses the hadith of Fatimah bint Qays regarding the *ʿiddah* residence for an irrevocably divorced woman. The Prophet directed her to stay with Ibn Umm Maktum (a blind man) rather than Umm Sharik's house (which was visited by many Companions), establishing that the divorced woman's *ʿiddah* accommodation must consider her privacy and dignity.^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-03.txt]

He also discusses Qur'an 33:49, which states that there is no *ʿiddah* (waiting period) when divorce occurs before consummation (*al-mass*), and notes that all Quran commentators and jurists — including the Zahiris — interpreted "touching" (*al-mass*) as "penetration" (*al-dakhul*), linking it to circumstances of strict seclusion.^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-03.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

### Points of Consensus

Qaradawi's positions align with the four Sunni madhahib on the following:

- **Three-fold divorce** — All schools agree that the first two pronouncements are revocable and the third is irrevocable (*ṭalāq bā'in*).
- ***ʿIddah* durations** — The varying *ʿiddah* periods for menstruating women, post-menopausal women, pregnant women, and widows are agreed upon across madhahib, derived directly from Quranic texts.
- **Financial obligations** — All schools require *mutʿah* (provision) and maintenance during *ʿiddah*, differing only in amount and conditions.
- **Divorce as last resort** — The hadith "least favored permissible thing" is accepted across all four schools.

### Notable Positions

- Qaradawi cites the traditional saying "Nothing is more painful than the company of someone you cannot like and you cannot leave" as justification for permissibility of divorce when reconciliation is impossible, reflecting a principle shared across madhahib.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]
- He emphasizes that Islam does not allow forcing anyone to stay in a loveless marriage, a position consistent with the Hanafi school's recognition of *khulʿ* (wife-initiated dissolution).
- On deferred *mahr* as zakat-deductible debt, Qaradawi takes a position that differs from some scholars, considering it deductible alongside all obligations owed to an ex-wife.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

## Contemporary Relevance

Qaradawi's treatment of divorce carries significant contemporary implications:

1. **Three divorces at once** — A widespread practice in some Muslim societies where a husband pronounces three divorces simultaneously. Qaradawi's presentation follows the Quranic graduated model, implicitly rejecting this practice as contrary to the spirit of *ṭalāq*.
2. **Women's rights** — His emphasis that Islam prohibits harming divorced women, mandates provision, and forbids preventing remarriage addresses common cultural abuses that violate the Quranic standard.
3. ***Khulʿ* and wife-initiated dissolution** — While Qaradawi does not extensively treat *khulʿ* in the extracted sources, his insistence that nobody should be forced to remain in a loveless marriage creates space for this discussion.
4. **Custom and context** — His argument that social norms define "acceptable terms" (*bil-maʿrūf*) in divorce settlements provides a framework for adapting Islamic divorce law across different cultural and economic contexts.
