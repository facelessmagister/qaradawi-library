# Haram (حَرَام)

**Arabic:** حَرَام  
**Transliteration:** *haram*  
**Domain:** Usul al-Fiqh / Fiqh al-Mu'amalat / Fiqh al-Ibadat

## Definition

*Haram* (حَرَام) denotes that which is unlawful, prohibited, and forbidden in Islamic law. An act classified as haram incurs sin if committed deliberately and may carry a prescribed worldly punishment (*hudud*) or a divine reckoning in the Hereafter. It is the direct antonym of [[concept-halal|Halal (حَلَال)]] and, together with it, constitutes the core moral binary of the Shariah.

Crucially, Qaradawi holds that **haram is the exception, not the rule**. The default state of all created things is permissibility ([[concept-halal|Halal]]); prohibition requires an explicit, unambiguous proof from the Qur'an, the authentic Sunnah, or the consensus (*ijma'*) of Muslim scholars. This is the principle of *al-ibahah al-asliyyah* (original permissibility). ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

## Qaradawi's Position

Qaradawi's treatment of haram is defined by a rigorous boundary-keeping impulse: only what Allah and His Messenger have clearly prohibited is haram, and expanding the haram beyond its textual boundaries is itself a grave religious error.

1. **Haram requires decisive proof.** The three — and only three — legitimate sources of prohibition are: (a) a clear Qur'anic verse, (b) an authentic and unambiguous hadith, and (c) the consensus of Muslim scholars. A weak hadith, a hadith free from ambiguities yet inauthentic, or a hadith with a continuous chain but containing obscurities, does not suffice to establish haram. ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

2. **Allah deliberately limited prohibitions.** The Prophet (P.B.U.H.) said: *"Allah enjoined certain duties (fara'id) which you should follow, and set boundary limits between lawful and unlawful things (hudud) which you should not violate. There are things which He deliberately did not mention by Mercy from Him, for He is never forgetful. Therefore, do not try to investigate into them any further."* (Reported by Darqutni on the authority of Abi Tha'labah al-Khashni; graded *hasan* by al-Nawawi and Abu Bakr al-Sama'ani.) The word *hudud* (boundaries) is precise: the haram is fenced and finite. ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

3. **What Allah omitted is forgiven, not forbidden.** The hadith of Abu al-Darda' (reported by Al-Hakim, authenticated by al-Bazzar) states: *"What Allah made permissible in His Book is lawful, and what He forbade is unlawful, and what He, deliberately, did not mention is forgiven."* The vast middle ground — things neither explicitly permitted nor explicitly prohibited — is a zone of divine pardon, not of suspicion. ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

4. **Declaring the lawful to be unlawful is itself a sin.** Qaradawi cites the Qur'anic censure of those who prohibit what Allah has allowed: *"O you who believe, do not prohibit the good things which Allah has made lawful for you"* (Sura 5:87). Over-prohibition encroaches on Allah's exclusive legislative authority (*tashri'*).

5. **Haram is specific, not general.** Qaradawi applies this to the case of singing and music: scholars agreed that obscene, erotic, and evil singing is haram (because the content — the words themselves — is haram), but they disagreed on singing in general. The correct methodological position is that only the specific elements that violate Shariah are prohibited, not the entire category. ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

6. **Necessity narrows haram.** Even what is haram becomes temporarily lawful under constraint (*darurah*), as Allah states: *"When He has explained to you that which He has forbidden you, except if you are constrained to it"* (Sura 6:119). This demonstrates that the scope of haram is not absolute but qualified by circumstance.

### Prophetic Hadiths

1. **Hadith of Abu al-Darda'** (Al-Hakim, al-Bazzar authenticated): *"What Allah made permissible in His Book is lawful, and what He forbade is unlawful, and what He, deliberately, did not mention is forgiven."*
2. **Hadith of Abi Tha'labah al-Khashni** (Darqutni, graded *hasan*): *"Allah enjoined certain duties (fara'id) which you should follow, and set boundary limits between lawful and unlawful things (hudud) which you should not violate. There are things which He deliberately did not mention by Mercy from Him, for He is never forgetful. Therefore, do not try to investigate into them any further."*

### Scholarly Consensus Points

- **Agreed haram in entertainment:** Obscene, erotic, and evil singing/content is unanimously haram (the words themselves are haram, and rhythm/melody amplify their effect). ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]
- **Agreed halal in entertainment:** Songs that do not play on sexual arousal and are not accompanied by prohibited instruments, sung by a woman among non-stranger men, are lawful by consensus. ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

| School | Position on the Scope of Haram | Relationship to Qaradawi |
|--------|-------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Hanafi** | Restrictive scope of haram; strong emphasis on *al-ibahah al-asliyyah*. The default in all worldly matters is permissibility; prohibition requires decisive proof. | Very close to Qaradawi's position. |
| **Maliki** | Restrictive scope of haram, but uses *sad al-dhari'ah* (blocking the means to haram) to prohibit some matters that could lead to haram. | Qaradawi accepts *dhari'ah* but applies it more narrowly. |
| **Shafi'i** | Restrictive in *mu'amalat* but stricter in *ibadat* (only what is textually permitted is valid in worship). The scope of haram is broader in worship. | Qaradawi follows the Shafi'i distinction between ibadat and mu'amalat but is more permissive in entertainment. |
| **Hanbali** | Tends toward a broader scope of haram, especially in music, arts, and entertainment. Ibn Taymiyyah and many Hanbali scholars held music to be categorically haram. | Qaradawi directly critiques this tendency; he argues it oversteps the boundaries of what the texts warrant. |

### Notable Individual Positions

- **Ibn Mas'ud and Ibn 'Abbas** — Interpreted *lahw al-hadith* ("vain talk" in Sura 31:6) as singing; declared singing haram. Qaradawi follows Ibn Hazm's refutation that this interpretation is contextually flawed, as the verse censures mockery of Allah's way, not entertainment per se. ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]
- **Ibn Hazm** — Rejected the prohibition of singing entirely on three grounds: (1) no authentic hadith from the Prophet forbidding singing, (2) the companions' disagreements negate any claim of consensus, and (3) the verse cited by prohibitionists (31:6) condemns disbelievers who mock the faith, not those who entertain themselves. Qaradawi aligns closely with Ibn Hazm on methodology. ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]
- **Al-Ghazali** — Held that listening to music and dancing is not haram per se, comparing it to profitless but not sinful activity. He cited: *"God will simply charge you for any unintentional oath you make"* (Sura 2:225) — if Allah does not hold one to account for an unintentional oath, then by analogy He does not hold one to account for harmless entertainment. Qaradawi builds on this but adds his intention-based framework. ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

## Contemporary Relevance

- **Cultural over-prohibition:** Many Muslim communities and preachers habitually declare activities haram — from music to sports to photography — without textual basis. Qaradawi's framework provides a principled rebuttal: expand not the haram beyond what Allah has delimited.
- **Intention (*niyyah*) as a differentiator:** Qaradawi argues that the same act (e.g., singing) can be good or evil depending on intention, content, and context. Good intention can turn diversion into an approach to Allah; evil intention corrupts even ostensibly good deeds. This nuanced approach resists both blanket prohibition and blanket permissiveness.
- **Islamic finance:** The concept of haram underpins all prohibitions in Islamic banking — [[concept-riba|Riba (رِبَا)]], *gharar* (excessive uncertainty), *maysir* (gambling). The principle that haram must be textually established prevents arbitrary restriction of financial innovation.
- **Digital and media ethics:** The same framework applies to social media, gaming, and streaming: these are halal by default; specific haram elements (pornographic content, fraud, gambling) must be identified and avoided, rather than condemning the entire medium.
- **Muslim minorities:** In non-Muslim societies, the principled restraint on declaring things haram is especially important — unfamiliar practices default to permissibility unless their haram element is identified.

## Classical Scholarly Context

The *usuli* principle *la tahrim illa bi-dalil* ("there is no prohibition except by a proof") was shared by the majority of jurists, with the Hanafis articulating it most forcefully. Qaradawi's distinctive contribution is not the principle itself but its vigorous application to entertainment and arts — a domain where many classical and contemporary scholars have expanded haram beyond textual warrant. His methodology — decisive proof required, weak traditions rejected, context scrutinised, and intention weighed — provides a model for navigating the gray zones of modern life without collapsing into either permissiveness or over-prohibition.
