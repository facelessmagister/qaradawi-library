# Art / Fann (فَنّ) — Visual and Aesthetic Expression in Islam

**Arabic:** فَنّ (plural: فُنُون)  
**Transliteration:** Fann / Funun  
**Domain:** Islamic Ethics (الأخلاق الإسلامية) — Diversion and Arts (الترفيه والفنون في الإسلام)

## Definition

*Fann* (فَنّ) — art, skill, craft — in the Islamic context refers to the creative and aesthetic dimensions of human expression, including visual art, poetry, music, singing, calligraphy, literature, and decorative design. Qaradawi's *Diversion and Arts in Islam* (*Al-Tarfiyah wa al-Fununn fi al-Islam*) constitutes the most comprehensive treatment of this concept in his corpus, establishing a middle course between the extremes of total prohibition and unlimited permissibility.

Qaradawi defines art as that which "refers to the creative and imaginative form which can elevate the mind and emotions of man to new heights." Islam does not reject art but channels it, insisting that content is more important than form and that aesthetic satisfaction must coexist with moral purpose.

## Qaradawi's Position

### 1. The Middle Course Between Extremes

Qaradawi opens *Diversion and Arts in Islam* by identifying two extremes that he rejects:

- **Extreme prohibition**: Those who believe Muslim society "should consecrate solely on the service and worship of Allah and hard work. There is no room for diversion or mirth. Singing is totally alien to such a society which forbids self-expression."
- **Extreme permissibility**: Those whose "life is a fair for fun and games" and who "seem to have forgotten that content is much more important than form."

Qaradawi chose "a middle course," guided by "sound and authentic texts" and "the torch of Shari'ah and Fiqh." This middle path recognizes that art fulfills legitimate human needs for beauty, emotional expression, and relaxation, while imposing boundaries to prevent moral corruption.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt]

### 2. Islam and the Sense of Reality

Islam's approach to life is realistic: it acknowledges human needs for bodily, spiritual, intellectual, and emotional fulfillment in moderation. "It is agreed that sports keep the body fit, worship purifies the soul and fulfils its needs, knowledge develops the intellect, and art is a source of emotional fulfilment."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt]

### 3. The Quran and Aesthetic Fulfillment

Qaradawi demonstrates that the Quran consistently pairs aesthetic appreciation with utilitarian benefit:

- **Cattle**: "God created cattle for you (you find) in them warmth, benefit and from them you eat" (16:5) — then: "And in them there is beauty for you when you bring them home and when you take them to pasture" (16:6).
- **Horses**: "God created horses, mules and donkeys for you to ride and for ornament" (16:8).
- **The sea**: "God is the One Who has subjected to you the sea, so that you eat from it fresh flesh, and you extract from it ornaments which you wear" (16:14).
- **Fruits and gardens**: "Look at their fruits when they bear it, and their ripening. Surely in that there are signs for people who believe" (6:141).

The Quran "orientates man towards the assimilation of aesthetic and utilitarian aspects in different fields of life."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt]

### 4. Allah Is Beautiful and He Loves Beauty

Qaradawi cites the hadith: "Allah is Beautiful and He loves beauty" — narrated via Ibn Mas'ud. The Prophet corrected those who confused love of beauty with arrogance: "Whoever has the weight of a grain of a mustard seed of arrogance in his heart, will not be admitted to Paradise." When a companion said, "All people like to wear attractive clothes and shoes," the Prophet replied: "Allah is Beautiful and He loves beauty, but arrogance is different. It means to deny the truth although one is certain that it is so, and to be thankless for the people who were helpful to one."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-02.txt]

### 5. The Quran as an Aesthetic Miracle

The Quran itself is Islam's greatest aesthetic miracle — "both an aesthetic and intellectual miracle." The Prophet said: "Adorn the Quran by reciting it with your nice voices." He also said: "The attractive audible sweet sounding voice adds to the beauty of the Quran." Qaradawi emphasizes that the Quran "encompasses religion, knowledge, literature and art. It fulfils man's spiritual, intellectual and emotional needs."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-02.txt]

### 6. Poetry and Literature

The Prophet listened to poetry, appreciated it, and used it as a tool for da'wah. He said: "Some poetry contains wisdom." Qaradawi affirms that poetry and literature are permissible art forms provided they carry a positive message. "Discipline and message are the bases of art, literature and poetry. Any work of art should convey a message." Unregulated, purely erotic, satiric, or vainglorious poetry is condemned.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-03.txt]

### 7. Singing and Music

Qaradawi addresses the most contested area of Islamic art law with characteristic nuance:

- **Agreement**: All scholars agree that obscene, erotic, and evil singing is *ḥarām*. Songs containing forbidden words and themes are prohibited.
- **Agreement**: Songs that are not sexually provocative, not accompanied by musical instruments, and not sung by a woman before non-maḥram men, are *ḥalāl*.
- **Disagreement**: The vast majority of scholars disagree on music with instruments, singing by women, and the general lawfulness of music as entertainment.

Qaradawi's position follows the principle that "everything is essentially allowed" unless explicitly forbidden: "The Muslim scholars are of the opinion that everything is essentially allowed."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

### 8. Visual Arts and Picture-Making

On the most controversial topic — images, statues, and visual art — Qaradawi provides a detailed analysis:

- **Idolatry of images**: The severe Prophetic warnings against picture-making are directed against images made for glorification, worship, or imitation of Allah's creation — the path that leads from commemorative images to idolatry.
- **Pictures as luxury**: Images used merely for decoration and luxury are *makrūh* (disliked), not *ḥarām*.
- **Utilitarian images**: Pictures on carpets, cushions, and items of common use are permitted.
- **Children's toys**: Dolls, puppets, and similar toys are explicitly permitted.
- **Relief vs. flat images**: Relief images (statues that cast shadows) are more restricted than flat images.

His conclusion follows Imam al-KhatTabi: "Forbiddenness is restricted to reliefs. For plain-surface pictures made on papers, walls, wood and so forth, he was of the opinion that they are disliked but not forbidden."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-05.txt]

### 9. Humor and Entertainment

Qaradawi affirms that "laughter distinguishes human beings from other species" and that "Islam is the religion of instinct; it cannot possibly repress the human urge to laugh and have a good time." The Prophet Muhammad himself "used to jest without resorting to lies" and "shared their laughters, joys and jests."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-07.txt]

### 10. Art and the Islamic Framework

Consistent with his position on punishment and ethics, Qaradawi insists that art must exist within the broader Islamic framework. Art that promotes virtue, beauty, or legitimate diversion is *ḥalāl*; art that promotes vice, idolatry, or moral corruption is *ḥarām*.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

### Imam al-Nawawi (d. AH 676)

Al-Nawawi adopted a strict position against picture-making, forbidding even flat-surface images. Qaradawi respectfully disagrees, following al-KhatTabi's more nuanced ruling that plain-surface images are *makrūh* (disliked) rather than *ḥarām* (forbidden), and that relief images (statues) are the primary target of the severe Prophetic warnings.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-05.txt]

### Ibn Abbas (d. AH 68)

Ibn Abbas provided the most pragmatic ruling: "If there is no other way left to make your living but picture-making, then at least make pictures of trees and inanimate objects." Qaradawi cites this as evidence that the prohibition is contextual, not absolute.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-05.txt]

### Al-Ghazali (d. AH 505)

Al-Ghazali's *Ihya'* discusses the permissibility of singing and music, and Qaradawi builds on this tradition. Ghazali argued that singing without instruments and without associated vices is permissible, and that musical instruments are permissible in certain contexts. Qaradawi extends this by anchoring the permissibility of art in the broader Quranic principle that Allah created beauty for human enjoyment.

## Key Principles Governing Art in Islam

1. **The default is permissibility**: Everything is *ḥalāl* unless explicitly forbidden.
2. **Content over form**: The moral content of art matters more than its medium or genre.
3. **The middle course**: Neither total prohibition nor unlimited permissibility.
4. **Art serves human needs**: Sports for the body, worship for the soul, knowledge for the intellect, art for the emotions.
5. **Art within the Islamic framework**: Art that promotes virtue is *ḥalāl*; art that promotes vice is *ḥarām*.
6. **Beauty is a divine attribute**: "Allah is Beautiful and He loves beauty" — appreciation of beauty is part of faith.
7. **Prevention of idolatry**: The primary concern with imagery is the prevention of *shirk*, not the suppression of aesthetics.

## Contemporary Relevance

1. **Muslim engagement with modern art**: Qaradawi's framework provides clear principles for Muslims navigating contemporary visual arts, cinema, photography, and digital media.
2. **Music in Muslim societies**: The nuanced approach to singing and music offers a middle ground between blanket prohibition and uncritical acceptance.
3. **Freedom of expression vs. moral boundaries**: Qaradawi's insistence on content over form provides a principled framework for balancing artistic freedom with moral responsibility.
4. **Islamic aesthetics**: The Quranic foundations for beauty and art provide a uniquely Islamic aesthetic philosophy that affirms beauty while preventing idolatry.
5. **Social media and imagery**: The principles governing picture-making apply directly to photography, video, and digital art in the age of smartphones and social media.
6. **Humor in public discourse**: The Prophetic example of dignified humor provides a model for constructive comedy and entertainment.
