# Singing / Ghina' (غِنَاء)

**Arabic:** غِنَاء (*ghinā'*) — vocal singing
**Domain:** Fiqh al-Ibadat, Islamic Ethics, Arts & Entertainment

## Definition

*Ghinā'* (غِنَاء) is the Arabic term for vocal singing — the musical production of melody through the human voice, whether with or without instrumental accompaniment. In Islamic jurisprudence, singing has been one of the most debated topics, with scholars ranging from blanket prohibition to full permissibility. Qaradawi's position, developed at length in *The Lawful and the Prohibited in Islam* and *Diversion and Arts in Islam* (Chapter 4), is that **vocal singing without instruments is lawful (*halāl*) by default**, subject to conditions regarding content, context, and the singer's identity, and that instrumental music merits a separate and more nuanced ruling (see [[concept-music]]).

The term *ghinā'* does not appear in the Qur'an itself; the relevant Qur'anic debates center on the terms *lahw al-hadīth* (vain talk, Q 31:6) and *laghw* (nonsense/trivial speech, Q 28:55), which some scholars interpreted as references to singing — an interpretation that Qaradawi systematically refutes.

## Qaradawi's Position

### 1. The Foundational Principle: Everything Is Permissible Unless Prohibited

Qaradawi begins with the established legal maxim: **everything is permissible unless explicitly prohibited by Qur'an, Sunnah, or consensus (*ijmā'*)**. Since no authentic, unambiguous text prohibits singing in general, the default ruling is permissibility (*ibāhah*). He cites the Qur'anic verse: **"He has explained to you that which He has forbidden you"** (Q 6:119) and the Prophetic hadith: **"What Allah made permissible in His Book is lawful, and what He forbade is unlawful, and what He deliberately did not mention is forgiven."**^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

### 2. Refutation of the Prohibition Arguments

Qaradawi systematically examines and refutes the proofs cited by opponents of singing:

- **Sura 31:6** ("vain talk" / *lahw al-hadīth*): Following Ibn Hazm, Qaradawi argues that this verse refers to disbelief and mockery of Allah's way, not to singing. The one who "buys vain talk to lead astray from Allah's Way" is, by consensus, a disbeliever — not a singer.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

- **Sura 28:55** ("speaking nonsense" / *laghw*): Qaradawi explains that *laghw* means insolent remarks and bad language, not singing per se. Even if singing were included under *laghw*, the verse indicates only that abstaining from it is preferred (*mandūb*), not that it is prohibited (*harām*).^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

- **Hadith "Every diversion is falsehood except three"**: This hadith is classified as *mudtarib* (shaky) due to disagreements among reporters. Even if sound, "falsehood" (*bātil*) here means "useless," not "prohibited."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

- **Hadith on *al-ma'āzif* (musical instruments)**: The hadith in *Sahih al-Bukhari* is *mu'allaq* (hanging — incomplete chain), which Ibn Hazm rejected. Even if authenticated, the meaning of *al-ma'āzif* is contested (instruments vs. festive gatherings), and the primary condemnation targets the combination of intoxicants, adultery, and luxury — not singing alone.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

- **Hadith forbidding buying/selling slave-girl singers**: This hadith is classified as *da'īf* (weak) due to discontinuity and unreliable narrators. Al-Ghazali noted that the context concerns slave-girls singing in taverns for male audiences — an environment of licentiousness, not singing per se.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

- **Hadith "Singing implants hypocrisy in the heart"**: No authentic chain traces this to the Prophet; it is merely the opinion of a companion or follower, subject to error.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

- **Ibn 'Umar putting his fingers in his ears on hearing a shepherd's pipe**: Even if authentic, the Prophet did not forbid the music in clear terms — he merely avoided it, as he avoided many permissible things. Ibn 'Umar likewise allowed his companion Nāfi' to continue listening, which proves the permissibility of listening.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

Qaradawi concludes: **"Judge Abū Bakr Ibn al-'Arabī said in *Al-Ahkām* that there is no authentic hadith which declares the forbiddenness of singing and music. Al-Ghazali and Ibn al-Nahhās in *Al-'Umdah* supported his opinion."**^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

### 3. Evidence for the Lawfulness of Singing

Qaradawi marshals extensive evidence for permissibility:

- **The hadith of the two slave-girls singing at 'Ā'isha's house**: The Prophet (PBUH) heard them and did not forbid them, telling Abū Bakr: **"Leave them, so that the Jews may know that our religion has latitude."**^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

- **The hadith of the Ansar brides**: The Prophet (PBUH) told 'Ā'isha: **"Haven't you got any amusement for the marriage ceremony? The Ansar like amusement."** He even suggested specific songs: "We are here especially for you; greet us so that we can greet you."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

- **Companions and followers who approved of singing**: Qaradawi documents that 'Abdullāh ibn Ja'far, Ibn 'Umar, 'Ā'isha, Abū 'Ubaydah ibn al-Jarrāh, Khārijah ibn Zayd, Shurayh the Judge, and many others permitted singing. The people of Madīnah reached a broad consensus (*ijmā'*) on its lawfulness.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

- **The fitrah (natural disposition) argument**: Singing appeals to human instinct — even babies calm to nursery rhymes, camels quicken their pace to caravanners' chants, and the Prophet himself watched Ethiopian warriors perform in his mosque.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

### 4. Conditions and Restrictions

Qaradawi imposes specific conditions that must be met for singing to remain lawful:

1. **Content must conform to the moral code of Islam.** Obscene, seductive, or blasphemous lyrics are prohibited. Songs praising alcohol, promoting skepticism, or flattering tyrants are *harām*.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

2. **Performance must not be sexually seductive.** A woman's singing before unrelated men (*ajānib*) is prohibited due to the risk of *fitnah* (seduction). The Qur'anic address to the Prophet's wives applies: "Speak honorable words" (Q 33:32).^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

3. **The environment must be free of prohibited elements.** Singing gatherings that involve alcohol, intermingling of the sexes, or other *harām* elements render the entire setting prohibited.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

4. **Moderation must be observed.** Even lawful singing becomes blameworthy if it consumes time owed to obligations or dominates one's life. "Excessiveness in diversion is forbidden even if moderate diversion is basically allowed."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

5. **Individual susceptibility must be considered.** If a particular type of music arouses or seduces a specific individual, that individual must shun it — even if it is generally permissible. This is the personal *fiqh* dimension of the ruling.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

### 5. Singing as Diversion and Its Spiritual Value

Qaradawi argues that moderate diversion — including singing — is psychologically necessary: **"The hearts get bored just as the bodies do, therefore you should always supply them with novel and useful pearls of wisdom"** (Imam 'Alī). He cites Abū al-Dardā': **"I devote some of my time to recreational activities which charge me with enough energy and impetus necessary for my battle for the sovereignty of truth."**^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

Intention (*niyyah*) determines the moral quality of listening: good intention transforms diversion into an approach to Allah, while evil intention corrupts even good deeds. This connects singing directly to the concept of [[concept-intention|Intention (نِيَّة)]].

### 6. Singing and the Islamic Heritage

Qaradawi documents the rich Islamic tradition of singing: the *adhān* (call to prayer), tilāwah (Qur'anic recitation), *munshidāt* (religious hymns), and folk songs celebrating harvests, weddings, and heroic deeds. He recalls the Ansar girls' song welcoming the Prophet to Madīnah and the work songs of laborers in the Battle of the Trench: **"O Allah, there is no life worth living except the life of the Hereafter, so please forgive the Ansar and the emigrants."**^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

## Evidence Base

### Qur'anic Verses

- **Q 31:6** — "And there are among people some who buy vain talk (*lahw al-hadīth*) to lead astray from Allah's Way" — interpreted by opponents as condemning singing, but Qaradawi follows Ibn Hazm in reading it as condemning disbelief and mockery, not singing per se.
- **Q 28:55** — "And when they hear people speaking nonsense (*laghw*), they avoid it" — *laghw* means trivial or offensive speech, not singing; even if it includes singing, only abstention is praised, not prohibition.
- **Q 2:29** — "Allah is the One Who created for you all that is on the earth" — foundational permissibility of all created things.
- **Q 5:4** — "Permitted to you is all that is good and pure" — the general permission of all wholesome things.
- **Q 10:59** and **Q 6:140** — condemnations of those who prohibit what Allah has permitted; forbidding what Allah allowed is an encroachment on divine sovereignty.
- **Q 33:32** — "Speak honorable words" — applied to female singing before unrelated men.

### Prophetic Hadiths

- **The two slave-girls singing at 'Ā'isha's house** — al-Bukhari and Muslim; the Prophet permitted and defended their singing.
- **"Haven't you got any amusement?"** — Ibn Mājah; the Prophet actively encouraged singing at weddings.
- **"O Hanzala, you should devote part of your time to the lawful enjoyment in this world"** — Muslim; establishes moderation as the norm.
- **"What Allah made permissible in His Book is lawful…"** — al-Hākim; foundational hadith on permissibility.
- **The Ethiopians playing in the Prophet's Mosque** — al-Bukhari and Muslim; the Prophet facilitated entertainment.

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

| Position | Scholars | Ruling |
|----------|----------|--------|
| **Blanket prohibition** | Some later Hanbalī scholars, Ibn Taymiyyah, Ibn al-Qayyim | All singing (with or without instruments) is *harām* |
| **Prohibited with instruments, permitted without** | Many Mālikī and Shāfi'ī scholars | Vocal singing is *mubāh* (permissible); instruments make it *harām* |
| **Permitted with or without instruments** | People of Madīnah, Zāhirīs, some Shāfi'ī scholars, Ibn Hazm | All singing and instrumental music is *mubāh* unless surrounded by *harām* elements |
| **Al-Ghazali's five-condition framework** | Al-Ghazali | Permissible under five conditions (see below) |
| **Qaradawi** | — | Vocal singing is *halāl* by default; instruments require separate ruling (see [[concept-music]]); permissibility subject to five conditions mirroring al-Ghazali |

Al-Ghazali, whom Qaradawi considers the most nuanced classical voice, established five conditions for singing's permissibility: (1) the listener must not be an adolescent susceptible to seduction; (2) the performer must not be a woman singing before unrelated men; (3) the lyrics must not be obscene or blasphemous; (4) the musical instruments must not be of the type associated with drinking gatherings; (5) the singing must not become an excessive preoccupation. Qaradawi adopts these conditions with modifications.

## Contemporary Relevance

1. **Digital music and streaming**: The conditions Qaradawi established for live performance (no seduction, no *harām* content, moderation) apply equally to recorded and streamed music. The individual-susceptibility test is particularly relevant in the age of on-demand music.

2. **Nasheed and Islamic music**: The rise of *nasheed* (Islamic vocal music) as an industry raises the question whether "religious" singing is automatically superior. Qaradawi's principle — that intention elevates diversion but content must also be lawful — applies.

3. **Gender and performance**: The prohibition on women singing before unrelated men is one of the most contested rulings in contemporary fiqh. Qaradawi maintains it based on Q 33:32 and the hadith evidence, while some modern scholars distinguish between live performance and recorded playback.

4. **Cultural decolonization and folk heritage**: Qaradawi's documentation of Islamic folk singing traditions (*mawawīl*, work songs, wedding songs) serves as a reminder that Islamic civilization has always produced its own musical heritage, countering claims that Islam is inherently hostile to song.
