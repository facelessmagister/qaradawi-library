# Taqwa / God-consciousness (تَقْوَى)

**Arabic:** تَقْوَى (*taqwa*) — plural: تَقْوَات (*taqwāt*)
**Related term:** بِرّ (*birr*) — piety, righteousness
**Domain:** Islamic Ethics, Tazkiyah, Aqeedah, Spirituality

## Definition

*Taqwa* (تَقْوَى) is the comprehensive Islamic virtue of God-consciousness — a state of inner awareness of Allah's presence, reverence for His commandments, and diligent avoidance of His prohibitions. It is commonly translated as "piety," "God-fearing," or "God-consciousness," but no single English term captures its full resonance. Taqwa encompasses both the *inner* disposition of the heart (fear of Allah, love of Allah, desire for His pleasure) and the *outer* discipline of actions (performing obligations, avoiding sins, fulfilling rights).

The term derives from the root و-ق-ي (*w-q-y*), meaning "to protect" or "to shield." The *muttaqi* (one who has taqwa) is thus one who shields himself from Allah's punishment by adhering to His commands. In Qur'anic usage, taqwa is the criterion by which Allah evaluates human worth: **"Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the one with the most taqwa"** (Q 49:13).

The related term *birr* (بِرّ) — often translated "piety" or "righteousness" — is treated by Qaradawi as effectively synonymous with taqwa in many contexts. The Qur'an itself defines *birr* in terms of taqwa: **"Piety is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but piety is [in] one who believes in Allah… and fears Allah"** (Q 2:177).

## Qaradawi's Position

### 1. Taqwa as the Foundation of Islamic Ethics

Qaradawi places taqwa at the foundation of Islamic moral life. In *Ethics in Islam*, he argues that the entire structure of Islamic ethics rests on the believer's consciousness of Allah's omniscience and omnipresence. Without taqwa, moral rules become mere external regulations; with taqwa, they become expressions of an inner reality. He cites the Prophetic hadith: **"Truth leads to piety (taqwa), and piety leads to Paradise. A man persists in telling the truth until he is recorded with Allah as truthful."** This chain — truth → taqwa → Paradise — demonstrates that taqwa is both the fruit of virtuous action and the gateway to salvation.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-03.txt]

### 2. Taqwa and Intention (Niyyah)

Taqwa is inseparable from intention (*niyyah*). In *Diversion and Arts in Islam*, Qaradawi states that **good intentions, piety, or sincerity do not excuse transgression on Allah's Sovereignty.** Only Allah has the right to determine the lawful and unlawful; human intention cannot override divine prohibition. Taqwa therefore means submitting to Allah's boundaries even when one's intention seems good, while simultaneously recognizing that a genuine act of taqwa must be animated by sincere intention — not mere outward compliance.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt] ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-02.txt]

### 3. Taqwa Balances Fear and Hope

Qaradawi, following the classical tradition, insists that taqwa must balance fear (*khawf*) and hope (*rajā'*). The believer who fears Allah without hoping in His mercy falls into despair; the one who hopes without fear falls into complacency. In *Ethics in Islam*, he presents this balance as one of the complementary principles of Islamic ethics — alongside mutual consultation (*shūrā*) and enjoining good and forbidding evil — that refine the five foundations of moral obligation, moral responsibility, punishment, intention, and effort.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

### 4. Taqwa as the Purpose of Worship

Qaradawi emphasizes that taqwa is the stated purpose of several acts of worship. The Qur'an declares regarding fasting: **"O you who believe, fasting is prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those before you, that you may attain taqwa"** (Q 2:183). Regarding pilgrimage: **"And take provisions, but indeed, the best provision is taqwa"** (Q 2:197). And regarding sacrifice: **"Their meat will not reach Allah, nor will their blood, but what reaches Him is taqwa from you"** (Q 22:37). These verses establish taqwa not as an incidental byproduct of worship but as its divinely intended outcome.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

### 5. Taqwa and Social Ethics

In *Ethics in Islam* (Chapter 4), Qaradawi connects taqwa to collective moral obligations: **"Cooperate in righteousness and piety (*birr* and *taqwa*), but do not cooperate in sin and aggression"** (Q 5:2). Taqwa is not merely a personal virtue; it has social dimensions. The person of taqwa not only avoids sin individually but also works to promote good and prevent evil in society. This connects taqwa directly to the Islamic obligation of *al-amr bi'l-ma'rūf wa'l-nahy 'an al-munkar* (enjoining good and forbidding evil).^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

### 6. Taqwa as the Criterion of Nobility

Drawing on Qur'an 49:13 — **"Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the one with the most taqwa"** — Qaradawi argues that taqwa is the sole basis of human distinction before Allah. Race, lineage, wealth, and power count for nothing in the divine estimation; taqwa alone determines worth. This principle undergirds the entire Islamic ethic of equality and anti-discrimination.^[raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-03.txt]

### 7. Taqwa in Diversion and Arts

In *Diversion and Arts in Islam*, Qaradawi addresses taqwa in the specific context of entertainment and leisure. He argues that moderate diversion is lawful and even psychologically necessary, but that the person of taqwa exercises self-regulation: diversion must not encroach upon religious obligations, must not involve prohibited content, and must be kept proportionate. The chapter opens by grounding the discussion in the Qur'anic affirmation of beauty and lawful enjoyment (Q 7:31-32), establishing that taqwa does not mean rejecting all pleasure but channeling it within divine boundaries.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt] ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-02.txt]

## Evidence Base

### Qur'anic Verses

- **Q 49:13** — "Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the one with the most taqwa" — the foundational verse establishing taqwa as the sole criterion of human distinction.
- **Q 2:177** — "Piety (*birr*) is not that you turn your faces toward the east or the west, but piety is [in] one who believes in Allah…" — defines birr in terms identical to taqwa.
- **Q 2:183** — "Fasting is prescribed for you… that you may attain taqwa" — taqwa is the stated purpose of fasting.
- **Q 2:197** — "And take provisions, but indeed, the best provision is taqwa" — taqwa as the spiritual provision for pilgrimage.
- **Q 22:37** — "Their meat will not reach Allah… but what reaches Him is taqwa from you" — taqwa as the essence of sacrifice.
- **Q 5:2** — "Cooperate in righteousness and taqwa, but do not cooperate in sin and aggression" — taqwa as a social obligation.
- **Q 7:31-32** — "O children of Adam, take your adornment at every masjid… Who has forbidden the adornment of Allah which He has produced for His servants?" — taqwa does not require rejecting lawful beauty.

### Prophetic Hadiths

- **"Truth leads to piety, and piety leads to Paradise"** — narrated in the two *Sahihs* from Abdullah ibn Mas'ud, establishing taqwa as the bridge between truthfulness and salvation.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-03.txt]
- **"O Allah! I beseech You for guidance, piety, chastity, and self-sufficiency"** — the Prophet's comprehensive supplication linking taqwa with guidance (*huda*), moral purity (*'afaf*), and contentment (*ghina*).^[raw/extracted/education-economy-sunnah/ch-01.txt]
- **"Allah does not look at your appearances and your wealth, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds"** — quoted in *Diversion and Arts* to demonstrate that taqwa is an inward reality, not an outward display.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

| Aspect | Qaradawi's View | Classical Consensus |
|--------|----------------|---------------------|
| Definition of taqwa | Comprehensive God-consciousness encompassing knowledge, fear, and action; the root of all virtue | Generally agreed; some emphasize fear, others obedience, others avoidance |
| Taqwa and birr | Effectively synonymous in Qur'anic usage; both refer to the comprehensive virtue of God-conscious righteousness | Some scholars distinguish birr (outward righteousness) from taqwa (inward fear), but Q 2:177 equates them |
| Taqwa and worship | Taqwa is the divinely stated purpose (*maqsud*) of fasting, pilgrimage, and sacrifice | Consensus; all four madhhabs affirm this Qur'anic designation |
| Taqwa and intention | Good intention cannot override divine prohibition; taqwa means submitting to Allah's boundaries even with good intention | Hanafis and most schools: intention determines moral value, but taqwa requires compliance regardless of intention |
| Taqwa and social ethics | Taqwa has collective dimensions: enjoining good, forbidding evil, cooperating in righteousness | Consensus on social obligation; *birr* and *taqwa* in Q 5:2 are read as both individual and communal |

Qaradawi's distinctive contribution is his insistence on **application**: taqwa is not merely an abstract spiritual state but a lived, social reality that must govern how Muslims engage with politics, economics, arts, and leisure. He applies taqwa to the domain of entertainment — arguing that the person of taqwa neither rejects lawful pleasure nor indulges in prohibited excess — thereby extending the concept beyond its traditional spiritual domain.

## Contemporary Relevance

1. **Moral psychology**: In an age of moral relativism, taqwa provides an objective, Allah-centered standard for ethical decision-making. Qaradawi's emphasis on the inward dimension — that Allah "looks at your hearts and your deeds" — guards against both secular moralism and legalistic externalism.

2. **Digital entertainment**: Qaradawi's application of taqwa to the domain of diversion is directly relevant to contemporary questions about music, social media, streaming, and gaming. Taqwa requires self-regulation: enjoying lawful diversions without allowing them to encroach on obligations or corrupt one's heart.

3. **Social justice**: If taqwa is the sole basis of nobility (Q 49:13), then racism, tribalism, and class-based discrimination are all violations of taqwa. Qaradawi's extension of taqwa into collective ethics demands that believers cooperate in promoting good, not merely avoid sin individually.

4. **Self-improvement and spiritual development**: In *tazkiyah* (spiritual purification), taqwa is both the starting point and the goal — the believer cultivates God-consciousness through worship, which in turn deepens the desire for worship, creating an upward spiral of spiritual growth.
