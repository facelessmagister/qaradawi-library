# Ijtihad (اجْتِهَاد)

**Arabic:** اجْتِهَاد  
**Literal meaning:** "Effort, exertion, striving" — from the root ج-ه-د (j-h-d)  
**Technical meaning:** Independent juristic reasoning by a qualified scholar (*mujtahid*) to derive a ruling from the primary sources of Sharīʿah when no explicit text (*naṣṣ*) exists. It is the intellectual process by which Islamic law adapts to new circumstances without altering its foundations.

## Definition

**Ijtihad** is the exertion of maximum intellectual effort by a qualified jurist (*mujtahid*) to derive a legal ruling (*ḥukm sharʿī*) from the Qur'an and Sunnah through the recognized methods of *uṣūl al-fiqh* — including analogy (*qiyās*), juristic preference (*istiḥsān*), consideration of public interest (*maṣlaḥah*), and other secondary sources. It applies only in the *zone of probability* (*ẓann*) where no definitive text (*naṣṣ qaṭʿī*) exists.

As al-Shāfiʿī stated: when the matter reaches the Followers (Tābiʿūn) such as Ibrāhīm, al-Ḥasan, ʿAṭā', and Ibn al-Musayyab — "people do ijtihad, and I do ijtihad as they do." When a sahih hadith is established, al-Shāfiʿī declared he follows it absolutely; but when the text is not definitive, the jurist must exert his own reasoning. ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

## Qaradawi's Position

Qaradawi is one of the foremost contemporary advocates of ijtihad over taqlid (blind following). His treatment of ijtihad appears across multiple works and can be organized into the following key positions:

### 1. The Door of Ijtihad Is Open

Qaradawi forcefully rejects the claim that "the door of ijtihad was closed" after the first three centuries of Islam:

> "To say that the door of ijtihad is closed would incorrectly leave these issues unsettled. None can close the door of ijtihad since it was opened by the Messenger of God (P)." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

He holds that this closure — a claim historically associated with rigid madhhab partisanship — is both doctrinally unjustified and practically devastating, because new circumstances continuously demand new juristic engagement. The pressing questions of the modern age — about zakatable asset classes, contemporary taxation, financial instruments, and the relationship between zakah and state tax systems — all require fresh ijtihad. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

### 2. Ijtihad Requires Genuine Competence, Not Reckless Innovation

While championing ijtihad, Qaradawi draws a sharp line between qualified juristic reasoning and reckless disregard for the tradition:

> "I do not subscribe to fanaticism that puts a taboo on all new opinions found after the early four imams, believing that none are accepted after them, that ijtihad came to an end after the first three centuries of Islam and that truth lies only in the writings of those schools and those generations. On the other hand, I would not blindly follow those who claim to be making ijtihad without fulfilling all its requirements, those who call for top-to-bottom renovation." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

This balanced stance — rejecting both the closure of ijtihad and its unqualified appropriation — is a hallmark of Qaradawi's methodology. He quotes al-Rāfiʿī's satirical line about those who "wanted to renovate religion, language, even the sun and the moon" as a caution against those who practice pseudo-ijtihad. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

### 3. Specialized Ijtihad

Qaradawi endorses the position of reliable scholars of *uṣūl* that ijtihad can be exercised on individual issues (*ijtihad fī al-nawāzil*) without requiring the jurist to be an absolute mujtahid across the entire Sharīʿah. In the modern age, "specialized ijtihad becomes the common rule rather than the exception":

> "Reliable scholars of uṣūl are determined that ijtihad can be exerted on individual issues, since some people may specialize in certain areas of jurisprudence. In our days this specialized ijtihad becomes the common rule rather than the exception. Hard work and exerted preparation are required to understand the texts of the original sources of this religion from the linguistic and the religious points of view, and those who have prepared themselves well for ijtihad can obviously exercise it." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

### 4. Collective Ijtihad (*Ijtihad Jamāʿī*)

While affirming individual ijtihad as preparatory and necessary, Qaradawi calls for a collective ijtihad (*ijtihad jamāʿī*) in which a body of qualified Muslim jurists deliberate together on contemporary challenges:

> "While I believe that giving fulfilling answers to such questions requires a collective ijtihad exercised by a group of Muslim scholars, I maintain that individual effort and research undoubtedly pave the way for sound collective ijtihad until a time comes when Muslim states make it feasible for reliable scholars to work together as teams of collective ijtihad." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

He applies this principle, for example, to the question of whether an equivalent of zakah may be levied on non-Muslim citizens as a civic tax — a "new matter which needs collective ijtihad of capable Muslim jurists." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-05.txt]

### 5. Ijtihad and Analogy (*Qiyās*) in Zakah

Throughout *Fiqh al-Zakah*, Qaradawi applies ijtihad through analogy (*qiyās*) to extend zakah rulings to new categories of wealth:

- **Zakah on gold:** The ruling is derived by analogy from silver, "because of the apparent similarity in use of the two metals." He upholds the story of ʿUmar with Yalā bin Umayyah as evidence "that analogy and ijtihad have an important position in zakah." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-02.txt]

- **Rates in the absence of text:** Qaradawi states plainly: "The rates, wherever there is no text, are subject to ijtihad." Where the Prophet (P) took zakah from certain asset types, "that does not mean that Muslims after him may not take it from other kinds that are similar. In fact every substantial growing asset must be zakatable." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-02.txt]

- **Cross-category extension:** "Applying this rate on all other animals acquired for breeding for profit is undoubtedly a correct ijtihad, based on analogy between similar growing assets." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-02.txt]

- **Minerals and sea products:** The rate on sea products is determined through ijtihad, as ʿUmar consulted the Companions. "There is nothing to prevent new opinions on this matter, as long as they are founded on valid evidence and reasoning." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-07.txt]

- **Rental income from buildings and factories:** Endorsing the view of three scholars on zakah on income of fixed assets as "correct ijtihad, based on sound analogy" between such income and agricultural produce. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-08.txt]

- **Third opinions on contested matters:** Where scholars of a generation differ on an issue, "this is in itself an indication that this issue is controversial in which all can exert effort and apply ijtihad. A third and later opinion is merely an outcome of such ijtihad and consequently should be accommodated." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

### 6. Renewal (*Tajdīd*) and Ijtihad

In *Approaching the Sunnah*, Qaradawi links ijtihad to the broader concept of religious renewal (*tajdīd*):

> "It is the same with the religion: its renewal does not mean the issuing of a 'new edition' of it. Rather, it means the reversion of it to where it was in the epoch of the Messenger and his Companions, and of those who followed them with sincerity. It means: the revival of ijtihad in it, and recourse to its original wellsprings, liberation from inflexibility and imitativeness (taqlīd) and examination of the legacy with a critical eye so as to benefit from its positive qualities and guard against any points of deficiency in it." ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-01.txt]

### 7. Non-Partisan Methodology and the Search for Truth

Qaradawi describes his own approach as rooted in ijtihad rather than taqlid:

> "I read texts and views as an investigator, an examiner, a researcher who seeks the truth regardless of where or whom it comes from. It may be found with predecessors jurists or with later generations… I am committed throughout my research to the principles of no bias, non-partisanship, righteousness, open-mindedness to every and all useful renovation and keenness to retain every correct ancient view or opinion." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

He considers following a single jurist in all his opinions — even weak ones — to be tantamount to "taking that jurist as a legislator or a maker of Sharīʿah," since "imitation itself is a form of annulment of mind and its virtue." He quotes Ibn al-Jawzī: "The mind is created to think and adore." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

## Evidence Base

### Qur'anic Principles

- **"And of the fruit of the earth which We have produced for you"** (Q 6:141) — general text whose scope is maintained through ijtihad unless restricted by a sound, specific text. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]
- **"God commands you to render trusts to those to whom they are due, and when you judge between people, judge with fairness"** (Q 4:58) — the principle of justice undergirds qiyās as ijtihad's primary tool.
- **"Out of their wealth take ṣadaqah"** (Q 9:103) — general obligation whose details require juristic elaboration.

### Prophetic Precedents

- The practice of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb in levying zakah on horses and adjusting rates — a prime example of ijtihad by a Rightly-Guided Caliph. "The majority of jurists consider the action of ʿUmar as his own ijtihad, which is not binding" in the sense of fixing a precedent, but it validates the principle that rates in the absence of text are subject to juristic reasoning. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-02.txt]
- The Prophet's dispatch of Muʿādh to Yemen: "According to what God has set in His Book… and according to my Sunnah… and if that does not suffice, then I exercise my own reasoning (ajtahidu ra'yī)." ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-03.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

### Ḥanafī School
- Historically associated with the closure of ijtihad after the eponym Abū Ḥanīfah, though later Ḥanafīs permitted *takhrīj* (extrapolation by disciples within the school's framework).
- Abū Ḥanīfah himself was a mujtahid who practiced qiyās extensively. Qaradawi notes that he "rejects" certain authentic hadiths restricting general texts, which Qaradawi considers a weakness in methodology when the hadith is sound.

### Mālikī School
- Imām Mālik practiced ijtihad through *amal ahl al-Madīnah* (the practice of the people of Medina) as a source alongside qiyās.
- The Mālikī principle of *istiḥsān* (juristic preference) represents ijtihad that departs from strict analogy when equity so demands.

### Shāfiʿī School
- Al-Shāfiʿī is the architect of *uṣūl al-fiqh* as a formal discipline. He articulated the hierarchy of sources (Qur'an → Sunnah → Ijmāʿ → Qiyās) that structures all subsequent ijtihad.
- His statement — "people do ijtihad, and I do ijtihad as they do" — demonstrates that ijtihad was recognized and practiced even by the systematizer of legal theory. ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

### Ḥanbalī School
- Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim are cited by Qaradawi as exemplars of renewed ijtihad who returned to the original sources. ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-02.txt]
- Ibn al-Qayyim's definition of correct analogy (*qiyās*) as "the Balance that God sent along with His Book" is adopted by Qaradawi. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

### Zāhirī School
- Rejected qiyās entirely, limiting ijtihad to direct extraction from texts.
- Qaradawi disagrees with this restriction, treating analogy as an integral tool of ijtihad.

### Post-Classical Reformers
- Qaradawi cites a lineage of reformers who practiced ijtihad after the supposed "closure": Ibn Ḥajar, al-Suyūṭī, Ibn Wazīr, al-Shawkānī, al-Ṣanʿānī, and Ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhāb as "scholars of high rank in ijtihad, and leaders of the reformers." ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

## Qaradawi's Criteria for Sound Ijtihad

Qaradawi outlines explicit criteria for weighing arguments and deriving new opinions across *Fiqh al-Zakah*:

1. **Adopting general texts** unless restricted by a sound, clear, and authentic specific text. General Qur'anic verses and Prophetic sayings "should be received and taken as general, except when their scopes are narrowed by another text that is correct, authentic and clear in its meaning." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

2. **Accepting genuine consensus (*ijmāʿ*)** as binding, but being skeptical of claimed consensus where disagreement existed. "I mentioned true ijmāʿ because some jurists reported ijmāʿ on issues that are still controversial." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

3. **Accommodating third opinions** where the Companions and Followers disagreed: "The mere fact that scholars of a certain generation differ on an issue and express two opinions is in itself an indication that this issue is controversial in which all can exert effort and apply ijtihad. A third and later opinion is merely an outcome of such ijtihad and consequently should be accommodated." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

4. **Elaborating appropriate analogy (*qiyās*)** — treating similar cases similarly based on a shared effective cause (*ʿillah*). Following Ibn Taymiyyah: "Analogy is an inclusive word. It includes correct analogy and incorrect analogy, but only the correct analogy is accepted in Sharīʿah." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

5. **Subordinating all opinions to the definitive texts** — "Only one thing limits all scholars. That is the unmistakable words of the Book of God or the tradition of His Messenger." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

## Contemporary Relevance

### Modern Financial Instruments
Qaradawi's ijtihad extends zakah to salary income, stocks, bonds, rental income, and retirement savings — categories unknown to the classical jurists but governed by the same principles of wealth (*māl*), growth (*namā'*), threshold (*niṣāb*), and annual cycle (*ḥawl*).

### State and Taxation
The question of whether contemporary state taxes can substitute for or coexist with zakah demands collective ijtihad by a body of qualified jurists, not merely individual opinions.

### Estimation (*ikhtīyāl*) and Ijtihad
Qaradawi endorses pre-harvest estimation of crops as a form of ijtihad: "Estimation is the result of the exertion of best efforts by experienced experts. It is therefore like ijtihad, and ijtihad is always practiced when a fixed rule is not available." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-05.txt]

### Between Taqlid and Free Innovation
Qaradawi occupies a middle position: rejecting the closure of ijtihad (taqlid) while equally rejecting reckless innovation that claims the mantle of ijtihad without meeting its rigorous scholarly requirements. His own *Fiqh al-Zakah* is an extended exercise of ijtihad — examining every opinion of the four madhhabs, the Zāhirīs, and independent scholars, then following the strongest evidence.
