# Music / Musiqa (مُوسِيقَى)

**Arabic:** مُوسِيقَى (*mūsīqā*) — instrumental music; also: *al-ma'āzif* (المَعَازِف) — musical instruments
**Related term:** غِنَاء (*ghinā'*) — vocal singing (see [[concept-singing]])
**Domain:** Fiqh al-Ibadat, Islamic Ethics, Arts & Entertainment

## Definition

*Mūsīqā* (مُوسِيقَى) is the Arabic term for music — specifically instrumental music produced by instruments such as the lute (*'ūd*), rebab, tambourine (*duff*), and flute (*nāy*). The closely related term *al-ma'āzif* (المَعَازِف) refers to musical instruments collectively and appears in the disputed hadith about people making intoxicants and *ma'āzif* lawful. The fiqh of **instrumental music** is distinct from the fiqh of **vocal singing** (see [[concept-singing]]), though the two are often discussed together. Vocal singing without instruments enjoys broader scholarly consensus on permissibility; instrumental music is far more contested.

Qaradawi treats the two topics sequentially in *Diversion and Arts in Islam*: vocal singing in Chapter 4, and then the broader question of musical instruments within the same chapter. He distinguishes between them while acknowledging their interconnection, ultimately ruling that **instrumental music requires a graduated, conditions-based ruling rather than blanket prohibition or blanket permission**.

## Qaradawi's Position

### 1. The Arabic Term and Its Heritage

The Arabic word *mūsīqā* is a loanword from Greek (*mousikē*), reflecting the historical integration of music into Islamic civilization. Qaradawi documents that the early Muslim community produced rich musical traditions: the *adhān* (call to prayer), Qur'anic recitation with melodic chanting (*tartīl*), liturgical poetry (*inshād*), work songs, wedding songs, and folk epics sung to the accompaniment of the rebab and other instruments. He cites the hadith: **"Adorn the Qur'an with your voices"** — establishing that aesthetic vocal production is not merely tolerated but positively encouraged in the context of worship.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-02.txt]

### 2. Musical Instruments in the Prophetic and Companion Era

Qaradawi provides extensive evidence that musical instruments were known and used in the time of the Prophet and the Companions without blanket prohibition:

- The *duff* (tambourine with jingling rings) was explicitly allowed, particularly at weddings and festive occasions. The Prophet said: **"The difference between what is lawful and what is unlawful is the *duff* and the voice at weddings."**^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-07.txt]

- The Prophet himself facilitated the Ethiopian warriors' display with spears in his mosque, which included rhythmic chanting and percussion.

- Numerous Companions and Followers permitted or practiced instrumental music: 'Abdullāh ibn Ja'far kept a slave-girl who played the lute; Ibn 'Umar listened to and approved of singing; and the people of Madīnah reached consensus (*ijmā'*) on allowing the lute.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

### 3. The Graduated Ruling Framework

Qaradawi rejects both the blanket prohibition of all instruments and the blanket permission of all instruments. Instead, he adopts a **graduated approach** that distinguishes based on the type of instrument, the context of use, and the effect on the listener:

**a) The *duff* (tambourine)** — **Permitted by consensus.** The *duff* is explicitly sanctioned in the Sunnah for weddings and celebrations. Its permissibility is not contested.

**b) String and wind instruments (*ma'āzif*)** — **Contextually evaluated.** Qaradawi, following al-Ghazali, argues that the prohibition of string and wind instruments in the classical sources is not based on the instruments themselves but on their **association with gatherings of drinking, licentiousness, and moral corruption**. In the context of the Abbasid era, these instruments were fixtures of taverns and courts of debauchery. The prohibition is therefore analogous to the prohibition of *khamr* (wine): not the drink per se but what it leads to (*sadd al-dharī'ah* — blocking the means to sin).

However, when the same instruments are used in settings free of prohibited elements — religious singing, folk heritage, personal entertainment without *harām* content — the rationale for prohibition falls away. Qaradawi notes: **"There is not a single authentic and clear-cut revealed text which forbids these instruments"** — their prohibition is based on indirect reasoning, not direct textual evidence.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

**c) Modern instruments (piano, guitar, etc.)** — **Evaluated by analogy.** Instruments unknown in the classical period are evaluated by the same criteria: their typical context of use, their effect on listeners, and whether their use leads to *harām*.

### 4. Al-Ghazali's Framework — Adopted and Modified

Qaradawi adopts al-Ghazali's five conditions for the permissibility of music, modifying them slightly:

1. **No seduction** (*fitnah*): A woman's singing or playing before unrelated men (*ajānib*) is prohibited.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

2. **No prohibited instruments in prohibited settings**: String and wind instruments associated with drinking gatherings are prohibited *because of their association with sin*, not because of the sound they produce.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

3. **No obscene or blasphemous lyrics**: The content of what is sung or played must conform to the moral code of Islam.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

4. **No excessive preoccupation**: If music dominates a person's time and attention to the exclusion of obligations, it becomes blameworthy or prohibited.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

5. **Individual susceptibility**: If a particular type of music personally arouses or seduces a specific individual, that individual must avoid it — even if it is generally permissible.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

### 5. The Role of Intention (*Niyyah*)

As with singing, intention determines the moral quality of listening to music. Qaradawi states: **"Good intention transforms diversion into an approach to Allah. It can intermingle fun with obedience to Allah. Evil intention corrupts good deeds which are done for mere pretence rather than worship of Allah."** This principle, drawn from the hadith "Actions are judged by intentions," applies directly to both singing and instrumental music.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

However, Qaradawi adds a crucial qualification: **"Good intentions, piety, or sincerity do not excuse transgression on Allah's Sovereignty."** Intention cannot transform a prohibited act into a permissible one; it can only elevate a permissible act into a meritorious one.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

### 6. Music and the Qur'an

Qaradawi devotes significant attention in *Diversion and Arts* (Chapter 2) to the Qur'an as an aesthetic experience. He cites the hadith encouraging melodious recitation: **"Adorn the Qur'an with your voices"** — proof that the aesthetic dimension of sound is not merely tolerated but positively encouraged when directed toward divine remembrance. He references Sheikh Muhammad Abdullah Draz's argument that listening to the Qur'an is not merely an obligation but an aesthetic experience fulfilling intellectual, emotional, and spiritual needs simultaneously.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-02.txt]

### 7. Sufi Music and Its Critics

Qaradawi addresses *samā'* — the Sufi practice of listening to music as a means of spiritual elevation. Some Sufis considered it worship; Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim condemned it as *bid'ah* (innovation). Qaradawi takes a nuanced position: **Sufi religious singing is not inherently forbidden**, but it becomes blameworthy when it replaces obligatory worship, leads to excess, or involves prohibited elements. He notes that Ibn Taymiyyah himself allowed singing as a form of entertainment or relief from embarrassment.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

## Evidence Base

### Qur'anic Verses

- **Q 7:31-32** — "O children of Adam, take your adornment at every masjid… Who has forbidden the adornment of Allah which He has produced for His servants?" — Establishes that beauty and enjoyment are divine gifts, not evils to be suppressed.
- **Q 31:6** — "And there are among people some who buy vain talk" — *lahw al-hadīth* refers to disbelief and mockery, not music.
- **Q 28:55** — "And when they hear people speaking nonsense, they avoid it" — abstention, not prohibition.
- **Q 10:59** — "Say: Have you seen the sustenance which Allah has sent down for you, then you make thereof some lawful and some unlawful?" — Condemnation of prohibiting what Allah has permitted.
- **Q 6:140** — Condemnation of those who "forbid what God has provided for them, falsely ascribing prohibitions to God."

### Prophetic Hadiths

- **"Adorn the Qur'an with your voices"** — al-Bukhari and others; establishes that aesthetic vocal production is encouraged.
- **"The difference between what is lawful and what is unlawful is the *duff* and the voice at weddings"** — establishes the *duff* as the marker of lawful celebration.
- **The *ma'āzif* hadith** (al-Bukhari, *mu'allaq*) — "There will be people of my nation who will make intoxicants, silk, and *ma'āzif* lawful" — the weakest of the prohibition hadiths due to its *mu'allaq* status and contested meaning of *ma'āzif*.
- **The Ethiopians in the Mosque** — al-Bukhari and Muslim; the Prophet facilitated rhythmic performance in his mosque.
- **"O Hanzala, you should devote part of your time to the lawful enjoyment in this world"** — Muslim; establishes leisure as legitimately needed.

### Scholarly Consensus Reports

- **Ibn Hazm**: Declared all prohibition hadiths either *da'īf* (weak) or *mawdū'* (fabricated), and that there is no authentic prohibition of music.
- **Judge Abū Bakr Ibn al-'Arabī**: Confirmed in *Al-Ahkām* that no authentic hadith declares music forbidden.
- **Al-Ghazali**: In *Ihyā' 'Ulūm al-Dīn*, concluded that singing — even with instruments — is permissible under the five conditions.
- **People of Madīnah**: Reached consensus (*ijmā'*) on the lawfulness of singing with instruments including the lute.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

| Position | Scholars | Ruling on Instruments |
|----------|----------|---------------------|
| **Absolute prohibition** | Some later Hanbalīs, Ibn Taymiyyah, Ibn al-Qayyim | All instruments are *harām* |
| **Prohibited except *duff*** | Many Mālikīs, Shāfi'īs, some Hanbalīs | *Duff* permitted at weddings; all other instruments *harām* |
| **Permitted with conditions** | Al-Ghazali, Zāhirīs, some Shāfi'īs, people of Madīnah | Instruments are *mubāh* unless surrounded by *harām* conditions |
| **Qaradawi** | — | Graduated ruling: *duff* permitted by consensus; other instruments contextually evaluated based on association with sin, not inherent prohibition; five conditions must be met |

Qaradawi's distinctive contribution is his insistence on **distinguishing the instrument from its context of use**. Classical discussions often conflated the Abbasid-era tavern setting with the instrument itself. Qaradawi argues that this conflation is a logical error: the prohibition is on the *gathering of sin* (*majlis al-ma'siyah*), not on the *sound of the instrument* (*sawt al-ālah*). When the context changes — a family celebration, a religious *inshād*, a personal listening environment free of *harām* — the rationale for prohibition no longer applies.

## Contemporary Relevance

1. **Recorded and streamed music**: Qaradawi's framework was developed for live performance contexts. The rise of recorded music changes the calculus: listeners are no longer forced to attend gatherings of sin to hear music. This, Qaradawi notes, "reduces the penalty" and "lowers the verdict to the level of permission and easiness."

2. **Islamic nasheed and religious music**: The explosion of the nasheed industry raises the question of whether setting religious lyrics to music changes the ruling on the instruments themselves. Qaradawi's intention principle suggests it does — but only when the five conditions are simultaneously met.

3. **Music education and therapy**: If music is *mubāh* (permissible) in principle, then learning music, studying music theory, and using music for therapeutic purposes are also permissible, provided the content and context conditions are satisfied.

4. **Secular vs. sacred binary**: Qaradawi rejects the notion that "religious" music is automatically superior to "secular" music. A patriotic folk song with wholesome lyrics may be more Islamically appropriate than a so-called religious song set in a context of *harām*. The evaluation depends on content, context, and intention — not the label "religious."
