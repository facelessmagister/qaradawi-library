# Justice / Adalah (عَدْل)

**Arabic:** عَدْل  
**Transliteration:** ʿAdalah / ʿAdl  
**Domain:** Islamic Ethics (الأخلاق الإسلامية) and Fiqh al-Muʿamalat

## Definition

*ʿAdl* (عَدْل) — justice, fairness, equity — is one of the most fundamental concepts in Islamic ethics and law. The Quran uses the term and its derivatives extensively, commanding: "Indeed, Allah orders justice and good conduct and giving to relatives and forbids immorality and bad conduct and oppression" (16:90). In Qaradawi's treatment, justice is not an abstract philosophical ideal but a concrete divine command that permeates every dimension of human life — personal, social, economic, and legal. Justice (*ʿadl*) is the standard by which Allah commands people to judge: "Indeed, Allah commands you to render trusts to whom they are due, and when you judge between people, to judge with justice" (4:58).

Justice in Islam encompasses: (1) **justice toward Allah** — fulfilling the rights of the Creator through worship, sincerity, and obedience; (2) **justice toward others** — upholding their rights in transactions, testimony, governance, and all social relations; and (3) **justice toward oneself** — refraining from self-harm and fulfilling one's own physical and spiritual needs in moderation.

## Qaradawi's Position

### 1. Justice Is a Divine Command and a Moral Imperative

Qaradawi establishes that justice is not merely a virtue to aspire to but a binding divine command. Allah says: "Indeed, Allah orders justice (*ʿadl*) and good conduct (*iḥsān*) and giving to relatives" (16:90). The command to establish justice is among the most emphatic in the Quran: "O you who have believed, be persistently standing firm for Allah, witnesses in justice, and do not let the hatred of a people prevent you from being just. Be just; that is nearer to righteousness" (5:8).^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-01-03.txt]

### 2. Justice Requires an Islamic Framework

In *Ethics in Islam* (Chapter 2), Qaradawi argues that Islamic justice cannot be implemented in fragments — it requires the whole Islamic system. He illustrates this with the example of *hudud* punishments: the penalty for theft cannot be applied in a society where zakat and social welfare are not properly implemented, where corruption and injustice are prevalent, and where people steal out of genuine poverty. "In a true Muslim society, no one steals out of poverty." The harshness of Islamic penalties is balanced by extremely high bars for conviction (e.g., four impeccable witnesses for adultery) and by the comprehensive social safety net that eliminates the economic motivations for crime.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

### 3. Justice and Punishment Are Inseparable

In *Ethics in Islam* (Section 3.3), Qaradawi discusses punishment (*ʿuqūbah*) as the legal consequence of violating justice. Islamic jurisprudence specifies sanctions for crimes that threaten social structure: theft, aggravated robbery, adultery, premeditated murder, and slander. The severity of these sanctions is proportional to the gravity of the injustice they address. However, Qaradawi emphasizes that the bar for conviction is deliberately very high — the system is designed to make punishment difficult to impose, preferring mercy over retribution where doubt exists.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-03-03.txt]

### 4. Justice in Economic Life

Qaradawi's *Fiqh al-Zakah* and *Economic Security in Islam* treat economic justice extensively. The zakat system is a mechanism of wealth redistribution that recognizes the poor's enforceable right (*ḥaqq*) in the wealth of the rich: "And in their wealth there is a right of the needy, he who asks, and he who is deprived" (51:19). Justice in economic life requires: zakat collection and distribution, prohibition of usury (*ribā*), fair wages, honest trade practices, and the elimination of exploitation.^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]

### 5. Justice and the Dignity of Man

In *Faith and Life* (Chapter 1), Qaradawi connects justice directly to human dignity as established by faith. The Islamic creed affirms that every human being is honored by Allah (*mukarram*, Quran 17:70), which means that justice must be extended to all regardless of race, class, or status. Racism, classism, and all forms of dehumanization are violations of the divine honor bestowed on every child of Adam.^[raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-01.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

### Al-Ghazali (d. AH 505)

Al-Ghazali treated justice as the middle virtue between extremes — courage between recklessness and cowardice, generosity between extravagance and stinginess, and so on. Qaradawi adopts this Aristotelian-Ghazalian framework of the middle path but emphasizes its systemic rather than merely individual dimension.

### Dr. Muhammad Abdullah Draz (d. 1958)

Draz's *Dustūr al-Akhlāq fī al-Qur'an* (The Moral World of the Qur'an) provides Qaradawi's primary framework for understanding moral obligation. Draz argued that moral obligation is the pivot of any ethical system: "Without a well-defined view of moral obligation, any ethical system would not be worthy of its name." Qaradawi adopts this and grounds moral obligation in divine command, making justice an imperative, not merely an aspiration.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-03-01.txt]

### Ibn al-Qayyim al-Jawziyya (d. AH 751)

Ibn al-Qayyim emphasized justice as the purpose (*maqṣid*) of all divine legislation, famously stating that the Sharia in its entirety is justice. Qaradawi echoes this position in arguing that Islamic law cannot be understood apart from its moral and social context.

## Dimensions of Justice in Qaradawi's Corpus

1. **Justice toward Allah (ḥaqq Allāh)** — Worship, sincerity, gratitude, and obedience.
2. **Justice toward people (ḥuqūq al-ʿibād)** — Fair transactions, honest testimony, equitable governance, and social welfare.
3. **Justice toward oneself (ḥaqq al-nafs)** — Moderation, self-discipline, and avoiding self-harm.
4. **Economic justice** — Zakat, prohibition of ribā, fair wages, and wealth redistribution.
5. **Legal justice** — Hudud with high evidentiary standards, proportionality, and mercy over retribution.

## Contemporary Relevance

1. **Rule of law**: Qaradawi's insistence that Islamic justice requires an Islamic framework — not piecemeal application of penalties — speaks directly to contemporary debates about implementing Sharia in Muslim-majority states.
2. **Economic inequality**: The zakat system and the prohibition of usury (*ribā*) address global wealth concentration and exploitation.
3. **Human rights**: The Quranic grounding of human dignity in divine honor (17:70) provides a robust theistic foundation for human rights that secular frameworks struggle to justify.
4. **Anti-discrimination**: Justice toward all people regardless of identity is a divine command, not a social convention — making racism, classism, and all forms of dehumanization religiously impermissible.
5. **Restorative vs. retributive justice**: Qaradawi's emphasis on the high bar for conviction and the systemic context of criminal justice anticipates modern restorative justice frameworks.
