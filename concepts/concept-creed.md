# Creed (عَقِيدَة)

**Arabic:** عَقِيدَة  
**Domain:** Aqeedah (Islamic Theology and Creed)

## Definition

Creed (*ʿaqīdah*, عَقِيدَة) is the foundational set of beliefs that forms the bedrock of Islamic faith (*īmān*, إِيمَان). It encompasses the Muslim's conviction in the existence and oneness of Allah (تَوْحِيد), the truth of revelation, the prophethood of Muhammad ﷺ, the angels, the revealed books, the Day of Judgment, and divine decree (*qadar*). In Qaradawi's treatment, creed is not merely an abstract theological construct but the living spring from which human dignity, moral purpose, and cosmic significance flow. The Islamic creed answers the fundamental questions of human existence — who man is, why he was created, and what his destiny entails — by anchoring him in the knowledge that he is a honored vicegerent (*khalīfah*, خَلِيفَة) of Allah on earth, created in the best of molds and endowed with a spirit breathed from Allah's own Spirit.

## Qaradawi's Position

Qaradawi presents creed as the lens through which the believer understands his entire existence. In *Faith and Life* (Chapter 1: "Iman and the Dignity of Man"), he structures his argument around a decisive contrast: the materialist conception of man versus the creedal conception. His treatment unfolds across several key themes:

### 1. Man's Dignity Flows from Creed, Not from Material Worth

Qaradawi opens by showing that materialism reduces man to mere matter — "a piece of flesh, blood, nerves, systems, glands, and cells" — whose elemental worth is negligible. Against this, the Islamic creed affirms man's supreme position: Allah created him in the best of molds (*aḥsan taqwīm*, أَحْسَنِ تَقْوِيم*), breathed into him of His Spirit, commanded the angels to prostrate before him, and made him His vicegerent on earth.^[raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-01.txt]

### 2. Man's Position Before Allah

The creed establishes that man is nearer to Allah than his own jugular vein (Quran 50:16). There is no need for mediators or intercessors between the believer and his Lord. Qaradawi cites the verse: *"When My servants ask thee concerning Me, I am indeed close (to them)"* (Al-Baqarah: 186) and the Divine hadith: *"I am as My servant thinks I am, and I am with him whenever he remembers Me."*^[raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-01.txt]

### 3. Man's Position Among the Angels

The creed teaches that man holds a station above the angels by virtue of the trust (*amānah*, أَمَانَة) given to him: *"We did indeed offer the Trust to the Heavens and the Earth and the Mountains; but they refused to undertake it, being afraid thereof: but man undertook it"* (Al-Ahzab: 72). Allah taught Adam the names, demonstrating a knowledge the angels lacked, and commanded angelic prostration before Adam — a creedal fact that provoked Iblis's rebellion.^[raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-01.txt]

### 4. Man's Dominion in the Material World

Everything in the heavens and earth has been subjected to man's service (Ibrahim: 32–34; Al-Jathiyah: 12–13; Luqman: 20; Al-Isra': 70). This subjugation is not an end in itself but a trust and a means by which man fulfills his vicegerency.^[raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-01.txt]

### 5. The Honor of Iman Transcends the Honor of Humanity

Qaradawi draws a crucial distinction: the dignity of being human (*karāmat al-insān*, كَرَامَةُ الإِنْسَان) is one thing, but the dignity of being a *believer* (*karāmat al-mu'min*, كَرَامَةُ المُؤْمِن) is far greater. He cites: *"Ye are the best of Peoples, evolved for mankind"* (Al `Imran: 110), *"He has chosen you, and has imposed no difficulties on you in religion"* (Al-Hajj: 78), and *"But honor belongs to Allah and His Messenger, and to the believers"* (Al-Munafiqun: 8).^[raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-01.txt]

### 6. Creed vs. Materialism: Three Essential Contradictions

Qaradawi identifies three axes where the Islamic creed contradicts the materialist worldview:
1. **Man's position in the universe** — vicegerent vs. evolved animal
2. **Man's instinctive nature** — dual (body + spirit, clay + divine breath) vs. purely material
3. **Man's goal and role** — worship and vicegerency leading to eternity vs. hedonistic futility ending in nothing

Islam holds a middle ground: it neither denies the body for the spirit (as in asceticism) nor the spirit for the body (as in materialism), but integrates both in harmony, giving each its right.^[raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-01.txt]

### 7. The Dual Nature of Man

The creed holds that man consists of a physical body (drawn from clay) and a transparent soul (from Allah's breath). This is not accidental but essential to his vicegerency. Qaradawi cites Surah As-Sajdah: 6–9.^[raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-01.txt]

### 8. Historical Illustrations of Creed's Transformative Power

Qaradawi demonstrates the practical power of creed with two examples:
- **Bilal ibn Rabah** — a black slave who, through the honor of iman, stood above the masters of Quraysh.
- **Ribʿi ibn ʿAmir** — an illiterate Bedouin who confronted the Persian general Rustum with the creedal declaration: *"We are people sent by Allah to lead people away from the worship of creatures, to the worship of the Lord of creatures."*^[raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-01.txt]

### Hadith and Scholarly Citations

- **Divine hadith** on proximity: *"I am as My servant thinks I am… if he draws near to Me a hand's span, I draw near to him an arm's length…"*
- **Divine hadith** on loving worship: *"My servant continues to draw near to Me with voluntary acts so that I shall love him… I become his hearing, his sight, his tongue…"*
- **Abu Bakr ibn al-ʿArabi**: *"Allah has never created a better creature than man, for He created him as living, knowledgeable, mighty, able to speak, able to see, able to dispose of his affairs, wise — and these are the attributes of the Lord."*
- **Imam al-Ghazali** (*Ihya' ʿUlum al-Din*): On the harmony between the entity of man and the entity of Allah as a reason for love — adopting the morals of Lordship through knowledge, beneficence, mercy, and guidance.
- **Ibn al-Qayyim** (*Madarij as-Salikin*): Allah favored man with special gifts, created him for Himself, subjected all creation to his use, and addressed him directly.
- **Prophetic hadith** on moderation: The Prophet counseled against extremes in worship — fast some days and break the fast on others, pray and sleep — balancing spiritual and physical needs.
- **Prophetic advice**: *"Act towards life as if you were living forever, and work for the Hereafter as if you were dying the following day."*

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

Qaradawi's treatment aligns with mainstream Sunni theological positions while emphasizing practical and existential dimensions:

- **With the Ash'ari school**: Qaradawi affirms the dual nature of man (body + spirit) and divine decree, consistent with Ash'ari theology, though his focus is pastoral rather than dialectical.
- **With the Maturidi school**: His emphasis on man's innate dignity and rational capacity resonates with Maturidi positions on human nature (*fiṭrah*, فِطْرَة*).
- **With Imam al-Ghazali**: Qaradawi draws directly from Ghazali's *Ihya'* on the theme of harmony between the servant and the Lord through adopting divine attributes.
- **With Ibn al-Qayyim**: He cites Ibn al-Qayyim extensively on man's special position among creation, reflecting Hanball theological tradition within the broader Sunni consensus.
- **Distinction**: Unlike classical kalam works that treat creed through propositional arguments (proofs of God's existence, attributes, etc.), Qaradawi frames creed existentially — as that which gives man dignity, purpose, and a cosmic role. This is a da'wah-oriented approach rather than a purely theological one.

## Contemporary Relevance

Qaradawi argues that the Islamic creed is the decisive answer to the crisis of modernity. With the spread of materialist philosophy, Darwinian reductionism, and existentialist despair, man has been reduced to an "evolved animal" whose worth is measured in market value of his chemical components. The creed restores:

1. **Human dignity** — against racism, classism, and all forms of dehumanization, the creed declares every human being honored by Allah (*mukarram*, مُكَرَّم).
2. **Moral accountability** — without divine purpose, moral frameworks collapse; creed anchors ethics in transcendence.
3. **Psychological wholeness** — the middle path between ascetic denial of the body and materialist denial of the spirit heals the modern fractured self.
4. **Civilizational confidence** — the examples of Bilal and Ribʿi ibn ʿAmir show that creed can transform the marginalized into leaders of history.
