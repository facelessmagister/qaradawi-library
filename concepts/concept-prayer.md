# Prayer (صَلَاة)

**Arabic:** صَلَاة (ṣalāh)
**Domain:** Fiqh al-ʿIbādāt (Worship)

## Definition

Ṣalāh (صَلَاة) is the ritual prayer of Islam, performed five times daily at prescribed times. It constitutes the second pillar of Islam after the shahādah (testimony of faith) and is the most regularly repeated obligation in a Muslim's life. The term derives from the root ص-ل-و, conveying the sense of connection, supplication, and blessing. In Qaradawi's treatment, ṣalāh is not merely a ritual formality but a moral-spiritual discipline whose purpose is to transform the worshipper's character and sustain them through worldly struggles.

## Qaradawi's Position

### 1. Prayer as the Best Deed at Its Proper Time

Qaradawi, citing the Sunnah, emphasises that the Prophet (Peace be upon him) ranked "the Prayer at its proper time" as the most beloved deed to Allah, followed by filial piety and then striving in Allah's cause. This is reported by al-Bukhārī and Muslim on the authority of ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd. However, the Prophet gave different answers about the "best deed" to different questioners — belief, kinship, enjoining good, feeding people and greeting others, or being safe from harm — depending on each questioner's state and needs. Qaradawi uses this to illustrate the Prophetic educational principle of individual variations. ^[raw/extracted/education-economy-sunnah/ch-01.txt]

### 2. Prayer Prohibits Immorality — The Moral Purpose of Worship

A central theme in Qaradawi's ethics is that all acts of worship (ʿibādāt) have well-defined moral objectives. He cites the Qur'anic verse:

> "Indeed, prayer prohibits immorality and wrongdoing" (Qurʾān, 29:45)

Ṣalāh performed five times daily has a direct, ongoing influence on moral behaviour. It also provides spiritual strength for life's difficulties:

> "Oh you who believe, seek help through patience and prayer" (Qurʾān, 2:153)

^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

### 3. Prayer Without Moral Fruit Is Void

Qaradawi stresses that worship divorced from moral transformation loses its value entirely. He quotes the hadith:

> "It may be that a person praying Qiyām at night gains nothing but staying up late." ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

The same principle applies to fasting and other acts of worship; the ethical outcome is the criterion of acceptance. If ṣalāh does not translate into upright conduct, it fails its purpose.

### 4. Faith Defined Through Prayer — Integrative View

Qaradawi demonstrates that the Qurʾān repeatedly defines true faith (īmān) as inclusive of establishing prayer alongside moral deeds. He cites:

- **Sūrat al-Muʾminūn (23:1–8):** "Certainly will the faithful have succeeded: they are those who during their prayer are humbly submissive, and they who turn away from ill speech, and they who are observant of Zakāh…"
- **Sūrat al-Anfāl (8:2–4):** "The believers are only those who, when Allah is mentioned, their hearts quiver… the ones who establish prayer and from what We have provided them, they spend."
- **Sūrat al-Baqarah (2:177):** Righteousness includes believing in Allah and the Last Day, establishing prayer, giving wealth to relatives and those in need, and patient perseverance — integrating creed, ritual, and ethics inseparably.

^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

### 5. The Social and Communal Dimension of Prayer

Qaradawi highlights prayer's communal function. The Friday prayer ([ṣalāt al-jumuʿah](#)) is required to be performed in congregation, bringing all social classes together at least once a week. Even the Fāṭihah, recited in every prayer, uses the plural pronoun "we," embedding the individual worshipper within the collective conscience of the Ummah. ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

### 6. Moderation in Worship — Against Extremism

Qaradawi notes that the Sunnah rejects religious extremism in prayer. He cites the hadith of the three companions who vowed to pray all night, fast perpetually, and abstain from marriage — the Prophet (Peace be upon him) rejected all three extremes, stating: "I fast and break my fast; I pray and go to sleep; and I also marry women. Whoever does not follow my tradition in religion is not one of my followers." Similarly, the counsel to ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr: "Your body has a right on you, your eyes have a right on you, your wife has a right on you." This establishes the moderate path (wasat) in prayer, consistent with Qaradawi's broader emphasis on the middle-ground Ummah. ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

### 7. Prophetic Individualisation in Prayer Guidance

From [[education-economy-sunnah-ch-01|Education and Economy in the Sunnah — Chapter 1]], Qaradawi shows the Prophet varied counsel about prayer depending on the questioner. To one seeking general advice he said: "Worship Allah, associating nothing with Him; perform the Prayers; pay the Zakāh; keep good relations with relatives." The Prophet also gave different answers to "Which deed is the best?" — including "the Prayer at its proper time" — because educational and spiritual needs differ. This individualisation extends to fatwā: legal rulings about prayer (and other obligations) can differ based on personal circumstances, as illustrated by the hadith of the fasting man who was given different rulings on kissing. ^[raw/extracted/education-economy-sunnah/ch-01.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

Qaradawi's position on prayer largely aligns with the consensus (ijmāʿ) of the four madhāhib (Ḥanafī, Mālikī, Shāfiʿī, Ḥanbalī) on the obligation of the five daily prayers. Where he makes a distinct contribution is in his **ethical-functional** reading: he treats ṣalāh not as a standalone ritual obligation but as inseparable from moral character (akhlāq). This approach echoes al-Ghazālī's treatment in *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn* — where prayer's inner reality matters more than its outward form — but Qaradawi frames it within his broader wasatiyyah (middle-ground) methodology, rejecting both ritual formalism and ascetic excess. His emphasis on prayer's communal dimension and social function also reflects the Ḥanbalī emphasis on congregational prayer's public importance, while his focus on moral outcomes parallels Mālikī concerns with maṣlaḥah (public welfare).

## Contemporary Relevance

- **Prayer as moral discipline:** In modern societies where ethical frameworks are fragmented, Qaradawi's argument that ṣalāh's primary purpose is moral transformation — prohibiting immorality and wrongdoing — offers a practical, experiential rather than merely theoretical, grounding for virtue ethics.
- **Moderation against extremism:** His insistence that the Sunnah rejects excess in worship (praying all night, perpetual fasting) speaks directly to contemporary tendencies toward religious extremism and asceticism.
- **Individualisation in daʿwah:** The Prophetic model of giving different prayer-related counsel to different people provides a framework for modern Islamic education and pastoral guidance that respects personal variation.
- **Communal identity:** The social dimension of prayer — especially congregational Friday prayer — addresses modern individualism and the erosion of community bonds.
