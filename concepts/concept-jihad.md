# Jihad (جِهَاد)

**Arabic:** جِهَاد  
**Domain:** Fiqh al-Da'wah, Islamic Ethics, Tazkiyah  
**Transliteration:** jihād

## Definition

Jihad (جِهَاد) literally means "striving" or "struggling" — from the Arabic root j-h-d (ج-ه-د), meaning to exert utmost effort. In the Islamic scholarly tradition, jihad denotes striving in the path of Allah (فِي سَبِيلِ الله), encompassing both an inward, spiritual dimension — the struggle against one's own base desires (جِهَاد النَّفْس, jihad al-nafs) — and an outward dimension of lawful struggle against oppression and in defense of the faith. Qaradawi repeatedly emphasizes that the broader, primary sense of jihad is the inner moral struggle to purify the self and obey Allah, which the Prophet (PBUH) called the "greater jihad" (الجِهَاد الأَكْبَر).

## Qaradawi's Position

### Jihad as Self-Purification (Jihad al-Nafs)

Qaradawi places jihad at the heart of Islamic ethics and self-purification (تَزْكِيَة). In his discussion of the higher objectives of Islamic ethics, he writes that in order to succeed and attain salvation, "people have to keep watching their inner selves and strive in jihad against desires, for the evil powers encourage us to do ill deeds until the evil side takes over and completely covers the good core." ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-01-03.txt]

This inward struggle is the primary arena of jihad. Qaradawi cites Imam Ali's designation of this inner striving as the "greater jihad" and the Prophetic hadith: "A mujahid, someone who fights in Allah's cause, is one who strives against his own inner self" (رواه البخاري). The Prophet (PBUH) further illustrates this principle: "A strong man is not someone who is good at wrestling, but someone who controls himself when angry" (متفق عليه). ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-03-06.txt]

### The Greater and Lesser Jihad

Qaradawi affirms the classical distinction between the "greater jihad" (الجِهَاد الأَكْبَر) — the struggle against one's own desires and temptations — and the "lesser jihad" (الجِهَاد الأَصْغَر) of armed combat. He states directly: "This inner struggle is in fact more difficult than fighting enemies. This is why it is often described in the tradition as the 'greater jihad'." ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-02.txt]

Allah promises guidance to those who engage in this inner struggle: "Those who strive for Us — We will surely guide them to Our ways. And indeed, Allah is with the doers of good" (Holy Qur'an, 29:69). ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-02.txt]

### Jihad as Care for Parents

Qaradawi highlights a striking Prophetic teaching that reorients jihad toward personal moral duty. A man came to the Prophet (PBUH) asking permission to join battle. The Prophet asked: "Are your parents alive?" The man said yes. The Prophet said: "Do your jihad by taking care of them" — فَفِيهِمَا فَجَاهِد. This narration, recorded by Al-Bukhari, redefines the scope of jihad to include devoted service to one's parents as a form of striving in Allah's path. ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

In Muslim's version, a man came to swear allegiance "for migration and jihad seeking reward only from Allah." The Prophet asked if his parents were alive, and upon learning both were alive, said: "Go back to your parents and treat them kindly." Another narration confirms: a man who cared for his mother would receive "the reward of a fighter and a pilgrim." ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

### Jihad in the Cause of Allah: Striving for Good

Qaradawi understands jihad in its broadest Qur'anic sense as striving for Allah with the effort that is due to Him. He cites the verse: "Oh you who have believed, bow and prostrate and worship your Lord and do good — that you may succeed. And strive for Allah with the striving due to Him" (Holy Qur'an, 22:77-78). This striving (جِهَاد) encompasses three relationships:

1. **Worship (Allah → servant):** "Bow and prostrate and worship your Lord" — the duty of devotion
2. **Social good (individual → society):** "Do good" — the duty of public benefit
3. **Resisting the enemies of faith (faithful → opposition):** "Strive for Allah with the striving due to Him" — the duty of defending truth ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-03-07.txt]

### Ethics of Warfare (Jihad al-Sayf)

Qaradawi devotes significant attention to the ethical constraints on armed jihad. He insists that war in Islam "has to be waged according to strict moral principles." War is permitted only:

- **Against tyrants and aggressors:** "Fight in the way of Allah those who fight you but do not transgress" (Qur'an, 2:190)
- **In the cause of justice:** "Those who believe fight in the cause of Allah" (Qur'an, 4:76)
- **To rescue the oppressed:** "What is [the matter] with you that you fight not in the cause of Allah and [for] the oppressed among men, women, and children" (Qur'an, 4:75)

Islamic rules of engagement expressly prohibit targeting non-combatants — women, children, the elderly, monks — and forbid mutilation, treachery, and destruction of property. The Prophet (PBUH) instructed: "Fight in the Name of Allah and in Allah's cause. Fight those who disbelieve in Allah, but do not be treacherous, mutilate, or kill a child" (رواه مسلم). ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

Qaradawi also references his own comprehensive work *Fiqh al-Jihad wa-Falsafatih* (فِقْه الجِهَاد وَفَلْسَفَتِه) regarding the treatment of prisoners of war, citing it for detailed rulings on the ethics of armed conflict. ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

### Jihad Against Desire and Temptation

Within the framework of moral development, Qaradawi presents jihad as an essential practice of self-regulation. The believer must "exercise their will by training their inner selves to resist temptation," which is what Imam Ali called the "greater jihad." Self-purification requires constant vigilance: "He has succeeded who purifies it, and he has failed who instills it [with corruption]" (Qur'an, 91:9-10). ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-03-06.txt]

Jihad against desire is also connected to the Qur'anic concept of success (فَلَاح): "Oh you who have believed, fear Allah and seek the means [of nearness] to Him and strive in His cause that you may succeed" (Qur'an, 5:35). Qaradawi understands this striving as encompassing both moral and devotional effort. ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-01-03.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

Qaradawi's position aligns with the established majority view across the four madhabs:

- **Greater vs. Lesser Jihad:** The distinction between jihad al-nafs (the greater jihad) and jihad al-sayf (the lesser jihad) is widely recognized in classical scholarship. Qaradawi cites Imam Ali and Prophetic hadith in support of this framework, consistent with the Shafi'i and Hanafi ethical traditions. Some Hadith scholars have questioned the authenticity of the specific narration calling inner struggle the "greater jihad," but Qaradawi treats the conceptual distinction as sound based on the totality of the evidence.

- **Ethics of War:** Qaradawi's strict rules of engagement — prohibition on killing non-combatants, prohibition on mutilation, duty to protect prisoners — mirror the classical fiqh positions found in all four schools. His reference to *Fiqh al-Jihad* signals his detailed engagement with the classical literature on the subject.

- **Jihad and Parental Duty:** The Prophetic narrations redirecting military jihad toward filial duty are recorded in the canonical collections (Al-Bukhari, Muslim) and are universally accepted by the madhabs as establishing that kindness to parents can carry the reward of armed jihad.

## Contemporary Relevance

Jihad is among the most widely misunderstood concepts in Islam, both by non-Muslims who reduce it to armed violence and by extremist groups who appropriate it to justify atrocities. Qaradawi's treatment directly addresses both distortions:

1. **Against reductionism:** By foregrounding the Prophetic hadith that the true mujahid is one who struggles against his own desires, and that caring for one's parents constitutes jihad, Qaradawi broadens the concept back to its Qur'anic scope — "strive for Allah with the striving due to Him" (22:78).

2. **Against extremism:** By detailing the strict ethical constraints on armed combat — prohibition on targeting non-combatants, prohibition on mutilation, obligation of mercy even toward enemies — Qaradawi demonstrates that legitimate armed jihad in Islam is a regulated, ethical practice, not a license for indiscriminate violence.

3. **Modern application:** Qaradawi's understanding of jihad as the threefold striving (worship, social good, resisting the enemies of faith) provides a framework for contemporary Muslims to locate their struggles — whether personal, communal, or intellectual — within the comprehensive Qur'anic vision of human effort in the service of Allah.
