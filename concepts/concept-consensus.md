# Consensus (إِجْمَاع)

**Arabic:** إِجْمَاع (*ijma'* — lit. "unanimity, agreement")
**Domain:** Usul al-Fiqh (Principles of Islamic Jurisprudence)

## Definition

Ijma' (consensus) is the third source of Islamic law after the Quran and the Sunnah. It refers to the agreement of the qualified Muslim scholars of a given age on a particular legal ruling. When the scholars of the Ummah (Muslim community) reach consensus on a matter of law, that ruling acquires authoritative and binding force, because the Prophet (peace be upon him) declared: "My Ummah does not agree on a falsehood." ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

Qaradawi treats ijma' as a definitive proof (*hujja qat'iyya*) in Islamic law — "ijma' is a proof in which there is no doubt" — while simultaneously cautioning that claims of consensus must be scrutinised, since a claimed ijma' may not be genuinely established if dissenting opinions from the salaf (early generations) are found. ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

## Qaradawi's Position

### Consensus as a Source of Legislation

Qaradawi affirms the classical Sunni position that ijma' is one of the four primary sources of Shariah alongside the Quran, Sunnah, and qiyas (analogical deduction). In *Fiqh al-Zakah*, he structures his legal arguments for the obligation of zakah on trade assets by citing all four sources in order: Quran, Sunnah, ijma', and analogy. He reports that "people of knowledge unanimously agreed that items intended for trade are zakatable once a full fiscal year passes," and this ijma' is transmitted by Ibn al-Mundhir and Abu 'Ubaid, traced back to the Companions including 'Umar, his son, Ibn 'Abbas, and the seven renowned jurists. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-04.txt]

### Ijma' and Moral Obligation

In *Ethics in Islam*, Qaradawi explains that consensus functions as a source of moral obligation when explicit texts (nass) from the Quran or Sunnah are absent. He cites Ibn Mas'ud's statement: "What the Muslim community agrees to see as good, Allah also sees as good, and what they agree to see as evil, Allah also sees it as evil." This is grounded in the Prophetic hadith: "My Ummah does not agree on a falsehood." ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

### Who Constitutes the Consensus

Qaradawi clarifies that consensus here "does not mean the consensus of the uneducated or the consensus of a particular social class or group. Consensus here means the consensus of scholars who are known for their knowledge, laudable ethics, love for what is good for Muslims, and more importantly for their fear of Allah." He emphasises that even if the majority of the community veer off the right path, "there will always be a small minority that would hold true to the truth," citing Ibn Mas'ud: "The community is all of those who agree on the truth, even if it was a single individual." ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

### The Four Sources Converge on Allah

Qaradawi argues that the four sources of moral judgment — Quran, Sunnah, consensus, and analogy — "are actually one source: Allah. Only Allah can determine what is permissible, forbidden, or commendable. The sources of moral obligation in Islam do not contradict each other, but support each other and gain their legitimacy from the same source, namely the Holy Qur'an." ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-03.txt]

### Claimed Consensus Must Be Verified

In *Approaching the Sunnah*, Qaradawi warns against invoking ijma' carelessly. Discussing the minority position on diya (bloodwit) for women, he notes that "they rest their case on an appeal to ijma' or consensus. Now ijma' is a proof in which there is no doubt. But in this instance ijma' is not established. For people have narrated that on this subject there is a different opinion from two of the scholars of the salaf." This demonstrates Qaradawi's principle that ijma' is binding only when genuinely established — a claimed consensus collapses upon discovery of dissent from qualified early authorities. ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

### Ijma' as a Criterion for Rejecting Fabricated Hadiths

Qaradawi holds that a report contradictory to "the definitive ijma' (consensus)" is a clear indicator of fabrication, just as contradiction to the definitive Quran or mutawatir Sunnah is. A fabricated hadith may be identified when "the report is contradictory to the definitive proofs of the Book or the Sunnah known as mutawatir, or to the definitive ijma' (consensus), and there is no possibility of reconciling the contradiction between the two." ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

### Ijma' as Establishment of Legal Categories

Qaradawi notes that the classification of deeds as mandub (recommended) or makruh (reprehensible) requires that "it had already been established, by a text [of Quran or Sunnah] or by ijma' [consensus], that God loved it or that God abhorred it." Ijma' thus serves alongside textual proof as a means of establishing legal categories, not merely confirming what is already known. ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

### Unanimity of All Jurists on the Sunnah

Qaradawi also uses consensus to affirm the authority of the Sunnah itself: "We can state with complete assurance that all the jurists of the Muslims — from different schools, from diverse cities, whose doctrine survives or has been cut off, is followed or is not followed — have been in agreement on acceptance of the Sunnah and the appeal to it in making rulings." Both ahl al-hadith and ahl al-ra'y differed only on criteria for accepting hadiths, not on the fundamental principle that the Sunnah is a source. ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

### Points of Agreement

Qaradawi's position on ijma' aligns with the mainstream Sunni consensus across all four madhhabs:

- **Ijma' is a binding proof** — agreed by Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali schools.
- **Ijma' ranks third** after Quran and Sunnah as a source of law.
- **Ijma' is definitive (*qat'i*)** when genuinely established — no later ruling may overturn it.
- **Claimed ijma' must be verified** — the discovery of a dissenting qualified early authority invalidates the claim.

### Points of Nuance

- **Scope of consensus** — Qaradawi emphasises that consensus belongs to qualified scholars, not the uneducated majority, echoing the Maliki and Shafi'i positions over more expansive understandings.
- **Qualitative over numerical** — his citation of Ibn Mas'ud ("even if it was a single individual") reflects the position of some Hanbali scholars that truth is not determined by majority count but by scholarly competence and alignment with textual evidence.
- **Complementarity** — Qaradawi's assertion that the four sources "are actually one source: Allah" is a philosophical framing shared by al-Shafi'i in his *Risala*, but articulated in an ethical rather than strictly usuli context.

## Contemporary Relevance

Qaradawi's treatment of ijma' carries several implications for contemporary Islamic thought:

1. **Verification over declaration** — In an age of rapid fatwas, his insistence that claimed consensus must be scrutinised against the record of early dissenting opinions is a corrective to hasty invocations of ijma'.
2. **Scholarly quality over mass opinion** — His definition restricts authoritative consensus to qualified scholars of known probity, not popular opinion or unqualified majorities.
3. **Inter-madhhab convergence** — By citing examples where ahl al-hadith and ahl al-ra'y agreed on the principle of Sunnah's authority, Qaradawi shows that genuine ijma' can bridge methodological divides.
4. **Ethical application** — In the field of Islamic ethics, consensus fills gaps where explicit texts are silent, allowing the tradition to respond to novel moral questions without departing from its textual foundations.
