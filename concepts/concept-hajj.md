# Hajj / Pilgrimage (حَجّ)

**Arabic:** حَجّ (*ḥajj*)
**Related term:** عُمْرَة (*ʿumrah*) — the lesser pilgrimage
**Domain:** Fiqh al-ʿIbādāt (Worship)

## Definition

**Ḥajj** (حَجّ) is the pilgrimage to Makkah, constituting the **Fifth Pillar of Islam**. It is an obligatory act of worship upon every Muslim who possesses the physical and financial ability (*istiṭāʿah*) to undertake the journey at least once in their lifetime. The term derives from the root ح-ج-ج, meaning "to intend a journey to a venerable place" or "to set out for a great object."

The ḥajj encompasses a series of ritual acts performed during the month of Dhū al-Ḥijjah, including entering the state of *iḥrām* (consecration), *ṭawāf* (circumambulation of the Kaʿbah), *saʿy* (running between Ṣafā and Marwah), standing at ʿArafāt, and the *ramy* (stoning of the pillars). The ʿumrah (lesser pilgrimage) shares some rites with ḥajj — *iḥrām*, *ṭawāf*, *saʿy*, and shaving/trimming — but can be performed at any time of year.

## Qaradawi's Treatment

### 1. Pilgrimage as an Act of Expiation and Moral Purification

Qaradawi, drawing on the broader Islamic tradition, treats ḥajj as one of the principal acts of expiation (*kaffārāt*) alongside prayer, fasting, and charity. In *Approaching the Sunnah*, he lists the forms of expiation appointed by God for sins:

> "He has appointed for the sins acts of expiation — many of the five prayers, and the Friday prayer, and the fasting in Ramadan, and the vigil therein, and different forms of charitable acts, and the pilgrimage, both ḥajj and ʿumrah, and the recital and remembrance of His Names, and the utterance of the formula of *tawḥīd* and the magnificat, and the praise of Him, and other kinds of remembrance and supplication. All of these serve to lessen the [weight of sins]." ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-03.txt]

This underscores the ḥajj's moral-spiritual function: like ṣalāh and ṣawm, pilgrimage is not merely a ritual but a means of moral purification and spiritual renewal.

### 2. Types of Ḥajj and the Sacrificial Rite

Qaradawi's *Economic Security in Islam* discusses the sacrificial rite at the time of ḥajj in the context of Islamic social welfare. The sacrifice (*udhiyah / qurbān*) serves three purposes during pilgrimage:

1. **As atonement (*kaffārah*)** for doing any prohibited act while in the state of *iḥrām*.
2. **For Ḥajj al-Tamattuʿ** — performing ḥajj and ʿumrah with two separate *iḥrāms*.
3. **For Ḥajj al-Qirān** — performing ḥajj and ʿumrah with the same *iḥrām*.

The Qur'an addresses the prohibition against hunting during *iḥrām* and the compensatory sacrifice required:

> "O ye who believe! Kill no wild game while ye are on the pilgrimage. Whoso of you killeth it of set purpose he shall pay its forfeit in the equivalent of that which he hath killed, of domestic animals, the judge to be two men among you known for justice, (the forfeit) to be brought as an offering to the Kaʿbah; or, for expiation, he shall feed poor persons…" (Qurʾān, 5:95) ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-05.txt]

And regarding the voluntary gift for those performing *tamattuʿ*:

> "…then whosoever contenteth himself with the Visit for the Pilgrimage (shall give) such gifts as can be had with ease…" (Qurʾān, 2:196) ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-05.txt]

Qaradawi emphasises that **the aim of the sacrificial rite is to provide meat to the poor** — linking it directly to the social welfare dimension of Islamic worship. He states: "its importance is known to the person having the knowledge of Sharīʿah who does not recognize to pay the cost of sacrificial animal, or its double, as an alms." The Qur'an commands the pilgrim: "Then eat thereof and feed therewith the poor unfortunate" (22:28) and "Then when their flanks fall (dead), eat thereof and feed the beggar and the suppliant" (22:36). ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-05.txt]

### 3. The Ḥajj as a Sign of Islam's Security and Victory

In *Auspices of the Ultimate Victory of Islam* and *Economic Security in Islam*, Qaradawi cites the famous Prophetic hadith narrated by ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim as a sign (*bishārah*) of Islam's triumph:

> The Prophet (peace and blessings of Allah be upon him) said: "If you live long, you will see a lonely woman travelling from Ḥīrah to Makkah to circumambulate the Kaʿbah without any fear save that of God." In another version: "A time will come when a camel would march to Makkah without any guard."

Qaradawi cites this as both a prophecy fulfilled and a demonstration that Islam's social order — founded on justice, zakat, and mutual security — creates the conditions where even the most vulnerable can travel safely to perform ḥajj. The ḥajj thus becomes a barometer of the Islamic society's moral and security health. ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-07.txt]

### 4. Women and Ḥajj — Travel Without a *Maḥram*

In *Approaching the Sunnah*, Qaradawi addresses a significant fiqh question: whether a woman may perform ḥajj without a *maḥram* (male guardian) accompanying her. He notes that some imams permitted a woman to perform ḥajj without a *maḥram* if she was with trustworthy women or in trusted company. He cites the precedent of ʿĀʾishah, who performed ḥajj and *ṭawāf* as one of the "mothers of the believers" during the rule of ʿUmar — without a single *maḥram* present. Rather, ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf accompanied them, as reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī.

> "Some people say: A single trustworthy woman is enough as a traveling companion. Others say: She may travel alone if the road is safe. The followers of the Shāfiʿī school have pronounced this view correct for traveling for ḥajj and ʿumrah. Other Shāfiʿīs include any journey in this permission, not just for pilgrimage." ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-03.txt]

Qaradawi's approach here is characteristic: he anchors fiqh rulings in their underlying rationale (*ʿillah*). When travel was dangerous, the *maḥram* condition served safety; when the Prophetic hadith of ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim foretells a time of safe travel, the condition's rationale diminishes, and permissibility expands. He also invokes Ibn Ḥazm, who "proceeded in accordance with this view" — citing it as an example of how understanding the *ʿillah* behind Prophetic rulings yields different conclusions than literal adherence to the text.

### 5. The Prophet's Circumambulation Amid Idolatry — Strategic Patience

Qaradawi draws a striking parallel between the Prophet's strategic patience in Makkah and the Muslim's duty of wisdom in confronting wrongdoing:

> "Certainly the Prophet was patient for thirteen years in Makkah against the idols and their worshippers. He used to pray in the Masjid al-Ḥarām, and circumambulate the Kaʿbah while there were three hundred and sixty idols in it. He circumambulated it with the delegation of his Companions in the seventh year of the Hijrah in the ʿumrat al-qadāʾ, and he saw the idols but did not touch them until the right time came on the day of the Great Conquest — the conquest of Makkah — and he destroyed them." ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

This passage illustrates that the Prophet performed *ṭawāf* and ʿumrah within a space still filled with idols, demonstrating the jurisprudential principle that "if the elimination of a wrong leads on to a greater wrong, silence is obligatory upon one until the conditions change." The ḥajj and ʿumrah thus become not only acts of worship but moments of strategic endurance and trust in divine timing.

### 6. The Pilgrimage and Standards of Measure

In *Fiqh al-Zakah*, Qaradawi discusses the *ṣāʿ* (a unit of measure essential to calculating zakat on agricultural produce) and tells the story of Abū Yūsuf, who changed his legal position after visiting Madinah on *ḥajj*:

> "We went to visit Abū Yūsuf upon his return from pilgrimage. He told us, 'I want to inform you of a matter that worried me for some time until I went to Madinah. There, I asked about the *ṣāʿ* and they answered that the *ṣāʿ* they have today is the same *ṣāʿ* used by the Messenger of God (p).'" ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-05.txt]

The Prophetic hadith "The measures are the measures of Madinah, and the weights are the weights of Makkah" establishes the significance of both cities in the Islamic metrological tradition — Makkah as a trading centre for weights, Madinah as an agricultural centre for volumetric measures. The *ṣāʿ* is also needed "in determining the kaffārah for a broken oath, and the recompense in pilgrimage." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-05.txt]

### 7. The Pilgrimage in Zakah Jurisprudence

Qaradawi discusses ḥajj in the context of zakah debt deduction rules. Debts owed to God — including **the cost of making pilgrimage** — are not deductible from zakatable wealth. This means a Muslim cannot reduce their zakah obligation by claiming they are setting aside funds for ḥajj. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-03.txt]

He also uses the pilgrimage as an analogy in the discussion of ʿushr (agricultural tithe) and kharāj (land tax): just as a pilgrim in *iḥrām* who kills an animal owned by another person must compensate the owner *in addition to* the *kaffārah* required for violating *iḥrām* — demonstrating that two obligations arising from different causes can coexist on the same subject — so too can ʿushr and kharāj both be levied on the same land. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-05.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

### Ḥanafī School
- Abū Yūsuf changed his position on the *ṣāʿ* after visiting Madinah for pilgrimage, adopting the Ḥijāzī position over Abū Ḥanīfah's. This demonstrates that even within a single madhhab, pilgrimage to the centres of sacred knowledge could shift legal conclusions. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-05.txt]

### Shāfiʿī School
- Some Shāfiʿīs permit women to travel for ḥajj and ʿumrah without a *maḥram* when the road is safe; others extend the permission to any journey. This is based on the hadith of ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim and the principle of changing conditions (*taghayyur al-aḥwāl*). ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-03.txt]

### Ibn Ḥazm (Zāhirī School)
- Permits a woman to travel alone if the road is safe, based on the literal wording of the Prophetic hadith about the woman travelling from Ḥīrah to the Kaʿbah. ^[raw/extracted/approaching-the-sunnah/ch-03.txt]

### Qaradawi's Distinctive Contributions
- Connects the ḥajj sacrifice directly to **social welfare and poverty alleviation**, not just ritual compliance — consistent with his broader approach of showing the *maqāṣid* (higher objectives) behind acts of worship.
- Applies the **principle of changing conditions**: the *maḥram* requirement for women's travel is tied to safety, not absolute prohibition; modern transportation changes the calculus.
- Uses the pilgrimage analogy (*iḥrām* violations incurring dual obligations) to clarify the coexistence of ʿushr and kharāj on agricultural land.
- Elevates the ḥajj as a **sign of civilisational security**: a society where the vulnerable can travel safely to Makkah is one in which Islam's social system is functioning as intended.

## Contemporary Relevance

- **Women's autonomous pilgrimage:** Qaradawi's position that safe travel conditions relax the *maḥram* requirement is directly applicable to the modern era, where air travel and group pilgrimage packages have transformed the safety calculus. This speaks to ongoing debates among Muslim communities worldwide.
- **Social welfare through sacrifice:** The ḥajj and ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā sacrificial meat distribution system, properly implemented, constitutes a massive annual redistribution of protein to the poor — a mechanism Qaradawi consistently situates within the broader Islamic economic justice framework alongside [[concept-zakat|Zakat (زَكَاة)]].
- **Pilgrimage as civilisational barometer:** The Prophetic vision of safe solo travel to Makkah is read by Qaradawi not only as a miracle but as a standard against which Muslim societies should measure their commitment to justice, security, and mutual care.
- **Inter-madhhab ijtihād:** The story of Abū Yūsuf modifying his ruling after visiting Madinah on pilgrimage illustrates the principle that jurists must remain open to revising their positions when confronted with stronger evidence — a core component of Qaradawi's methodology of *taḥarrī* (seeking the strongest position).
- **Strategic patience:** The Prophet's ʿumrah amidst 360 idols models a principled pragmatism — participating in righteous worship even in an imperfect environment, while working toward reform when conditions permit.
