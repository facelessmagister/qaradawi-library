# Zakat (زَكَاة)

## Definition

**Zakat** (زَكَاة) — literally "purification, growth, blessing" — is the obligatory alms-tax levied on eligible Muslim wealth at prescribed rates. It constitutes the **Third Pillar of Islam** and is one of the four forms of worship alongside *ṣalāh* (prayer), *ṣawm* (fasting), and *ḥajj* (pilgrimage). The Qur'an pairs zakat with prayer (*ṣalāh*) in dozens of verses, signalling that the two are inseparable pillars of the faith.

Zakat is a right (*ḥaqq*) of the poor defined by God within the wealth of the rich — "And in their wealth there is a right of the needy, he who asks, and he who is deprived" (Q 51:19). Its primary purpose is the purification of the giver's wealth and soul and the eradication of poverty from the Muslim community.

## Qaradawi's Position

Dr. Yusuf al-Qaradawi's *Fiqh al-Zakah* is widely regarded as the most comprehensive contemporary work on the subject. His key positions include:

1. **Zakat applies to all categories of wealth**, not merely the classical four (gold/silver, livestock, crops, trade goods). Qaradawi extends zakatability to salary income, professional earnings, industrial production, stocks, bonds, and retirement savings — anything that meets the criteria of *māl* (owned material asset), *niṣāb* (minimum threshold), and *ḥawl* (lapse of a year, except for agricultural produce).

2. **The definition of *māl* (wealth/asset) must be material and acquirable.** Following the Ḥanafī position, Qaradawi holds that *māl* means material things that can be obtained, owned, and saved for use at the time of need. Services, utilities, and rights (such as custodianship) are not *māl* and therefore not zakatable. Zakah "can only be fulfilled by giving the recipient a material thing that has value." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

3. **Unlawful wealth is not zakatable.** Assets acquired by theft, bribery, interest (*ribā*), or oppression cannot be zakated because the possessor lacks legitimate ownership. "God does not accept ṣadaqah from stolen property." ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

4. **Doubtful/dead debts are not zakatable** until recovered; once recovered, they are treated as newly earned assets with zakah due for one year only — agreeing with Abū Ḥanīfah on the substance while preferring a single-year payment upon recovery. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

5. **Publicly owned assets and public trusts are not zakatable**, while private trusts (e.g., children's trusts) are zakatable because exclusive benefit accrues to identifiable beneficiaries. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

6. **Growth (*namā')** — actual or potential — is a condition for zakatability. Wealth must either be generating profit or capable of generating it. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

## Evidence Base

### Qur'anic Verses

- **"Out of their wealth take ṣadaqah so by it you purify and sanctify them"** (Q 9:103) — the foundational verse establishing zakat as purification and an obligation.
- **"And in their wealths and properties is the right of the poor, he who asks, and he who is deprived"** (Q 51:19) — establishes the poor's right in the wealth of the rich.
- **"And there are those who hoard gold and silver and spend it not in the way of God. Announce unto them a most grievous penalty"** (Q 9:34) — zakat on gold and silver (monetary wealth).
- **"Eat of their fruit in their season, but render the dues that are proper on the day that the harvest is gathered"** (Q 6:141) — zakat on crops and fruits.
- **"O ye who believe, give of the good things which ye have earned"** (Q 2:267) — zakat on trade earnings.
- **"And of that which We have produced for you from the earth"** (Q 2:267) — zakat on mineral wealth.
- **"But if they repent and establish Prayer and pay the Poor-due, then leave their way free"** (Q 9:5) and **"But if they repent and establish Prayer and pay the Poor-due, then are they your brethren in religion"** (Q 9:11) — zakat paired with prayer as a condition of belonging to the Muslim community. ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]

### Prophetic Hadiths

- **"Islam is based on five things…"** — including the payment of zakat (Bukhari & Muslim). ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]
- The Prophet dispatched Mu'adh to Yemen with the instruction: **"Collect the Poor-due from the rich and distribute it among the needy and the handicapped."** ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]
- **"Poor-due makes to stretch a wide gulf between Islam and infidelity, between Faith and hypocrisy and between piety and wretchedness."** (Sahih al-Bukhari) ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]
- On agricultural zakat: **"If the land is cultivated with rain water, the rate will be only one-tenth of produce, and if the means of irrigation are river water or canal water or wells the rate will be one-twentieth."** ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

### Ḥanafī School
- Defines *māl* as anything that can be acquired and usually used, requiring both possibility of acquisition and general usability. Services and rights are excluded.
- Doubtful/dead debts: not zakatable; once recovered, treated as a new asset.
- Fiqh al-Zakah aligns with the Ḥanafī definition of *māl* and their position on dead debts.

### Mālikī School
- Considers services and utilities as *māl*, broadening the scope beyond material things.
- Imām Mālik makes no variation in livestock zakat whether animals are grazed free or fed by the owner. ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]

### Shāfi'ī School
- Joins the Mālikīs in considering services as *māl*.
- Qaradawi disagrees with this broadening, arguing that zakah cannot be collected from utilities and distributed to the deserving categories — only material assets fulfill the purpose. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

### Ḥanbalī School
- Also considers services as *māl*.
- Some Companions and their followers declared zakat obligatory on horses; Abū Ḥanīfah agreed, while other schools did not. ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]

### Zāhirī School (Ibn Ḥazm)
- Takes the strictest position: neither debtor nor creditor pays zakat on debts, since each party's ownership is incomplete.
- Qaradawi considers the Zāhirī stance too restrictive but notes the condition of complete ownership.

### Qaradawi's Distinctive Views
- Agrees with Ḥanafīs on the material definition of *māl* and on the unzakatability of dead debts.
- Differs from Abū ʿUbayd on doubtful debts, siding with Abū Ḥanīfah that ownership must be complete for zakatability.
- Extends zakatability beyond classical categories to modern financial instruments (salaries, stocks, retirement funds) — a position that goes beyond all four madhhabs but is grounded in the same principles.

## Conditions for Zakatability

Qaradawi identifies the following conditions for wealth to be subject to zakat:

| # | Condition | Explanation |
|---|-----------|-------------|
| 1 | **Undivided and absolute right of ownership** (*milk kāmil*) | The owner must have full control and authority of disposition. Stolen property, merchandise not yet received, and assets held as lien are excluded. ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt] |
| 2 | **Growth or potential for growth** (*namā'*) | Wealth must be actually growing (business, trade) or have the latent capacity to grow (gold, silver, cash). ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt] |
| 3 | **Reaching the minimum threshold** (*niṣāb*) | Zakat is not due on trivial amounts; there is a minimum threshold specific to each category of wealth. |
| 4 | **Lapse of one year** (*ḥawl*) | Zakat is due after a lunar year of ownership, except for agricultural produce (due at harvest) and rikaz/mineral wealth (due upon discovery). |

## Zakat al-Fitr

Zakat al-Fitr (also called *Fiṭrana*) is a distinct form of zakat:

- **Levied on individuals**, not on wealth — every Muslim (male or female, free or slave, rich or poor) is obligated, provided one has more than a day's sustenance on the day of *ʿĪd al-Fiṭr*.
- **To be paid before *ʿĪd* prayer** — specifically, on the completion of Ramadan fasts.
- **Purposes:** (1) compensation for shortcomings during Ramadan fasts, and (2) enabling the poor to enjoy the *ʿĪd* festival.
- **Amount:** one *ṣā'* (approx. 2.25 seers) of dates, wheat, or the local staple; Imām Abū Ḥanīfah permits cash equivalent.
- **Head of household** pays on behalf of dependents. ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]

## Contemporary Relevance

### Economic Justice
Zakat is an institutional mechanism for wealth redistribution, not merely voluntary charity. It acknowledges that wealth is held in trust from God — **"And spend out of the sustenance whereof He has made you vicegerent"** (Q 4:37) — and that the poor have an enforceable right (*ḥaqq*) in the wealth of the rich.

### Poverty Eradication
The Prophet's instruction to Mu'adh was to "collect from the rich and distribute to the needy," targeting the permanent elimination of poverty, not its mere alleviation. Qaradawi argues that when the full zakat system is implemented — including its extension to modern asset classes — it can substantially eradicate absolute poverty in Muslim societies.

### Separation from Taxation
Zakat is a religious obligation with spiritual consequences (it "stretches a wide gulf between Islam and infidelity"), not a secular tax. Withholding it is an act of apostasy according to the practice of Abū Bakr, who waged war against those who differentiated between prayer and zakat.

### Modern Asset Classes
Qaradawi extends zakatability to salary income, professional fees, industrial output, stock portfolios, and retirement savings — applying the same principles (complete ownership, growth/potential, *niṣāb*, *ḥawl*) to contemporary financial instruments that the classical fuqahā' applied to camels and date orchards.

### Public vs. Private Ownership
Publicly owned assets (state revenues, public trusts for mosques, schools, and the poor) are not zakatable. Private trusts that benefit identifiable beneficiaries (e.g., children's trusts) are zakatable. This distinction has important implications for state-owned enterprises and endowments (*awqāf*).
