# Marriage (نِكَاح)

## Definition

**Marriage** (*nikah*, نِكَاح) in Islam is the solemn contract (*'aqd*) between a man and a woman that establishes a lawful family unit. It is not merely a civil arrangement but a covenant blessed by God — the Qur'an calls it a "solemn oath" (*mīthāqan ghalīẓan*, Q 4:21). Unlike some religious traditions that treat celibacy as a higher spiritual calling, Islam considers marriage a *sunnah* (prophetic tradition) and near-obligation for those who can afford it. The Prophet (PBUH) declared: "I marry women too. He who turns away from my Sunnah has no relation with me." (Narrated by Anas, Al-Bukhari 5063; Muslim 1401) ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

The term *nikah* encompasses the entire institution: the consent of the woman (and her *walī* / guardian), the payment of *mahr* (dowry/gift from groom to bride), the witnessing of at least two Muslim witnesses, and the public announcement of the union.

## Qaradawi's Position

Dr. Yusuf al-Qaradawi treats marriage primarily through the lens of **Islamic ethics** and the **objectives of the Sharī'ah** (*maqāṣid al-sharī'ah*). His key positions include:

### 1. Marriage Is the Foundation of Social Structure

Islam views people as social beings who cannot survive in isolation. Marriage is the foundation upon which the entire social structure is built. It must be established on solid grounds: mutual acceptance, a witnessed contract, and a *mahr* paid by the man to the woman as a gift: "And give the women [upon marriage] their [bridal] gifts graciously" (Q 4:4). ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

### 2. Marriage Is Encouraged, Celibacy Is Rejected

Islam categorically forbids adultery while actively encouraging legal marriage. The tradition "There is no monasticism in Islam" is cited, and Qaradawi explains that Islam rejects asceticism, mortification of the flesh, and celibacy. The Prophet (PBUH) corrected followers who vowed extreme ascetic practices, stating: "Your body has its rights, your wife has her rights, and your guest has his rights. Give each their rights." (Narrated by Amr Ibn Al-As, Al-Bukhari 1975; Muslim 1159) ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

### 3. Marriage Must Be Affordable — Excessive Dowries Are Cultural Violations

Qaradawi strongly criticises cultural practices that make marriage prohibitively expensive. Islam recommends that dowries be kept within reason. The Prophet (PBUH) said: "The best of dowries are the simplest ones," and in another version: "The best of marriages are the simplest ones." When Ali Ibn Abi Talib proposed to Fatima and was asked what he possessed, he replied that he had nothing but a shield; the Prophet said: "Then, give it to her." (Abu Dawud 2125; Al-Nassa'i 3375) ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

Qaradawi specifically calls out dowry inflation in South Asia (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh) where the bride's father pays the groom — the reverse of the Islamic *mahr* — and in Egypt where the bride's family is expected to furnish the couple's home luxuriously. These cultural patterns "make it difficult for young people to get married" and push the average marriage age upward, contravening the Prophetic directive. ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

### 4. Spouse Selection: Religion and Character Over Wealth and Beauty

Islam offers clear guidance on the qualities to seek in a spouse. The Prophet (PBUH) said: "If a man with a good moral character and religious commitment comes to propose, then marry [your daughter] to him, for if you do not do that, there will be social strife and widespread corruption." (Al-Tirmidhi 1084; Ibn Majah 1967) He also said: "A woman is married for four reasons: her wealth, her social status, her beauty, and her religion. Marry the religious one; otherwise, you will regret it." (Al-Bukhari 5090; Muslim 1466) ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

### 5. Engagement Before Marriage — With Limits

Qaradawi affirms that engagement (*khiṭbah*) is a recognized period for the couple to get to know each other. The Prophet encouraged seeing one's prospective spouse: when Al-Mughirah Ibn Shu'bah proposed, the Prophet said: "Look at her, for indeed that is more likely to make things better between the two of you." (Ahmed 18137; Al-Tirmidhi 2087) However, engaged couples are not married, and it is not recommended that they spend time alone, since engagements do not always end in marriage. The marriage contract is a "solemn oath" (*mīthāqan ghalīẓan*) and cannot be assumed before its formal conclusion. ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

### 6. Marriage Poverty Concerns — Zakat and Community Support

In *Economic Security in Islam*, Qaradawi discusses how poverty stands in the way of marriage and dowry. He cites the Qur'anic directive: "And let those who find not the wherewithal for marriage keep themselves chaste, until Allah gives them means out of His Grace" (Q 24:33), while also pointing to the Qur'an's counter-instruction for the community: "And marry such of you as are solitary and the pious of your slaves and maid-servants. If they be poor, Allah will enrich them of His bounty" (Q 24:32). The community bears responsibility to facilitate marriage for those who cannot afford it. ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-01.txt]

Zakat funds can be disbursed for helping the poor marry. Qaradawi cites the precedent of Umar ibn Al-Khattab arranging his son's marriage and paying from the Public Treasury, and the general principle that those desiring marriage but unable to meet expenses qualify as recipients of zakat. ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]

### 7. Marital Relations: Affection, Mercy, and Mutual Rights

Marriage in Islam is built on *mawaddah* (affection) and *raḥmah* (mercy), as stated in Q 30:21. Qaradawi notes that the Qur'an uses the second-person plural pronoun in "He placed between you affection and mercy" rather than the dual, because "the affection and mercy move from the nucleus of the married couple to the rest of their family and across society." ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

The Qur'an commands men to be kind to women: "O you who have believed, it is not lawful for you to inherit women by compulsion. And do not make difficulties for them in order to take [back] part of what you gave them unless they commit a clear immorality. And live with them in kindness, for if you dislike them — perhaps you dislike a thing and Allah makes therein much good" (Q 4:19). ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

Sexual intimacy is a mutual right of both spouses, except during menstruation and postpartum bleeding (Q 2:222-223). The Prophet (PBUH) also taught the supplication before intimacy: "In the name of Allah. O Allah! Protect us from Satan and also protect what you bestow upon us [the coming offspring] from Satan." (Al-Bukhari 141; Muslim 1434) ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

### 8. Marriage Forbidden with Certain Categories

Qaradawi outlines the *maḥārim* (prohibited marriage partners) in detail:

- **Consanguine relatives**: mothers, daughters, sisters, paternal aunts, maternal aunts, nieces (Q 4:23).
- **Affinal relatives**: wives' mothers, step-daughters, daughters-in-law (Q 4:22-23).
- **Foster relatives**: wet nurses, sisters through nursing.
- **Polytheists and atheists**: "Do not marry polytheistic women until they believe" (Q 2:221).
- **Already-married women**: unless after divorce and completion of the waiting period (*'iddah*) (Q 4:24).
- **Two sisters simultaneously**: (Q 4:23).
- **Adulterers/adulteresses**: "The adulterer shall not marry save an adulteress or an idolatress" (Q 24:3).

Muslim men are however permitted to marry chaste women from the People of the Book (Christians and Jews) (Q 5:5). ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

### 9. Marriage Creates Kinship Beyond the Couple

Marriage is not only a relationship between two individuals — it binds entire families together: "It is He who has created from water mankind and made them related through lineage and marriage" (Q 25:54). This makes marriage a vehicle for social cohesion. ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

### 10. Marriage and the Prohibition of Fornication

To protect the sanctity of marriage, Islam prohibits fornication (*zinā*) and all forms of extramarital sexual relations. Fornication leads to "the spread of many social evils." At the same time, Islam makes marriage accessible by simplifying dowries and removing harmful cultural stipulations, so that people are not driven toward sin by economic barriers. ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt]

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

### Ḥanafī School
- Permits marriage to People of the Book women, same as Qaradawi's position.
- Emphasises the *mahr* as a mandatory gift; the minimum is set at ten dirhams.
- Recognises *khiṭbah* (engagement) as a promise, not a binding contract, aligning with Qaradawi's view.

### Mālikī School
- Strongly emphasises the *walī*s (guardian's) role in the contract; a marriage without a *walī* is invalid.
- Permits viewing the prospective spouse, same as the Prophetic tradition Qaradawi cites.
- Discourages excessive *mahr* and considers the *mahr al-mithl* (dowry of equals) as a benchmark.

### Shāfi'ī School
- Requires two witnesses for a valid contract, which Qaradawi also affirms.
- Permits marriage to Kitābiyy women, though some Shāfi'ī jurists advise against it for political/social concerns.
- Considers *mahr* obligatory but allows any amount mutually agreed.

### Ḥanbalī School
- Takes the strictest position on *maḥārim* and prohibits marriage to apostates and polytheists more firmly.
- Encourages early marriage and discourages delays; aligns with Qaradawi's view against cultural barriers.
- Considers marriage obligatory for anyone who fears *zinā* (fornication).

### Key Point of Agreement
All four Sunnī schools agree that marriage is a solemn covenant (*'aqd*), that *mahr* is the right of the bride, that at least two witnesses are required, and that the Prophet's sunnah affirms the permissibility and encouragement of marriage. Qaradawi's positions largely align with classical consensus, with his distinctive contribution being the **ethical and social analysis** of how cultural corruption of *mahr* and marriage customs obstructs the Prophetic sunnah.

## Contemporary Relevance

Qaradawi addresses several contemporary issues surrounding marriage:

1. **Excessive dowry inflation and extravagant weddings** — Cultural practices (particularly in South Asia and the Arab world) that reverse the Islamic direction of *mahr* (bride-price to groom's family instead of groom to bride) and impose unjust financial burdens that delay or prevent marriage.

2. **Rising age of marriage** — Cultural and economic barriers push marriage to later ages, increasing the risk of fornication. The Prophetic directive is to facilitate, not obstruct, marriage.

3. **Poverty as a barrier to marriage** — The Qur'an promises enrichment for those who marry in faith (Q 24:32), and the Muslim community must support those who cannot afford marriage — including through zakat disbursement.

4. **Spouse selection criteria** — Modern materialism shifts preference toward wealth, status, and beauty; Qaradawi reasserts the Prophetic priority on religion and character.

5. **Engagement practices** — Modern relaxed norms around unsupervised engagement periods contravene Prophetic guidance against *khalwah* (seclusion) before the contract.

6. **Children and anti-natalism** — Islam sees children as God's provision, not a financial burden, and condemns both pre-Islamic female infanticide and modern anti-natalist attitudes.
