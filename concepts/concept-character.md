# Character / Akhlaq (أَخْلَاق)

**Arabic:** أَخْلَاق (plural of خُلُق)
**Transliteration:** Akhlaq
**Domain:** Islamic Ethics (الأخلاق الإسلامية)

## Definition

*Akhlaq* (أَخْلَاق) is the Arabic term for "ethics" or "moral character" — the plural of *khuluq* (خُلُق), meaning an individual's moral disposition. The singular form appears in the Quran as a descriptor of the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH): "And indeed, you are of a great moral character" (Quran 68:4).

Qaradawi adopts a definition rooted in the hadith: "Nothing is heavier on the scales of deeds than good morals" (narrated via Abu al-Darda'). Accordingly, moral character (*khuluq*) is **the individual's inner self with its properties and traits, which can be good or evil**. Divine reward and punishment are tied directly to these properties and traits, not to physical features.

This definition synthesizes insights from several classical scholars:

- **Al-Mu'jam al-Wasit Dictionary**: "A set of stable personal traits that motivate actions, good or bad" — adopted by the Arabic Language Academy, drawing on Ibn Miskawayh (d. AH 421).
- **Abu al-Baqa' al-Kafawi**: "Moral character is a set of innate personal traits, such as gallantry and religiosity."
- **Al-Qamus al-Muhit** (and its revision in *Taj al-'Arus* by al-Zubaydi): "Innate personality traits with which people are created."
- **Al-Ghazali** (d. AH 505): Moral character is "a set of well-established personality traits that lead to actions without thinking or planning." If good, actions are lauded by both common sense and religion; if bad, actions are condemned by both.
- **Dr. Draz** (mid-20th century): Revised earlier definitions to emphasize that moral traits are specifically **willful powers** — distinct from intellectual or aesthetic capacities. Moral character is "a set of well-established willful powers that trigger good or evil choices."

## Linguistic Root and Significance

The word *khuluq* (خُلُق) shares a root with *khalq* (خَلْق), meaning "creation." This linguistic connection has been the subject of extensive scholarly discussion:

- **Abu Hayyan al-Tawhidi** (d. ~AH 350): "The word for moral character is derived from the word for creation, for it is impossible to change moral character, just as it is impossible to change Allah's creation. However, it is not wrong or futile to encourage people to improve their moral character; the value of good morals is unquestionable." He uses the analogy of a person with a dark complexion scrubbing with soap and water — not to become lighter, but to reach a purer state.

- **Al-Raghib al-Asfahani** (d. AH 502): "Moral character and creation are two sides of the same coin. Creation has to do with the physical features that can be perceived visually, whereas moral character has to do with personal qualities that cannot be perceived directly."

- **Al-Mutanabbi** (d. AH 354) encapsulated the connection between inner character and outward appearance: "What honor is in a young lad's handsome face / If his actions and manners show no grace?"

## Qaradawi's Position

Qaradawi takes *akhlaq* as the central organizing discipline of his work **Ethics in Islam**. His position can be summarized:

1. **Akhlaq is the core Islamic concern.** The Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) stated: "I have been sent to perfect good morals and conduct." This prophetic mission statement makes ethical perfection the ultimate aim of the message of Islam.

2. **Good character has the heaviest weight on the scales of deeds.** Multiple hadiths elevate akhlaq above ritual acts: "The most faithful are those with the best moral character," and "A person can reach the status of those who fast and spend the night praying by having a good moral character."

3. **Akhlaq encompasses both innate and acquired traits.** Qaradawi affirms the classical two-fold classification (see Types below) and holds that acquired traits, through effort and discipline, can become as ingrained as innate ones.

4. **Moral character is mutable, not fixed.** Following al-Ghazali and Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya, Qaradawi rejects the notion that akhlaq is as unchangeable as physical features: "How can anyone deny that human behavior can be modified and improved? Animals can learn to behave better, and surely humans can too."

## Evidence Base

### Quranic Verses

- **Quran 68:4**: "And indeed, you are of a great moral character" (*wa-innaka la-'ala khuluqin 'azim*) — the foundational verse establishing *khuluq* as a divinely praised quality of the Prophet.
- **Quran 5:4**: "Say, 'Lawful for you are [all] good foods and [game caught by] what you have trained of hunting animals which you train as Allah has taught you'" — cited to support the idea that training can reshape nature, including moral nature.

### Prophetic Hadiths

- **"Nothing is heavier on the scales of deeds than good morals"** — narrated via Abu al-Darda' (Ahmed 27496, Abu Dawud 4799, al-Tirmidhi 2003). Qaradawi adopts this as his baseline definition.
- **"The most faithful are those with the best moral character"** — narrated via Aisha (Ahmed 24677, al-Tirmidhi).
- **"A person can reach the status of those who fast and spend the night praying by having a good moral character"** — narrated via Aisha (Ahmed 25013, Abu Dawud 4798, Ibn Hibban 480).
- **"I have been sent to perfect good morals and conduct"** — narrated via Abu Hurayrah (Ahmed 8952, al-Bukhari, al-Hakim 2/613, al-Albani in *al-Sahihah* 45).
- **The hadith of al-Mundhir al-Ashaj**: When told by the Prophet that he possessed two traits Allah likes — patience and forbearance — al-Mundhir asked whether he had chosen these or whether Allah had endowed him with them. The Prophet replied: "It is Allah who endowed you with them" (narrated by al-Tabarani 5/275, Abu Dawud 5225).
- **Aisha's statement**: When asked about the Prophet's manners, she replied: "His manners were the Qur'an" — narrated by Muslim (746) and Ahmed (24601).

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

### Al-Ghazali (d. AH 505)

Al-Ghazali defines moral character as "a set of well-established personality traits that lead to actions without thinking or planning." He draws a sharp distinction between **moral character** (stable dispositions) and **moral acts** (individual behaviors):
- A person acting generously on a whim or for ulterior motives is not genuinely generous.
- A patient person must be patient naturally and consistently, not merely struggling to suppress anger.
- A stingy person might act generously for show or hidden agendas.

This distinction is crucial: character is what you *are*, not what you *appear to do*.

### Dr. Draz (20th century)

Dr. Draz refines al-Ghazali's definition by emphasizing that moral traits must be **willful** — they involve choices, not mere abilities:
- Intellectual capacities (memory, imagination, aesthetic taste) are not moral traits, even though they are properties of the inner self.
- Moral traits are specifically those willful powers that trigger choices judged as good or evil.
- Artistic or aesthetic acts fall outside moral evaluation unless they involve a moral dimension.

### Al-Ezz Ibn Abd al-Salam (d. AH 660)

He classifies moral traits into four types:

1. **Honorable traits**: Values that motivate people to follow Allah's laws — shyness (promotes virtue, discourages sin), generosity (builds the earth and sows seeds of afterlife success), jealousy (prevents adultery), courage (protects against tyrants), mercifulness and tenderness (helps the weak and needy), patience, kindness, sympathy.

2. **Evil traits**: Immoral dispositions that motivate sinful deeds and distance people from good. Resisting them earns reward; succumbing brings punishment.

3. **Beneficial desires**: Natural instincts and desires that should be fulfilled within religious boundaries — beneficial in this world, the afterlife, or both.

4. **Harmful desires**: Sinful impulses — neglecting religious duties or committing forbidden acts.

He also adds a fifth category for unclassifiable actions and asserts a key principle of moral responsibility: **People are not rewarded merely for having good traits or punished for having evil ones; they are rewarded or punished for their willing choices and actions.** Innate dispositions are from Allah; accountability lies in what one does with them.

### Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya (d. AH 751)

He builds on al-Ezz Ibn Abd al-Salam's framework but focuses specifically on the distinction between **innate and acquired** moral traits. He affirms that innate traits are more powerful than acquired ones initially, but acquired traits, through persistent practice, can become as firmly established as innate ones — "habits form character."

### Al-Raghib al-Asfahani (d. AH 502)

He emphasizes the environmental dimension of akhlaq: "Company makes bad people good, and the reverse is also true." He warns readers to be careful about companionship because moral traits are acquired not only through interaction but even through observation. He cites the Quranic example of the dog of the People of the Cave (28:18) and notes that even animals and plants are affected by their surroundings.

## Types of Akhlaq

Qaradawi, following the classical tradition, identifies two fundamental types:

1. **Innate (*gharizi*)**: moral traits a person is born with, endowed by Allah. Evidence: the hadith of al-Mundhir al-Ashaj, in which the Prophet confirmed that his companion's patience and forbearance were divinely endowed, not self-chosen.

2. **Acquired (*muktasab*)**: moral traits developed through practice, training, companionship, and deliberate effort. Support: the Quran itself teaches that training can reshape nature (5:4), and Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya states unequivocally that "moral traits can be acquired through practice and training until they become second nature."

Acquired traits arise through:
- **Deliberate practice**: Acting virtuously until virtue becomes habitual.
- **Companionship**: Associating with people of good character, whose traits "rub off" through interaction and even mere observation.
- **Self-discipline**: Persistent effort to reform one's own disposition.

## Key Distinctions

### Character vs. Acts

Al-Ghazali's distinction is fundamental to the concept:
- **Moral character** (*khuluq*) = stable, well-established dispositions
- **Moral acts** (*af'al akhlaqiyya*) = individual behaviors motivated by character
- A generous act does not make someone generous; consistent, natural generosity does.
- A stingy person may perform generous acts — but these are motivated by factors other than generosity (hypocrisy, hidden agenda, social pressure).

### Character vs. Other Inner-Self Properties

Dr. Draz's clarification:
- **Intellectual abilities** (memory, imagination, reasoning) — not moral traits; cannot be judged good or evil in themselves.
- **Aesthetic capacities** (taste, eloquence) — willful but not morally evaluable.
- **Moral traits** — specifically those willful powers that generate morally evaluable choices.

### Two Criteria for Judging Character from Actions

For an action to reliably indicate moral character, it must meet two criteria:
1. **Consistency**: It recurs in a pattern; single actions cannot establish character judgment.
2. **Self-motivation**: It is willful and self-directed, not driven by fear, shyness, social pressure, or external compulsion.

## The Mutability Question

A central debate in Islamic ethics: Can moral character be changed?

- **Al-Tawhidi**: Moral character seems as unchangeable as physical creation, but encouraging improvement is neither wrong nor futile.
- **Al-Ghazali**: Emphatically rejects the claim that character is immutable. If character were truly unchangeable, all moral teachings would be in vain. "Dogs and horses can be trained to be gentle, and people too."
- **Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya**: "Moral traits can be acquired through practice and training until they become second nature."
- **Qaradawi**: Affirms mutability. Moral improvement is possible through practice, companionship, and divine assistance.

^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-01-01.txt]

## Responsibility and Accountability

Al-Ezz Ibn Abd al-Salam articulates a key principle:
- **Innate good traits**: A person is not rewarded merely for possessing them (since they did not choose them), but is rewarded for acting upon them.
- **Innate evil traits**: A person is not punished merely for possessing them, but is punished for choosing to act on them.

This balances divine sovereignty (Allah creates dispositions) with human accountability (people choose their actions). Everyone endowed with good traits is rewarded for acting accordingly; everyone with immoral tendencies is punished only for willfully following through.

## Contemporary Relevance

Akhlaq remains the central concern of Islamic ethics in the modern era. Qaradawi's treatment addresses several contemporary questions:
- **Nature vs. nurture**: The balance between innate disposition and acquired character directly engages modern debates about genetics and environment, which Qaradawi addresses further in Ethics in Islam (section 1.1).
- **Moral relativism**: By anchoring akhlaq in divine revelation (Quran and Sunnah), Qaradawi offers an objective basis for moral evaluation against secular relativism.
- **Self-improvement**: The mutability of character — confirmed by al-Ghazali, Ibn Qayyim, and Qaradawi — means that ethical self-improvement is always possible and religiously obligatory, not merely aspirational.
- **Companionship in the digital age**: Al-Raghib al-Asfahani's insight about the formative power of companionship and environment extends to media, social networks, and digital interactions.
