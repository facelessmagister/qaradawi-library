# Punishment / Uqubah (عُقُوبَة)

**Arabic:** عُقُوبَة  
**Transliteration:** ʿUqūbah  
**Domain:** Islamic Ethics (الأخلاق الإسلامية) and Fiqh al-Jināyāt (Criminal Law)

## Definition

*ʿUqūbah* (عُقُوبَة) — punishment, penalty, sanction — refers in Islamic discourse to the consequences imposed for violating moral, legal, or divine commands. In Qaradawi's treatment, punishment is never an end in itself but always serves broader objectives: deterrence (*zajr*), protection of society (*ḥifẓ al-mujtamaʿ*), retribution (*qiṣāṣ*) where due, and ultimately the purification of the individual (*tazkiyah*). Islamic punishment exists within a comprehensive moral framework where justice (*ʿadl*), mercy (*raḥmah*), and social welfare (*maṣlaḥah*) are inseparable.

The concept of punishment in Islam operates on three levels: (1) **moral consequences** — the natural rewards and punishments that follow from good and evil actions; (2) **legal sanctions** — penalties prescribed by the Quran, the Sunnah, or scholarly judgment for specific offenses; and (3) **divine punishment in the afterlife** — the ultimate recompense that Allah gives for unrepented sins.

## Qaradawi's Position

### 1. Punishment as Part of a Moral System, Not an Isolated Mechanism

Qaradawi argues emphatically that Islamic punishments cannot be extracted from their moral and social context. In *Ethics in Islam* (Chapter 2), he demonstrates that *hudud* penalties were never designed to operate in a vacuum. For example, the punishment for adultery presumes a society where marriage is affordable and accessible, where temptation is minimized, and where social welfare is guaranteed. When these preconditions are absent, "it is difficult for young people to satisfy their sexual needs in permissible ways, leaving sin as the only outlet."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

Similarly, the punishment for theft presumes a functioning zakat system, fair economic distribution, and the absence of poverty-driven desperation. "There is only one verse in the Holy Qur'an that specifies the penalty for stealing, but there are tens of verses that command people to establish Zakat, spend their money in Allah's cause, feed the poor, and fight corruption and all forms of social injustice."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

### 2. Three Levels of Consequences

Drawing on Dr. Draz's classification, Qaradawi identifies three levels of consequences:

1. **Moral consequences** — the natural emotional states that follow from good or evil actions: satisfaction and peace for moral behavior, regret and anguish for immoral behavior. "The joy or pain we feel after doing moral or immoral actions are natural reactions that stem from our conscience."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-03-03.txt]

2. **Legal sanctions** — the formal penalties prescribed for specific offenses: *ḥudūd* (Quran-specified penalties for theft, robbery, adultery, slander, etc.), *qiṣāṣ* (retaliation for murder and injury), and *taʿzīr* (discretionary penalties determined by judges).

3. **Divine punishment in the afterlife** — the ultimate recompense, including the promise of Paradise for the righteous and Hell for the unrepentant. "The greatest penalty is indeed the one in the afterlife."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-03-03.txt]

### 3. The High Bar for Conviction

Qaradawi emphasizes that Islamic law makes conviction extremely difficult as a safeguard against injustice. For adultery, four impeccable witnesses must testify to having directly witnessed the act — a bar "so high that it cannot be achieved without the use of tricks that are not permissible." In Islamic history, only four cases resulted in the penalty for adultery, and in all four cases "it was the adulterers who came out willingly and asked to be purified."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-03-03.txt]

### 4. Punishment and Social Conditions

Qaradawi's signature contribution is linking punishment to the moral and economic health of society. He argues that:

- **Adultery penalties** require: affordable marriage, reduced social temptation (eliminating permissive media, immodest fashion), and cultural facilitation of lawful fulfillment.
- **Theft penalties** require: a functioning zakat and welfare system, the absence of corruption, and economic justice that eliminates poverty-driven crime.
- **Alcohol and other penalties** require: a society that does not normalize or profit from the prohibited activity.

When these societal preconditions are absent, punishment becomes unjust and counterproductive.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

### 5. Punishment in the Context of Art and Diversion

In *Diversion and Arts in Islam*, Qaradawi addresses prohibitions related to art and entertainment as forms of protective punishment. The prohibition of idolatrous imagery, the regulation of music and singing, and the boundaries placed on visual representation all serve to protect the Muslim from spiritual corruption. However, he consistently argues for a middle course between total prohibition and unlimited permissibility, emphasizing that "everything is essentially allowed" except what is explicitly forbidden.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt] ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-05.txt]

### 6. Repentance and Forgiveness

A key theme in Qaradawi's treatment is that Allah's mercy always accompanies His justice. The Quran repeatedly offers forgiveness to those who repent: "Say, 'Oh My servants, who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins. Indeed, it is He who is the Forgiving, the Merciful'" (39:53). Good deeds are multiplied by 10 or even 700, while evil deeds are counted only once (6:160).^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-03-03.txt]

## The Relationship Between Punishment and Justice

Qaradawi's central argument is that **punishment serves justice**, and justice cannot be served by applying punishment in an unjust context. The severity of Islamic penalties is itself a form of justice — it protects the vulnerable from exploitation and violence. But this severity is tempered by:

1. **Extremely high evidentiary standards** (four witnesses for adultery, two for theft, etc.)
2. **Preference for mercy** in cases of doubt ("avert the *ḥadd* when there is doubt")
3. **Systemic preconditions** (social welfare, economic justice, moral education)
4. **Divine forgiveness** available through repentance

## Contemporary Relevance

1. **Decontextualized application of hudud**: Qaradawi's argument that punishments require preconditions challenges any attempt to apply hudud in societies lacking the Islamic social framework.
2. **Criminal justice reform**: The high evidentiary bar and preference for mercy provide Islamic resources for reforming harsh penal systems.
3. **Restorative justice**: The Islamic emphasis on zakat, social welfare, and the prevention of crime through economic justice aligns with modern restorative justice models.
4. **Repentance and rehabilitation**: The Quranic emphasis on repentance and forgiveness (39:53; 25:68–70) supports rehabilitation over purely retributive approaches.
5. **Social determinants of crime**: Qaradawi's linkage of criminal behavior to economic injustice and social corruption anticipates modern criminological insights.
