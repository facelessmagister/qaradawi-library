# Intention (نِيَّة)

**Arabic:** نِيَّة (*niyyah*) — plural: نِيَّات (*niyyāt*)
**Domain:** Fiqh al-Ibadat, Islamic Ethics, Usul al-Fiqh, Tazkiyah

## Definition

Niyyah (نِيَّة) is the inward resolve or purpose behind an action. In Islamic ethics and jurisprudence, intention is the primary determinant of the moral and legal worth of an act. The Prophet Muhammad (PBUH) declared: **"(The value of) an action depends on the intentions behind it. A man will be rewarded only for what he intends"** — a hadith narrated via Umar ibn al-Khattab and recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim, which scholars regard as one of the foundations of Islam, opening both al-Bukhari's *Sahih* and many treatises on ethics.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt] ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

Intention is not merely a passing thought; it is the settled purpose of the heart that directs an act toward its object — whether toward Allah (sincerity/ikhlas) or toward worldly aims. Qaradawi treats niyyah as the hinge connecting the outer form of an act to its inner reality, making it simultaneously an ethical and a legal concept.

## Qaradawi's Position

### 1. Intention Defines the Moral Value of Actions

Qaradawi emphasizes that in Islam, the moral status of acts depends not on their outward appearance but on the intentions and motives behind them and the values they reflect. A minor deed performed with pure intention may lead to Paradise, while the same outward deed tainted by hypocrisy may lead to Hell. He illustrates this with the Prophet's hadith about two men who passed by idol-worshippers: one offered flies to the idol and went to Hell for it; the other refused and was killed, going to Heaven. "It is not an issue of quantity, but it is an issue of giving up one's faith."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

Similarly, a woman went to Hell because of a cat she imprisoned (cruelty of heart), while a prostitute went to Heaven for giving a dog water (mercy of heart). The outward acts are small; what matters is the intention-condition of the heart behind them.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

### 2. Sincerity (Ikhlas) as a Matter of Intention

Qaradawi states directly: **"Sincerity is, first and foremost, a matter of intention."** Prayer is required of every Muslim, but prayer is meaningful only if its sole purpose is to worship Allah. Otherwise it is tainted with hypocrisy (riya'), as Allah describes the hypocrites: "When they stand for prayers, they stand lazily, showing [themselves to] people and not remembering Allah except a little" (Qur'an 4:142).^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

The moral ideal requires focusing "all of one's intentions, energy, and actions on Allah," because Allah accepts only actions performed for His sake (Qur'an 18:110; 98:5; 39:11).^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

### 3. Intention Balances Ritual and Inwardness

Qaradawi draws a comparative-religious point: Judaism focuses on rituals while Christianity focuses on intentions. **Islam establishes a balance by making intentions necessary for the validity of rituals and acts of worship.** The hadith "actions are judged by intentions" does not cancel the obligation of ritual; rather, it validates it. Islam does not permit people to do evil or neglect required acts of worship, no matter how good their intentions. A mosque cannot be built with ill-gotten money from usury, no matter how pious the intention: "Allah is pure, and He accepts only what is pure."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

This principle is reinforced in *Diversion and Arts in Islam*, where Qaradawi states that **good intentions, piety, or sincerity do not excuse transgression on Allah's Sovereignty.** Only Allah has the right to determine the lawful and unlawful; human intention cannot override divine prohibition.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

### 4. Reward for Unfulfilled Intentions

Qaradawi teaches that people are rewarded for their sincere intentions even when those intentions do not materialize as actions. He cites Qur'an 4:100: "Whoever emigrates for the cause of Allah will find on the earth many [alternative] locations and abundance. And whoever leaves his home as an emigrant to Allah and His Messenger and then death overtakes him — his reward has already become incumbent upon Allah."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

The four-rank hadith on knowledge and wealth illustrates this further: the person who has knowledge but not wealth, yet truthfully says "If I had wealth, I would do the deeds of so-and-so," receives the same reward as the one who actually spends in charity — because of the sincerity of intention. Conversely, the one who says "If I had wealth, I would do the deeds of so-and-so" in envy shares the same sin as the one who actually spends foolishly.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-02.txt]

### 5. Sinful Intentions

Intentions define actions even when the intended sin does not come to pass. The Prophet (PBUH) said: "When two Muslims engage in combat against each other and one is killed, both are doomed to Hell," because the slain man was just as eager to kill his opponent.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

Qaradawi extends this to legal distinctions: premeditated murder (intentional killing) leads to eternal punishment (Qur'an 4:93), while involuntary manslaughter is not a sin because there was no intention to kill — requiring only compensation (Qur'an 4:92). The distinction between intentional and unintentional acts is a recurring principle in Islamic law.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

### 6. Forgiveness of Unintentional Acts

Qaradawi emphasizes that Islam distinguishes clearly between intentional wrongdoing and unintentional mistakes. Allah says: "There is no blame upon you for that in which you have erred but [only for] what your hearts intended" (Qur'an 33:5).^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-03.txt]

This applies to:
- **Mistakes:** If a Muslim forgets they are fasting and eats, the fast remains valid.
- **Oaths:** "Allah does not impose blame upon you for what is unintentional in your oaths, but He imposes blame upon you for what your hearts have earned" (Qur'an 2:225). Scholars distinguish three types of oaths: meaningless oaths (lacking intention), intentional oaths (breakable with expiation), and false oaths (intended to mislead — a grave sin).^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-03.txt]

### 7. Intention in Fiqh: Trade and Zakah

In *Fiqh al-Zakah*, Qaradawi addresses intention as a legal condition (shart) that determines zakatability. Trade (*tijarah*) is defined by two elements: the action of buying/selling and **the intention of making profit**. Items bought for personal or household use are not trade assets regardless of their value; conversely, changing one's intention from personal use to trade makes an item zakatable. The majority view holds that changing the intention from selling for profit to personal use removes zakatability, while the Hanbalites (Ibn 'Aqil, Abu Bakr) hold that merely changing one's intention from personal use to trade suffices to make the item zakatable.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-04.txt]

In zakah on livestock, Qaradawi notes that the intention behind holding livestock — whether for trade or for personal use — determines which zakah rules apply. The Prophet (p) intentionally left certain areas of zakah undefined to preserve flexibility for the Muslim community.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-02.txt] ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-10.txt]

### 8. Intention and Direction of Life (Himmah)

In *Faith and Life*, Qaradawi connects niyyah to the broader concept of *himmah* (aspiration). He cites the hadith: "Whoever's intention is directed to the Hereafter, Allah makes his richness in his heart and brings his self together, and the good things of worldly life come to him even unwillingly. But whoever's intention is directed to worldly life, Allah makes his poverty between his eyes and disunites his self." The direction of one's intention determines whether life becomes one of contentment or perpetual dissatisfaction.^[raw/extracted/faith-and-life/ch-02.txt]

### 9. Intention in Contracts: The Case of Marriage

Qaradawi discusses how the Hanbali school treats intentions as equivalent to written agreements in certain contracts. In the context of *mutha* (temporary marriage), if the husband harbors the intention to divorce at a fixed time, the marriage agreement is considered null — because "the value of an action depends on the intentions behind it."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/section-04-03.txt]

## Evidence Base

### Quranic Verses
- **Qur'an 4:100** — Reward for emigration based on intention
- **Qur'an 4:93** — Intentional killing punished with Hell
- **Qur'an 4:92** — Unintentional killing requires compensation, not punishment
- **Qur'an 33:5** — "No blame upon you for that in which you have erred but [only for] what your hearts intended"
- **Qur'an 2:225** — Unintentional oaths not blamed
- **Qur'an 5:89** — Intention of oaths determines accountability
- **Qur'an 4:142** — Hypocrites' prayers invalid because of flawed intention
- **Qur'an 18:110** — Righteous work must be for Allah alone
- **Qur'an 98:5** — Commanded to worship Allah with sincerity
- **Qur'an 39:11** — The Prophet commanded to worship with sincerity
- **Qur'an 2:134, 2:141** — Each soul earns only what it strives for

### Prophetic Hadiths
- **"Actions are judged by intentions"** — Narrated via Umar ibn al-Khattab by al-Bukhari (on the beginning of revelations, 1) and Muslim (on leadership, 1907)
- **"When two Muslims engage in combat, both are doomed to Hell"** — Narrated via Abu Bakrah by al-Bukhari (on faith, 31) and Muslim (on social strife, 2888)
- **The four kinds of people (knowledge/wealth hadith)** — Narrated in various collections
- **"Allah is pure, and He accepts only what is pure"** — Narrated via Abu Hurayrah by Muslim (on Zakat, 1015)
- **"Whoever emigrates to Allah…"** — Based on Qur'an 4:100
- **The hadith on flies (idol offering)** — Narrated via Imam Ahmad

## Comparison with Classical Scholars

| Issue | Qaradawi's View | Hanafi | Maliki | Shafi'i | Hanbali |
|-------|----------------|--------|--------|---------|---------|
| Intention as condition for valid worship | Required; balances inward and outward | Intention required for salah; placed in heart | Intention required; specific formula for each act | Intention is first pillar of worship; must accompany act | Intention required; emphasizes sincerity |
| Intention in trade/zakah | Intention of profit defines trade asset; change of intention changes zakatability | Change of intention from trade to personal use removes zakatability | Similar to Hanafi view | Agrees on intention as condition | Ibn 'Aqil and Abu Bakr: mere change of intention from personal to trade makes item zakatable |
| Intention as equivalent to written contract (in marriage) | Notes Hanbali position | Follows explicit terms of contract | Follows explicit terms | Follows explicit terms | Intentions equivalent to written agreements in marriage contracts |
| Reward for unfulfilled sincere intention | Affirmed based on hadith and Qur'an 4:100 | Affirmed | Affirmed | Affirmed by al-Shafi'i | Affirmed, with detailed treatment by Ibn Rajab |

Qaradawi's distinctive contribution is his emphasis on the **balance** between intention and outward action — niyyah without action is insincere when action is possible; action without niyyah is empty ritual. He also applies the intention principle to modern economic questions (zakah on trade assets, determination of business vs. personal property), showing its continuing legal relevance.

## Contemporary Relevance

The principle that "actions are judged by intentions" has far-reaching implications for contemporary Muslim life:

1. **Economic ethics:** Intention determines whether a financial transaction is trade (zakatable) or personal use (not zakatable), a distinction of practical importance for modern Muslim business owners calculating their zakah obligations.
2. **Moral psychology:** In an age of social media and performative piety, the warning against praying "to be seen" (Qur'an 4:142) resonates with the challenge of maintaining sincerity in public religious expression.
3. **Legal responsibility:** The distinction between intentional and unintentional acts — in criminal law, in oaths, and in contracts — remains foundational to Islamic jurisprudence and contemporary Muslim minority legal discussions.
4. **Activism and da'wah:** Good intentions do not legitimize prohibited means; Qaradawi's insistence that "good intentions, piety, or sincerity do not excuse transgression on Allah's Sovereignty" cautions against ends-justify-means reasoning.
5. **Mental health and contentment:** The hadith on direction of intention (Hereafter vs. worldly life) links niyyah directly to psychological well-being: contentment vs. perpetual dissatisfaction.
