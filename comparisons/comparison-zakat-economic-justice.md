# Zakat and Economic Justice Across Qaradawi's Works

> Cross-book comparison of how Yusuf al-Qaradawi treats zakat (زَكَاة) across legal, theological, and economic dimensions in *Fiqh al-Zakah*, *Economic Security in Islam*, and *Education and Economy in the Sunnah*.

## 1. Genre and Methodological Mode

Each work deploys zakat within a distinct discursive register:

- **FZ** operates in **fiqh mode**: it treats zakat as a branch of *ahkam 'amaliyyah* (practical legal rulings). Every claim is anchored to one of the four usul (Qur'an, Sunnah, ijma', qiyas), and Qaradawi meticulously records intra-school and inter-school disagreements. The mode is adversarial-argumentative: positions are stated, evidence marshalled, objections enumerated and refuted.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-04.txt]

- **ES** operates in **comparative-ideological mode**: zakat is positioned as Islam's answer to the social-security gaps of capitalism and communism. Qaradawi argues that neither private insurance schemes nor state expropriation can match a divinely ordained, state-enforced, wealth-purification levy that has operated for fourteen centuries "without generating resentment among either givers or recipients."^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt] The mode is prescriptive-apologetic.

- **EES** operates in **prophetic-ethical mode**: zakat appears not as a standalone institution but as one node in a web of economic values the Prophet (peace be upon him) taught—production, anti-hoarding, neighbourly sharing, anti-extravagance. The famous hadith tying zakah to prayer in the creedal formula ("I have been commanded to fight people until they testify… and establish Prayer and pay Zakah") is cited to show that economic conduct is inseparable from faith.^[raw/extracted/education-economy-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

## 2. Definition of Zakat and Its Core Purpose

### FZ: Zakat as Legal Obligation and Wealth Purification

Qaradawi opens with the linguistic meaning of *zakah* (purification, growth) and immediately ties it to the Qur'anic verse "Out of their wealth take sadaqah so by it you purify and sanctify them" (9:103). Zakat purifies wealth from *shubuhat* (doubtful mixtures) and the giver from *bukhl* (miserliness). It is a *haqq* (right) of the poor embedded in wealth itself—not a gift the rich bestow voluntarily. The entire apparatus of nisab, haul, and rates exists to operationalize this divine right.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

### ES: Zakat as Social-Security Architecture

ES calls zakat "the centrepiece of Islam's economic security architecture" and frames its purpose in three tiers:
1. **Where family maintenance fails**, the community becomes responsible.
2. **Where the community falls short**, the state must act through zakat.
3. Zakat is "not voluntary charity but a legally enforceable right of the poor; it is collected and distributed by the state, not left to individual discretion."^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]

This tiered responsibility model is absent from FZ, which treats zakat's purpose theologically (purification, sanctification) and juristically (who pays, what, how much) rather than institutionally.

### EES: Zakat as Pillar Paired with Prayer

EES cites the hadith of Ibn 'Umar equating the zakah-payer and the prayer-estabisher as the threshold of communal belonging: "I have been commanded to fight people till they declare… and establish Prayer and pay Zakah." Zakat is "the sister of Prayer in the Qur'an and Sunnah. Prayer is the main pillar of Islam and Zakah is its bridge."^[raw/extracted/education-economy-sunnah/ch-02.txt] Qaradawi here emphasises the creedal dimension: non-payment equates to apostasy, following Abu Bakr's war against the apostates—though EES does not develop this argument as fully as ES does.

## 3. What Is Zakatable? — The Legal Dimension

### FZ: Exhaustive Jurisprudential Classification

FZ Ch 1 provides the most granular treatment anywhere in Qaradawi's corpus:

- **Definition of mal (pl. amwal)**: Hanafi jurists restrict mal to material, acquirable, conventionally usable things—excluding services, utilities, and rights. Shafi'i, Maliki, and Hanbali schools include utilities as mal. Qaradawi endorses the Hanafi position because "zakah cannot be collected from utilities and distributed to the deserving categories."^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

- **Condition of undivided, absolute ownership**: Zakat requires that the owner possess full disposal authority. Excluded: stolen property, liened assets, merchandise not yet received, public trust property. Debts are split into collectable (zakatable per year) and doubtful/dead (not zakatable until recovered).^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

- **Condition of growth (nama')**: Zakatable assets must be actually growing or have growth potential—livestock (reproduction), monetary wealth (trade), agriculture (crops). This excludes personal-use items (homes, tools, household furniture).^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

- **Business inventory ('urud al-tijarah)**: FZ Ch 4 devotes an entire chapter to proving zakatability of trade goods through all four usul. Qaradawi records the ijma' from Abu 'Ubaid: "all Muslims unanimously agree that trade assets are zakatable." He then refutes the Zahiri and Imami objections point by point.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-04.txt]

### ES: Rates and Categories in Summary

ES Ch 4 summarises the same classification in compressed, declarative form:
- 10% or 5% on agricultural produce (depending on irrigation)
- 2.5% on cash and commercial goods above nisab
- Graduated rates on livestock
- One-fifth on excavated treasure and minerals (noting juristic debate on distribution)
- Zakat al-Fitr as a per-capita levy in kind^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt]

ES does not argue *why* each category is zakatable or engage school disputes; it states what Islam prescribes and moves immediately to the socio-economic implications.

### EES: Zakat as One Form Among Many

EES categorises zakat under "worship-related traditions" and notes that "when you talk about Zakah, one immediately thinks of Zakat-ul-Mal in its different forms. But there are other forms of Zakah which were detailed by the Sunnah, such as the Zakah upon cattle and sheep, and the Zakah to end Fasting (Zakat-ul-Fitr)."^[raw/extracted/education-economy-sunnah/ch-02.txt] EES does not classify zakatable assets at all; instead it shows that economic values permeate every category of hadith—creedal, worship, moral, supplicatory.

## 4. Zakat and the State — The Institutional Dimension

### FZ: The State as Collector and Distributor (Implicit)

FZ assumes state collection as part of the historical model ('Umar's collectors assessing merchants) but its primary audience is the individual Muslim jurist or zakah-payer. Qaradawi records that 'Umar bin al-Khattab "used to calculate the assets of merchants, both present and absent assets, and then take zakah on them all."^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-04.txt] This is presented as historical precedent for the obligation, not as an argument for the modern Islamic state's fiscal role.

### ES: The State as Guarantor of Last Resort

ES is explicit: "where family maintenance fails, the community becomes responsible, and where the community falls short, the state must act through zakat."^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt] Qaradawi here draws a full institutional schema: the nuclear family → extended kin → neighbourhood → state-managed zakat fund. This graduated responsibility model is entirely absent from FZ, which addresses the *fard* on the individual, not the *mas'uliyyah* of the state.

ES also cites Abu Bakr's declaration of war against zakat-withholders—"By God! Whoso differentiates between the rights of Prayer and Poor-due shall be opposed by me"—and Ibn Hazm's ruling that zakat "must be extracted from a wealthy person even if he is reluctant."^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt] These are presented as evidence for the state's coercive role, not merely the individual's obligation.

### EES: Prophetic Paradigm Without State Architecture

EES presents zakat under the Prophetic model of personal moral accountability. It highlights the hadith linking zakah to faith-identity but does not develop the institutional-collection argument. Instead, it places zakat alongside exhortations to plant, cultivate, avoid waste, and protect neighbours—embedding redistribution in the moral economy of the *ummah* rather than its fiscal apparatus.^[raw/extracted/education-economy-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

## 5. Beyond Zakat: Supplementary Redistribution

### FZ: No Coverage (By Design)

FZ is exclusively about zakat and does not discuss sadaqah, expiatory payments, or neighbourly rights. Its domain is the mandatory levy, not the broader moral economy.

### ES: A Full Complement of Wealth-Transfer Mechanisms

ES Ch 5 is precisely about what lies *beyond* zakat:
- **Neighbourly rights**: forty houses in each direction; "he is not a believer whose neighbour goes hungry."
- **'Id sacrifice**: obligatory per Abu Hanifa on every solvent Muslim.
- **Oath-breaking expiation (kaffarah)**: feeding ten persons, clothing them, or freeing a slave.
- **Zihar expiation**: freeing a slave, fasting two consecutive months, or feeding sixty poor.
- **Ramadan daytime-intimacy expiation**: similarly structured graded redemption.
- **Fidya for those unable to fast**: feeding one poor person per day.^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-05.txt]

ES argues that "Islamic economic security is not confined to a single annual levy but permeates the entire fabric of worship, neighbourly conduct, and personal morality."^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-05.txt] This broader redistributive ecosystem contextualises zakat within a comprehensive wealth-transfer regime.

### EES: Economic Ethics in Every Hadith Category

EES approaches the same terrain from the Prophetic-sunnah side:
- Production hadiths: planting as continuous sadaqah ("no bird, human being, or animal eats from it without its being accepted as charity").
- Anti-hoarding hadiths: "None keeps goods till the price rises but a sinner."
- Anti-waste hadiths: the prohibition of water extravagance even at a running river.
- Debt hadiths: funeral prayer withheld from debtors.
- Neighbourly hadiths: "He is not one of the believers who eats his fill while his neighbour goes hungry."^[raw/extracted/education-economy-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

Where ES catalogues the *fiqh* of supplementary obligations, EES shows the same obligations embedded in the Prophet's moral pedagogy.

## 6. The Theology of Wealth — Divine Sovereignty vs. Human Stewardship

### FZ: Dual Attribution of Ownership

FZ Ch 1 devotes sustained attention to the Qur'anic paradox: wealth is simultaneously attributed to God ("give them out of the wealth of God that He has given") and to human vicegerents ("and spend out of the sustenance whereof He has made you vicegerents"). Qaradawi resolves this by distinguishing **ultimate ownership** (God's) from **dispositional authority** (the human's delegated right). Zakat is obligatory precisely because this delegated authority comes with a divinely mandated charge.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

Ahmad bin 'Abd al-Rahim al-Dahlawi is cited: "When God made it permissible for people to use what He created for them… people got greedy about things, so there was a need to establish a rule based on the priorities of acquiring things."^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

### ES: Trusteeship and Social Right

ES presents the same theology in compressed form: "No human being is the master of the wealth that he possesses. He is a mere trustee of it."^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-04.txt] God's verse is cited: "Believe in Allah and His Messenger, and spend of that whereof He hath made you trustees." The economic implication is that the poor's share is a *pre-existing right within the wealth itself*, not a transfer from the rich.

### EES: Implicit — Faith-Property Nexus

EES does not explicitly theorise divine sovereignty over wealth. Instead it demonstrates this principle through the creedal hadith: the protection of life and property is contingent on testifying, praying, and paying zakah. Property rights, in other words, are not absolute but conditional on fulfilling the zakah obligation.^[raw/extracted/education-economy-sunnah/ch-02.txt]

| Imami 20% levy on trade profits | Full rebuttal (Ch 4) | Not addressed | Not addressed |
| Scholars who deny zakat on business inventory | Systematic refutation (Ch 4) | Not addressed | Not addressed |
| Capitalism's individualist insurance | Not addressed | Rebuttal ("cannot replicate") | Not addressed |
| Socialism's coercive expropriation | Not addressed | Rebuttal ("materialist frameworks") | Not addressed |
| Hoarding and price manipulation | Not addressed | Not addressed | Prophetic condemnation cited |
| Extravagance and waste | Not addressed | Not addressed | Prophetic condemnation cited |

FZ is an intra-fiqh argument; ES is an inter-systemic argument; EES is a Prophetic-ethical argument.

## 9. Convergence Points

Despite their different modes, all three works converge on fundamental positions:

1. **Zakat is not voluntary**: It is a *haqq* (right) of the poor embedded in wealth, divinely ordained, and state-enforceable. FZ proves this juristically; ES argues it institutionally; EES demonstrates it creedally.

2. **Zakat purifies wealth and the giver**: The Qur'anic formula "take sadaqah so that you may purify and sanctify them" (9:103) appears in all three works as the theological anchor.

3. **Zakat targets surplus, not subsistence**: FZ expounds nisab and growth conditions; ES argues that zakat "reduces capital concentration" citing the verse "lest it circulate solely among the wealthy"; EES exhorts productive use of land and condemns hoarding.

4. **Zakat operates within a broader moral economy**: FZ acknowledges this implicitly by defining zakah against *sadaqah*; ES makes it explicit with a full chapter on supplementary mechanisms; EES embeds zakah within the Prophetic economic ethic.

5. **Non-payment is a grave sin with communal consequences**: FZ cites the prophetic warning about wealth decreasing; ES cites Abu Bakr's war and Ibn Hazm; EES ties payment to faith-identity itself.

## 10. Divergence and Complementarity

| Dimension | FZ Emphasis | ES Emphasis | EES Emphasis |
|-----------|------------|------------|-------------|
| **Primary audience** | Jurists and muftis | Policy-makers and students of Islamic economics | General Muslims seeking Prophetic guidance |
| **Level of detail** | Exhaustive (school-by-school) | Summarised (rates and categories) | Illustrative (selected hadiths) |
| **Argumentation** | Usul-based: Qur'an → Sunnah → ijma' → qiyas | Ideological: Islam vs. capitalism vs. socialism | Ethical: Prophetic model of production and distribution |
| **Role of the state** | Implicit (historical collectors mentioned) | Explicit (state as guarantor of last resort) | Absent (focus on individual and community) |
| **Scope beyond zakat** | None (by design) | Full chapter on supplementary mechanisms | Broad economic ethic encompassing all hadith types |
| **Treatment of opponents** | Intramadhhabi polemic (Zahiri, Imami) | Intersystemic polemic (capitalism, socialism) | Moral denunciation (hoarders, cheats, wastrels) |

---

## 11. Synthesis: Qaradawi's Integrated Vision

Taken together, these three works form a layered argument:

1. **FZ** establishes *what zakat is* in precise juristic terms—its categories, conditions, rates, and proofs. Without this foundation, the other two works have no legs.

2. **ES** shows *why zakat matters* as an institution—how it functions as a social-security mechanism that neither capitalism nor socialism can replicate, and how it fits within a graduated system of family → community → state responsibility.

3. **EES** reveals *the Prophetic moral economy* in which zakat is embedded—where production is worship, waste is sin, neighbours have enforceable rights, and economic conduct is inseparable from faith.

Qaradawi's genius is that the same scholar who wrote a two-volume fiqh manual also wrote a macro-economic argument and a sunnah-based moral treatise, and the three perspectives are mutually reinforcing rather than contradictory. The juristic precision of FZ prevents ES from becoming mere rhetoric; the institutional vision of ES prevents FZ from becoming a technical manual disconnected from purpose; and the Prophetic ethos of EES gives both works their spiritual grounding.
