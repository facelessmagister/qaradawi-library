# Halal/Haram and Islamic Permissibility Across Qaradawi's Works

> Cross-book comparison of how Yusuf al-Qaradawi's signature principle — *al-aṣl fī al-ashyāʾ al-ibāḥah* (the original permissibility of things) — operates across fiqh, ethics, and lifestyle contexts in *Diversion and Arts in Islam*, *Fiqh al-Zakah*, *Ethics in Islam*, and *Economic Security in Islam*.

## 1. The Core Principle: Al-Aṣl fī al-Ashyāʾ al-Ibāḥah

Al-Qaradawi's jurisprudential signature is the maxim **al-aṣl fī al-ashyāʾ al-ibāḥah** — "the original principle in all things is permissibility." This principle holds that everything is lawful unless a clear proof (*dalīl*) from the Qur'an, authentic Sunnah, or scholarly consensus (*ijmāʿ*) establishes its prohibition. The burden of proof falls on the one who prohibits, not on the one who permits.

Qaradawi articulates this principle most explicitly in *Diversion and Arts* Ch. 4, where he states: "The Muslim scholars are of the opinion that everything is essentially allowed," citing Sura 2:29 ("God is the One Who created for you all that is on the earth") and the Prophetic hadith: "What Allah made permissible in His Book is lawful, and what He forbidden is unlawful, and what He deliberately did not mention is forgiven."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

The principle is not abstract theory — it is a methodological weapon that Qaradawi deploys against two extremes: ascetic over-prohibition and libertine over-permission. As he writes in DA Ch. 1, "Avoiding the two extremes, I have chosen to follow a middle course. I have exerted my utmost effort to reach a just and unbiased conclusion. My argument depends on sound and authentic texts and is guided by the torch of Shariah and Fiqh."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt]

This comparison traces how the same principle produces different results when applied in three distinct domains: (a) lifestyle and cultural practice, (b) economic law, and (c) moral character.

## 2. Permissibility in the Lifestyle Context: Diversion and Arts

### 2.1 The Middle Course Against Two Extremes

*Diversion and Arts* is Qaradawi's most sustained application of the permissibility principle to lifestyle questions. The book opens by identifying two deviant positions: (1) the ascetic rejection of all entertainment as incompatible with piety, and (2) the libertine embrace of all entertainment without regard for divine boundaries.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt]

Qaradawi's Qur'anic argument is ontological: Allah created the world with both utilitarian and aesthetic dimensions. Cattle provide warmth and food yet also beauty (Sura 16:5-6). Horses and mules serve as transport and ornamentation (Sura 16:8). The sea yields both sustenance and adornments (Sura 16:14). God explicitly asks: "Who has forbidden the ornaments of God which He brought forth for His servants, and who has forbidden the good things which He has provided?" (Sura 7:32). The interrogative pronoun "who" is a divine rejection of those who prohibit what Allah has made lawful.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt]

This ontological grounding is the foundation on which all specific rulings rest. Beauty and diversion are built into creation; they require no proof of permissibility — prohibition requires proof.

### 2.2 Singing and Music: The Paradigm Case

DA Ch. 4 provides the most detailed application of the permissibility principle to a single issue. Qaradawi surveys four scholarly positions:

1. **Total permission** — all singing and music are lawful.
2. **Partial permission** — singing without instruments is lawful; instruments are forbidden.
3. **Total prohibition** — all singing and music are forbidden.
4. **Conditions-based approach** — permissibility depends on content, context, accompaniment, and intention.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

Qaradawi endorses the fourth position. His method:
- He begins from the principle of original permissibility.
- He examines each proof offered by the prohibitionists (Sura 31:6 on "vain talk," Sura 28:55 on "nonsense," various hadiths) and refutes them through the methodology of Ibn Hazm, arguing that the Qur'anic verses target disbelief and mockery, not music per se.
- He evaluates hadith chains: the Bukhari hadith on "Al-Ma'azef" is a *mu'allaq* (hanging) narration with a weak chain; hadiths on girl singers suffer from *da'if* (weak) transmission; the hadith on the shephard's pipe actually proves permissibility (the Prophet merely avoided it, he did not forbid it).
- He introduces the crucial concept of **intention (niyya)**: "Good intention turns diversion into an approach to Allah. It can intermingle fun with obedience to Allah. Evil intention corrupts good deeds which are done for mere pretence rather than worship of Allah."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

This is the permissibility principle in full operation: the default is lawfulness; prohibition requires clear proof; intention transforms the moral quality of permissible acts; and the middle course is between absolute prohibition and absolute license.

### 2.3 Visual Arts and Photography: Graduated Rulings

DA Ch. 5 and Ch. 6 apply the same principle to visual representation, but here the analysis produces a *graduated scale* of rulings rather than a binary halal/haram determination.

Qaradawi identifies four reasons for picture-making prohibitions in hadith:^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-05.txt]

1. **Idolatry** — images made for worship (gravest sin; constitutes *kufr* if done knowingly).
2. **Religious symbolism** — images of other religions' symbols (e.g., the cross; forbidden).
3. **Imitation of divine creation** — making images with the intent to rival Allah as Creator (serious sin).
4. **Luxury and conspicuous consumption** — images serving as status symbols in the home (disliked, *makrūh*).

This graduated approach — from *kufr* through *ḥarām* through *makrūh* to *ḥalāl* — is a direct consequence of the permissibility principle combined with intention analysis. Since the original ruling is permissibility, the *degree* of prohibition corresponds to the *degree* of harm identified in the specific context.

For photography (Ch. 6), Qaradawi relies on the pivotal fatwa of Shaykh Muhammad Bakheet, the Mufti of Egypt, who distinguished photography from picture-making on the grounds that a photograph captures a reflection of existing reality rather than creating something from imagination. The photographer is a "reflector," not a creator.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-06.txt]

This produces a nine-point graduated ruling ranging from the most strictly forbidden (images for worship) to the broadly permissible (photographs of inanimate objects, photographs taken for lawful purposes). The key insight is that *permissibility is the default*, and prohibition attaches to specific purposes, not to the medium itself.

## 3. Permissibility in the Legal Context: Fiqh al-Zakah

### 3.1 The Definition of *Māl* and the Scope of Zakatability

*Fiqh al-Zakah* Ch. 1 applies the permissibility principle in an entirely different register. Here, the question is not "Is this thing lawful to enjoy?" but "Is this category of wealth subject to zakah?"^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

Qaradawi's method is consistent: he begins from the Qur'anic general text (*naṣṣ ʿāmm*) and refuses to restrict it without compelling evidence. The Qur'an legislates zakah on wealth (*amwāl*) in general (Sura 9:103, Sura 51:19), and the specific categories it mentions — gold, silver, crops, trade earnings, minerals — are illustrative, not exhaustive.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

The permissibility principle operates here as an *expansion principle*: just as things are originally permissible unless proven prohibited, zakatable categories are originally expansive unless proven restricted. Restrictions require proof; the general Qur'anic text needs no proof.

Qaradawi endorses the Hanafi definition of *māl* (wealth) as material things that can be acquired and conventionally used, excluding services and non-material rights. He argues that zakah cannot be collected from utilities and distributed to recipients — only material assets are zakatable.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

### 3.2 Ownership and the Condition of Absolute Disposition

The zakatability conditions further reflect the permissibility logic: wealth must be under undivided and absolute ownership. Stolen property, liened assets, and merchandise not yet received are excluded — not because they are *ḥarām* but because the owner lacks full dispositional authority.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

This is a crucial distinction: the permissibility principle in fiqh distinguishes between *lawfulness of acquisition* (the thing itself is lawful) and *obligation of zakah* (the condition of ownership triggers the duty). A thing can be perfectly halal to own and yet not zakatable because the ownership conditions are not met.

### 3.3 Halal Wealth vs. Haram Income

*Fiqh al-Zakah* also implicitly addresses the converse of permissibility — the effect of *ḥarām* on wealth. Zakah is a purification (*tanzikah*) of wealth from doubtful mixtures (*shubuhāt*). This creates a dual relationship:

- **Permissible wealth** that meets the conditions is *zakatable* — the obligation confirms the lawfulness of the wealth.
- **Prohibited wealth** (riba, gambling proceeds, stolen goods) is not merely excluded from zakah; it must be disposed of entirely. Zakah cannot purify what is inherently unlawful.

This echoes the permissibility principle in reverse: just as everything is permissible unless proven prohibited, zakah applies to all permissible wealth unless a specific condition excludes it. But the prohibited is not merely "not zakatable" — it is "not ownable."

## 4. Permissibility in the Ethical Context: Ethics in Islam

### 4.1 Intention as the Axis of Moral Permissibility

In *Ethics in Islam*, the permissibility principle takes on a distinctly moral character. Where DA uses intention (*niyya*) to modulate the ruling on a *fiqh* question (is music halal or haram?), EI uses intention as the *defining criterion of moral worth*.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

The Prophet's hadith "Actions are judged by intentions" (*innamā al-aʿmāl bi-al-niyyāt*) is deployed not to establish a legal ruling but to ground the philosophical principle that moral worth resides in intention. This produces three key insights:

1. **Permissible acts can become virtuous through good intention.** "Good intention turns diversion into an approach to Allah" (DA Ch. 4) finds its ethical counterpart in the idea that sincerity (*ikhlāṣ*) transforms everyday permissible actions into worship.

2. **Permissible acts can become sinful through evil intention.** Eating, drinking, and recreation — all originally permissible — become blameworthy if done with the intention of ostentation, wastefulness, or disobedience.

3. **The moral quality of permissible acts is indeterminate until intention is specified.** A neutral act — walking to the market, listening to birdsong, wearing fine clothing — is neither virtuous nor sinful in itself. Intention determines its moral character.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

This is an *expansion* of the permissibility principle from the fiqh domain into the ethical domain. In fiqh, permissibility is a legal category: a thing is halal unless proven haram. In ethics, permissibility is a *platform* for moral elevation or moral degradation — the same act can be worship, indifference, or sin depending on the heart.

### 4.2 Prohibitions as Moral Boundaries

EI Ch. 4's discussion of divine ethics — sincerity, observing Allah (*murāqaba*), self-accountability (*muḥāsaba*), reliance on Allah (*tawakkul*) — reframes prohibitions not merely as legal boundaries but as moral safeguards. The prohibition of riba, for instance, is not only a fiqh ruling (as in FZ and ES) but also a moral requirement: it protects the social bond between believers and prevents the exploitation that destroys trust.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

The chapter on applied ethics demonstrates that Islamic morality operates as an integrated whole: divine, individual, and social dimensions are inseparable. A haram act is not merely "illegal" in the fiqh sense — it is a violation of the relationship with Allah (divine ethics), a corruption of the self (individual ethics), and a disruption of social harmony (collective ethics). Permissibility, conversely, means that the act is *aligned* with all three dimensions.

### 4.3 Faith, Morality, and the Permissibility of Enjoyment

EI Ch. 1 establishes the inseparability of faith and moral conduct, citing the hadith: "One who cannot be trusted cannot be described as faithful."^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt] This has a direct bearing on the permissibility principle: the believer who enjoys halal pleasures with gratitude and moderation is *fulfilling* the moral requirement of thanksgiving (*shukr*), not violating it. The one who prohibits what Allah has made lawful is, paradoxically, committing a moral error — the error of ingratitude to the Creator.

This is Qaradawi's answer to the ascetic extreme: prohibiting the permissible is not piety but a form of *bid'ah* (religious innovation) that contradicts the Qur'anic command: "Who has forbidden the ornaments of God which He brought forth for His servants?"

## 5. Permissibility in the Economic Context: Economic Security in Islam

### 5.1 Trusteeship, Not Absolute Ownership

ES Ch. 2 reframes the permissibility of wealth through the lens of divine trusteeship. Allah is the real owner of all wealth; human beings are mere custodians: "And spend of that whereof He hath made you trustees" (Sura 57:7).^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-02.txt]

This produces a distinctive configuration of the permissibility principle:

- **Wealth is originally permissible** — private property is a natural right that Islam affirms.
- **Permissibility is conditioned** — ownership is not absolute; it operates within divinely ordained limits.
- **Prohibition defines boundaries** — riba, fraud, hoarding, and exploitation are not merely "illegal" but violations of the trusteeship relationship.

Islam accepts the right of private property, "because it is a satisfaction of man's natural sentiment," but subjects it to social safeguards. "There appears no wrong in the ownership of personal property. The real evil lies with the people who exploit others for increasing their riches."^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-02.txt]

### 5.2 Riba as the Paradigmatic Economic Prohibition

ES Ch. 2 positions riba (usury) as the primary economic haram, in direct contrast to zakah which is the primary economic halal obligation. The Qur'anic principle "no laden soul bears another's load" (Sura 6:165) means that Islam does not punish all the wealthy for the crimes of a few exploiters — prohibitions target specific harms, not the category of wealth itself.^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-02.txt]

Riba is prohibited not because wealth is haram but because the method of acquiring it is exploitative. This is the economic corollary of the permissibility principle: just as entertainment is originally permissible unless proven haram, wealth is originally permissible unless acquired through a haram method.

### 5.3 The Middle Path Between Capitalism and Communism

ES Ch. 2 explicitly frames Islam as a middle path:^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-02.txt]

- **Capitalism** treats wealth as absolutely owned by the individual, with no enforceable obligations to the poor beyond voluntary charity.
- **Communism** abolishes private property entirely, treating all wealth as state-owned.
- **Islam** affirms private ownership as originally permissible but subjects it to divine conditions (zakah is compulsory, not voluntary) and moral restraints (riba is prohibited, hoarding is condemned).

This mirrors DA's middle course between asceticism and libertinism: just as the permissibility principle rejects blanket prohibition of the arts while also rejecting unqualified license, it rejects both absolute private property and absolute confiscation while affirming regulated permissibility.

### 6.3 The Function of Intention (Niyya)

Intention operates differently in each context:

- **In DA**, intention *modulates* the ruling: singing with a good intention (refreshment for worship) is virtuous; singing with an evil intention (disobedience) is sinful; singing with a neutral intention (mere diversion) is forgiven.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

- **In FZ**, intention is relevant to the *fulfillment* of the obligation (zakah must be paid with the intention of zakah, not merely as a tax), but the *category of zakatable wealth* is determined objectively, not by intention.^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

- **In EI**, intention *constitutes* the moral quality of the act: "Actions are judged by intentions." The same outward action — praying, fasting, giving charity — is either worship or hypocrisy depending on intention.^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

- **In ES**, intention recedes behind the *objective social function* of the economic obligation: zakah is collected and distributed by the state regardless of the payer's private intention; riba is prohibited regardless of whether both parties consent.^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-02.txt]

This progression — from intention as modulator (DA) through intention as fulfillment condition (FZ) to intention as moral constitution (EI) and finally to intention giving way to objective obligation (ES) — reveals that the permissibility principle is not a single fixed doctrine but a methodological orientation that interacts differently with the *niyya* framework depending on the domain.

### 6.4 The Graduated Scale of Prohibition

A striking structural similarity across all four works is Qaradawi's use of *graduated scales of prohibition* rather than binary halal/haram classifications:

- **DA Ch. 6** produces a nine-point scale for images and photography, from images made for worship (gravest prohibition, potentially *kufr*) to photographs of inanimate objects for lawful purposes (fully permissible).^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-06.txt]

- **DA Ch. 4** distinguishes singing that is: (a) obligatory (e.g., calling to prayer), (b) permissible (wholesome diversion), (c) disliked (distraction from obligations), and (d) forbidden (obscene content, female singers before unrelated men).^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt]

- **FZ Ch. 1** distinguishes wealth that is: (a) fully zakatable, (b) conditionally exempt (debts, liened assets), and (c) entirely prohibited (riba-based, stolen).^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

- **EI Ch. 4** distinguishes acts that are: (a) worship (sincere intention toward Allah), (b) neutral (permissible acts with no particular moral valence), (c) disliked (permissible acts done with vanity or negligence), and (d) sinful (prohibited by explicit text).^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

- **ES Ch. 2** distinguishes economic activity that is: (a) halal and zakatable (normal trade and production), (b) halal but socially conditioned (wealth with compulsory zakah obligation), and (c) haram (riba, fraud, hoarding).^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-02.txt]

This insistence on graduated rulings rather than binary categories is the practical consequence of the permissibility principle. If the default is permissibility, then prohibition must be proportional to the evidence for it — and the evidence varies in strength, context, and degree.

## 7. The Permissibility Principle as Qaradawi's Signature Method

### 7.1 From Legal Maxim to Worldview

What emerges from this comparison is that al-Qaradawi has elevated the fiqh maxim *al-aṣl fī al-ashyāʾ al-ibāḥah* from a rule of legal interpretation to a comprehensive hermeneutical orientation. In the hands of a conventional jurist, the maxim is a tie-breaking rule: when proof for prohibition is absent, the thing remains permissible. In Qaradawi's hands, the maxim becomes:

1. **An ontological claim**: The Creator made the world with both useful and beautiful dimensions, and both are blessings for which gratitude is due (DA Ch. 1, citing Sura 7:31-32, 16:5-8, 16:14).

2. **A legal method**: The burden of proof falls on the prohibitivist. Restrictions require evidence; liberties do not (DA Ch. 4, FZ Ch. 1).

3. **A moral principle**: Permissible acts are morally indeterminate until intention specifies their character. The same act can be worship, indifference, or sin (EI Ch. 4, DA Ch. 4).

4. **An economic philosophy**: Private property and business activity are originally permissible but subject to divinely ordained social obligations and moral constraints (ES Ch. 2, FZ Ch. 1).

5. **A polemical stance**: Against ascetics who prohibit what Allah permits (DA Ch. 1), against literalists who restrict general texts without proof (FZ Ch. 1), against moralists who separate ethics from law (EI Ch. 1), and against both laissez-faire capitalists and coercive communists (ES Ch. 2).

### 7.2 Tensions Within the Method

The permissibility principle generates its own internal tensions:

- **Expansive vs. restrictive**: In DA, the principle *expands* the scope of permissible entertainment. In FZ, the same principle *expands* the scope of zakatable wealth. But in EI and ES, the principle also *affirms* that prohibitions (riba, exploitation, hypocrisy) are real and binding. The principle does not abolish prohibition; it insists that prohibition requires proof.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt] ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt] ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-02.txt]

- **Individual intention vs. objective obligation**: In DA, intention determines the moral quality of entertainment. In ES, intention recedes behind the objective obligation of zakah and the objective prohibition of riba. The same scholar who uses *niyya* to defend permissibility in lifestyle also uses *fard* to enforce obligation in economics.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt] ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-02.txt]

- **Graduation vs. clarity**: The nine-point scale for images in DA Ch. 6 produces a nuanced ruling system, but Qaradawi also insists that authentic hadith texts "cannot be overridden by arbitrary interpretation."^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-06.txt] Graduation serves to preserve the *spirit* of prohibition while accommodating context; it does not abolish the *text* of prohibition.

Qaradawi resolves these tensions through his consistent method: begin with permissibility, examine the evidence, calibrate the ruling to the context, and always position the outcome between the two extremes. The result is not a monolithic halal/haram binary but a spectrum calibrated to the strength of the proof, the gravity of the harm, and the quality of the intention.

## 8. Convergence Points

Despite their different domains, all four works converge on fundamental positions:

1. **The default is permissibility**: Every work opens from the assumption that things are lawful unless proven otherwise. None opens from the assumption of prohibition.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt] ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt] ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-01.txt] ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-02.txt]

2. **The burden of proof falls on the prohibitivist**: Whether the question is music, zakah categories, moral prohibitions, or economic restrictions, the one who claims prohibition must bring evidence. Absent evidence, the thing remains permissible.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt] ^[raw/extracted/fiqh-al-zakah/ch-01.txt]

3. **Prohibitions are real and binding**: The permissibility principle does not dissolve prohibition. Ribā is haram. Exploitation is haram. Idolatry is haram. Obscenity is haram. The principle says that prohibition requires proof — not that proof is impossible.^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-02.txt] ^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-05.txt]

4. **Intention matters**: In every domain, the quality of intention transforms the moral character of permissible acts. This is not arbitrary subjectivism but a systematic recognition that moral worth resides in the heart's orientation.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-04.txt] ^[raw/extracted/ethics-in-islam/ch-04.txt]

5. **The middle course is the Islamic course**: Whether between asceticism and libertinism (DA), between restriction and expansion (FZ), between legalism and moralism (EI), or between capitalism and communism (ES), Islam charts a path that affirms the lawful while respecting the prohibited.^[raw/extracted/diversion-arts/ch-01.txt] ^[raw/extracted/economic-security/ch-02.txt]
