## Summary

The final chapter turns to humor, mirth, games, and recreational activities, arguing that laughter and playfulness are intrinsic to human nature and fully compatible with Islamic life. Al-Qaradawi opens with the Qur'anic reminder that life is a journey of struggle (Sura 90:4) and that believers especially face hardship, making lawful entertainment a psychological necessity rather than a luxury. He then documents the Prophet's own humor in extensive detail: his jest with the old woman about Paradise, his playful remark about camels to a man requesting a mount, his teasing of A'isha and Sawda over food, and his delight in festive occasions such as Eid celebrations and weddings. The chapter emphasizes that the Prophet's humor never involved lies, setting an ethical boundary for permissible jesting. Al-Qaradawi describes the rich tradition of Arab humor — political jokes, Goha stories, riddles, puppetry (khayal al-zill), and caricature — and evaluates each form against Islamic norms. He classifies games and recreational activities as lawful by default, subject to conditions (no gambling, no neglect of obligations, no immoral content), and concludes the book by reiterating that Islam is a religion of fitra (natural disposition) that embraces permissible joy while rejecting both puritanical severity and unbridled indulgence.
