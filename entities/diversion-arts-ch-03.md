## Summary

Chapter 3 addresses the Islamic verdict on poetry and literary arts, beginning with the principle that Islam encourages both the appreciation and expression of beauty. Al-Qaradawi documents the Prophet's engagement with poetry — he listened to Ka'b ibn Zuhayr's famous ode "Banat Su'ad," supplicated for the poet al-Nabigha al-Ja'di, and commissioned Hassan ibn Thabit to defend the da'wa through verse. Several companions and great Imams, including al-Shafi'i and Ibn al-Mubarak, composed poetry. The chapter cites the hadith "Some poetry contains wisdom; some eloquent speeches have the influence of magic," while also noting that the Qur'an (Sura 26:224-227) censures poets whose actions contradict their words — except those who believe and act righteously. Al-Qaradawi argues that discipline and message are the bases of art: content must not go astray, though new literary forms (free verse, plays, novels) are permissible innovations. He insists on preserving classical Arabic against colloquial dilution, viewing it as the essential bond of the ummah. The chapter also tackles the fiqh of fiction, arguing that literary make-believe does not constitute unlawful lying because the audience knows it is fiction, and it transitions into the topic of audio-aesthetic arts (singing and music) that Chapter 4 will address in detail.
