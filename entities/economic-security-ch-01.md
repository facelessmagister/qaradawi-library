## Summary

This opening chapter surveys six distinct historical and ideological perspectives on poverty before presenting the Islamic viewpoint. Qaradawi first examines advocates of world-desertion who view poverty as a divine blessing fostering spiritual elevation, then the Jabariyah (deniers of free will) who counsel passive acceptance of poverty as divinely ordained. A third group favours individual charity but lacks institutional mechanisms to secure the poor's rights. The capitalistic view holds individuals solely responsible for their economic condition and treats welfare as discretionary, a position that historically bred exploitation, selfishness, and the subjugation of labour. Marxist socialism and communism, the fifth perspective, seek to abolish private property entirely and foment class hatred, yet share with capitalism a materialist worldview that excludes religion from social order. Islam, the sixth and final position, rejects both passive resignation and violent revolution. It affirms the dignity of work, recognises poverty as a social problem requiring institutional solutions, and establishes zakat as a legally enforced right of the poor rather than a mere moral injunction. Each non-Islamic approach is shown to suffer from fundamental defects—world-desertion neglects material welfare, predestination paralyses initiative, individual charity lacks coercive force, capitalism breeds exploitation, and socialism incites class hatred—thereby establishing the need for Islam's distinctive synthesis of personal responsibility and institutional obligation.
