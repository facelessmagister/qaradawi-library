## Summary

This chapter shifts the evidentiary basis from Qur'anic promises to Prophetic traditions, arguing that the Sunnah abounds with glad tidings of Islam's eventual triumph but that these auspices were neglected during eras of decline, when Muslims circulated only the hadiths of sedition and despair. Al-Qaradawi clarifies that the Prophet does not independently know the Unseen; his foreknowledge derives solely from divine revelation, making these prophetic reports authoritative indicators of a divinely guaranteed future. The chapter's first substantive auspice is the worldwide prevalence of Islam, anchored in the hadith of Tamim al-Dari: "This affair will reach as far as night and day reach, and Allah will not leave a dwelling of brick or fur but Allah will make this religion enter it." This tradition, corroborated by the narration of al-Miqdad bin al-Aswad, affirms Islam's universal reach — urban and Bedouin alike — and echoes the tripartite Qur'anic verse (At-Tawbah 33; Al-Fath 48; As-Saff 9). The second auspice is the prophesied return of Islam to Europe and the conquest of Rome, based on the hadith reported by Imam Ahmad in which the Prophet was asked which city would fall first, Constantinople or Rome, and answered "the city of Heraclius" — fulfilled when Muhammad the Conqueror took Constantinople in 857 AH, leaving the conquest of Rome as a still-pending prophecy that reinforces Muslim hope.
