## Summary

This chapter draws auspices from Islamic and pre-Islamic history to demonstrate that divine victory arrives precisely when believers are at their most vulnerable and have exhausted all recourse except Allah. Al-Qaradawi begins with two pivotal historical certainties. The first is the principle that Allah grants victory only when people are in dire need and have surrendered all trust in their own power — illustrated by the Emigration (Hijrah), when the Prophet and Abu Bakr hid in the cave with pursuers at the entrance, and the Qur'anic narrative of divine succor (At-Tawbah 9:40). The same pattern appears at Badr, where a vastly outnumbered and ill-equipped Muslim force prevailed because they called upon Allah, who responded with angelic reinforcement (Al-Anfal 8:9-10). The second historical certainty is that the rise of Islam from nothingness — from a persecuted minority in Mecca to a civilization spanning continents — is itself proof that the religion carries an inherent power of revival. Al-Qaradawi then catalogues the rapid early conquests, the liberation of Jerusalem under 'Umar, and the eventual spread of Islam across continents. He argues that just as the first Muslims could not have foreseen their triumphs from a position of weakness, contemporary Muslims should not despair: history shows that the arc of Islam bends repeatedly toward resurgence, and every period of decline has been followed by renewal.
