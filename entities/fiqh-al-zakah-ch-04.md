## Summary

Chapter 4 treats the zakah obligation on business inventory, a category that jurists term 'urud al-tijarah—commodities acquired for resale at profit. Al-Qaradawi establishes the mandate for this zakah through all four sources of Islamic law. The Qur'anic evidence includes the verse "O ye who believe, spend out of the good things which ye have earned," which early exegetes such as Mujahid, al-Hasan, and al-Tabari understood as encompassing trade earnings. The Prophetic Sunnah is represented by the hadith reported by Abu Daud from Samurah bin Jundub: "The Prophet used to order us to pay sadaqah out of what we have for sale," as well as the general command "give zakah on your wealth." The chapter then presents a substantial body of ijma evidence: Umar bin al-Khattab's well-known practice of calculating and collecting zakah from merchants, the agreement of Ibn Umar, Ibn Abbas, the seven renowned jurists of Medina, and the subsequent generations of Followers. Abu Ubaid records that "all Muslims unanimously agree that trade assets are zakatable." The analogy (qiyas) is equally compelling: trade assets resemble monetary wealth in their capacity for growth and exchange, and were zakah not obligatory on them, wealthy merchants would simply convert cash into inventory to evade payment. Al-Qaradawi also addresses the conditions for zakah on business, including the nisab threshold measured in monetary value, the passage of a full year, the intention to trade, and the methods of calculation and valuation—applying the standard rate of two-and-a-half percent to the market value of inventory at the close of the fiscal year. The chapter systematically refutes those who deny zakah on business inventory, demonstrating that their position contradicts established texts and continuous practice.
