## Summary

This chapter extends the discussion of zakah on business inventory into the modern financial domain, focusing on shares (stocks), bonds, and corporate zakah obligations. Al-Qaradawi first distinguishes shares—representing proportional ownership of corporate capital and bearing profits or losses—from bonds, which are interest-bearing debt instruments. He then presents two contemporary opinions. The first, represented by Shaikh 'Abd al-Rahman 'Isa, differentiates between industrial and trading corporations: shares in purely industrial companies are not zakatable (since capital is tied up in buildings and machinery), though their dividends are; trading-company shares are zakatable at 2.5% of market value minus the fixed-asset portion. Al-Qaradawi critiques this distinction as lacking foundation in Qur'an, Sunnah, ijma', or sound qiyas, noting that both industrial and trading shares represent growing capital earning renewable returns. The second opinion, held by Abu Zahrah, Khallaf, and 'Abd al-Rahman Hasan, treats all shares and bonds uniformly as trade inventory, assessing zakah at 2.5% of market value plus earnings, provided the total reaches nisab. Al-Qaradawi favors this approach for individual payers due to its simplicity; however, he suggests that an Islamic state might adopt the first opinion's differentiated regime at the institutional level. On bonds, he argues they constitute hopeful debts and are therefore zakatable every year—not merely upon redemption—regarding the prohibition of interest as no grounds for exemption. A key concern addressed is double taxation: whether both a corporation and its shareholders can be assessed zakah simultaneously. Al-Qaradawi concludes that duplication is forbidden by the Prophetic rule "No duplication in sadaqah," and therefore only one levy—either at the corporate or shareholder level—should apply. He draws the analogy to livestock held as trade inventory, where the Prophet prohibited combining the trade-zakah and livestock-zakah on the same animals.
