## Summary

The third chapter constructs the philosophical underpinnings of Islamic moral theory by articulating five foundations — moral obligation, moral responsibility, punishment, intentions and motives, and work and exerted effort — supplemented by complementary principles and three higher transcendental values. Drawing on Professor Draz's framework in *The Moral World of the Qur'an* and *The Principles of Moral Ethics*, Al-Qaradawi argues that Qur'anic morality is neither arbitrary nor purely duty-based: obligation in Islam flows from divine authority tempered by human capacity, responsibility presupposes free will and accountability, and punishment serves a deterrent and restorative function rather than mere retribution. The chapter emphasizes the centrality of intention (*niyyah*) in determining moral worth — echoing the well-known hadith that "actions are judged by their intentions" — and situates individual effort alongside divine assistance as co-requisites for ethical action. Complementary principles such as mutual consultation (*shura*), enjoining good and forbidding evil, and the balance between hope and fear further refine the framework. The chapter culminates in three transcendental values — truthfulness, freedom, and justice — which serve as the ultimate ends toward which all Islamic moral obligations are directed, providing a coherent, revelation-grounded alternative to both secular moral relativism and rigid legalism.
