## Summary

This final chapter tackles the misconceived hadiths that have fostered despair among Muslims by seeming to foretell an exclusively dark future. Al-Qaradawi begins with the well-known hadith "Islam started a stranger and it will return a stranger as it has started, so Tuba is to the strangers," establishing its authenticity through multiple chains of transmission (Muslim, Ibn Majah, al-Tirmidhi, al-Tabarani). He then argues that the word gharib derives from estrangement (ghurbah) — loneliness and isolation — not from strangeness, and that the "strangers" are those who remain faithful when others corrupt the religion and who increase in righteousness when people degenerate. Crucially, he reads the hadith as describing cyclical ebb and flow rather than permanent decline: just as Islam began in weakness and grew to strength, its return to estrangement is a temporary phase that will again be followed by triumph. The chapter proceeds to correct other commonly misunderstood narrations, including the hadith about "the knot of Islam" being untied one by one, which he interprets as describing a process that can be reversed through individual and collective effort, not an irreversible decree of doom. Al-Qaradawi's method throughout is to restore these prophetic traditions to their proper context, showing that when read correctly they inspire hope and action rather than fatalism and paralysis. The book thus closes as it opened — with an insistence that Islam's ultimate victory is divinely guaranteed and that the duty of every Muslim is to work toward it with confident expectation.
