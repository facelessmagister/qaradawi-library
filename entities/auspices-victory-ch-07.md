## Summary

This chapter serves as an interstitial reflection — a "necessary pause" — between the presentation of auspices and the final chapter on misconceived hadiths. Al-Qaradawi first expresses his hope that the preceding evidence has penetrated the reader's heart and dispersed the mists of despair. He then makes a twofold declaration of optimism: whether the twentieth century is viewed as the century of communism or of Judaism/Zionism, the twenty-first century will be the century of Islam, because Islam inherently combines the factors of immortality, national revival, and global relevance that capitalism and communism lack. He cites Dr. Murad Hofmann's prediction that Islam will become the world's most professed religion and Congressman Jim Meran's affirmation that Islam embodies peace, discipline, and love. The chapter then issues two warnings. First, a religious caller overwhelmed by despair can never succeed; the Prophet's Companions were instilled with faith, pride, and hope. Al-Qaradawi illustrates this through the example of Hassan al-Banna, whose writings — particularly the articles "Albeit" and "Four Evidences" — maintained unwavering hope even during the darkest crises of 1948. Second, he warns against two pitfalls: the complacent belief that Islam's victory is inevitable without human effort, and the despairing belief that effort is futile. The correct posture combines trust in Allah's promise with relentless striving, drawing on both a sociological view (today's realities were yesterday's dreams) and a historical view (every great civilization rose from apparent impossibility).
